## 18.1 Short MD with OpenMM and GROMACS

The structure that survived Chapter 17's filter is a single, energy-minimized snapshot — one configuration, frozen at an arbitrary instant. It looks right because it was selected to look right. But proteins are not crystals; they breathe, and an active site that is perfectly arranged in a static model may relax, drift, or fall apart the moment thermal motion is switched on. **Molecular dynamics (MD)** asks the question a static model cannot: *does the designed geometry persist under physiological motion?* The answer is the difference between a site that is genuinely poised for catalysis and one that was poised only because a minimizer put it there.

MD integrates Newton's equations of motion for every atom under a **force field** — a parameterized potential energy function (AMBER ff19SB or CHARMM36m for the protein; GAFF2 or the OpenFF "Sage" force field for small-molecule ligands) — in **explicit solvent**, typically a TIP3P or OPC water box with neutralizing ions at ~0.15 M. The standard preparatory pipeline is the same regardless of engine: add hydrogens at the target pH, solvate in a periodic box with at least ~10 Å of padding, neutralize, energy-minimize, then equilibrate in two stages — first NVT (constant volume, heating to 300 K with positional restraints on heavy atoms) and then NPT (constant pressure, releasing restraints gradually) — before collecting a production trajectory.

For design filtering we run **short MD**: production runs of roughly **10–50 ns** per design, not the microseconds used to study slow conformational change. The goal is not to fold the protein or sample a reaction; it is to test local stability of the active site over a window long enough that an unstable geometry will reveal itself. A catalytic distance that drifts within the first few nanoseconds tells you everything you need to know; you do not need a microsecond to learn that a site does not hold together. Two engines dominate. **OpenMM** is a Python-native, GPU-accelerated library that integrates cleanly into a filtering pipeline — you script the whole system setup and run in a notebook. **GROMACS** is a mature, extremely fast C/MPI package favored for large systems and high-throughput batches. Both produce the same physics; OpenMM is usually the better fit for a design lab because it lives inside the Python ecosystem the rest of the pipeline uses.

A short production run at 2 fs timestep (or 4 fs with hydrogen-mass repartitioning) generates frames every few picoseconds. From those frames you extract two families of observables: **global stability** — backbone RMSD relative to the starting structure, and per-residue RMSF (root-mean-square fluctuation) to see which regions are mobile — and **active-site stability**, the catalytic distances that are the real subject of Section 18.2. A backbone RMSD that plateaus below ~2–3 Å indicates the fold is stable on this timescale; runaway RMSD or a catalytic loop with extreme RMSF is an early warning.

## 18.2 Monitoring Catalytic Distances

The single most useful MD analysis for enzyme design is the **catalytic-distance time series**: plot the key geometric measurements of the active site as a function of simulation time and read whether they are *stable* or *drifting*. These distances are the same ones that defined your theozyme — the nucleophile-to-electrophile distance, a catalytic His-to-substrate hydrogen-bond length, a general base to the proton it abstracts, a metal-to-ligand coordination distance. A design is promising if these distances fluctuate tightly around their designed values; it is suspect if they wander away and do not return.

![**Figure 18.1** Catalytic-distance time series over a short MD run. A stable trace fluctuates tightly around the designed distance (here ~3.0 Å); a drifting trace departs and does not return, signaling a site that does not hold its catalytic geometry under thermal motion.](assets/figures/md_distance.png)

Reading these traces is a skill. A **stable trace** fluctuates within roughly ±0.3–0.5 Å of the designed distance with no sustained departure — thermal noise around a well-defined minimum. A **drifting trace** shows a sustained increase (or a jump to a new value that persists), indicating the interaction has broken and the system has found a different, catalytically irrelevant arrangement. The distinction is not the *amount* of fluctuation but whether the distance has a stable mean. A distance that rattles between 2.7 and 3.3 Å is fine; a distance that starts at 2.9 Å and climbs past 6 Å by 8 ns and stays there has lost the interaction. Always look at the trace, not just a summary mean and standard deviation — a bimodal distribution (half the time bonded, half the time broken) can produce a deceptively reasonable mean.

The right summary statistics are the **fraction of frames within a catalytic cutoff** (e.g., the fraction of frames where the nucleophile–electrophile distance is < 3.5 Å) and the **mean and standard deviation over the equilibrated portion** of the trajectory (discard the first few nanoseconds of equilibration). A design that holds its catalytic distance in > 80–90% of equilibrated frames is doing what you designed it to do; one that holds it in 20% of frames has an active site that is mostly disassembled. **MDAnalysis** or **MDTraj** make these computations a few lines of Python over the trajectory.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 18.1 · Interpreting a stable vs. a drifting distance trace"}
**Problem.** Two designs were each run for 20 ns. You track the catalytic Ser Oγ–to–substrate-carbonyl-C distance (designed value 3.0 Å). Design A: mean 3.05 Å, SD 0.28 Å over 5–20 ns, with 94% of frames < 3.5 Å. Design B: mean 4.6 Å, SD 1.9 Å, with 31% of frames < 3.5 Å; the trace sits near 3.0 Å until 6 ns, then jumps to ~6.5 Å and stays there. Rank the designs and explain.

**Solution.** **Design A is clearly superior.** Its distance fluctuates tightly (SD 0.28 Å) around the designed value with no sustained departure, and 94% of equilibrated frames are catalytically competent — the site holds its geometry under thermal motion. **Design B is failing.** The high mean (4.6 Å) and large SD (1.9 Å) already suggest trouble, but the *trace* is decisive: it was competent for the first 6 ns and then irreversibly opened, so the favorable early frames are not a stable state but a relic of the starting structure relaxing away. The 31% competent fraction overstates B's quality because those frames are front-loaded before the geometry broke. The lesson: never rank on the mean alone — B's mean would look merely mediocre, but the trace reveals a one-way collapse. Discard B; advance A. Report for each design the equilibrated mean ± SD, the competent-frame fraction, *and* the trace itself.
:::

## 18.3 Substrate Docking

Refolding and MD test the protein; **docking** tests the *fit between protein and substrate*. Even a perfectly stable active site is useless if the substrate cannot reach it, binds in the wrong orientation, or finds the pocket too small or too closed. Docking places the substrate (or transition-state analog) into the pocket and scores candidate poses, answering three questions: is the pocket **accessible**, does the substrate adopt a **catalytically relevant orientation**, and is the predicted binding plausible.

Three approaches are in common use, and they make different trade-offs. **AutoDock Vina** is the classic physics-scored docking tool: fast, free, and well-understood, it searches translations, rotations, and rotatable bonds against a grid-based scoring function and reports poses ranked by an estimated binding energy (in kcal/mol). It is excellent for asking "can this substrate fit, and roughly where," and it lets you define the search box around the designed pocket. **DiffDock** is a diffusion-based deep-learning docker that generates ligand poses directly and often captures binding modes that grid search misses, with a confidence score per pose. **Boltz-2** (and AlphaFold3) effectively dock as part of holo complex prediction — you get the protein–ligand pose co-folded, which is the most integrated option and dovetails with the holo refolding of Chapter 17.

For a designed enzyme, the docking question is sharper than for a drug target. You are not searching blindly for a binding site; you *know* where the substrate should sit, because you designed the pocket around it. So docking here is really a **consistency and accessibility check**: does an independent docking method, given no information about your intended pose, place the substrate where you designed it, with the reacting atom near the catalytic machinery? If Vina's top pose recapitulates your designed orientation, that is corroboration; if every pose buries the wrong end of the substrate or the ligand cannot be placed at all, the pocket has a problem — too occluded, wrong shape, or a missing channel. **Pocket accessibility** can be quantified directly with cavity-detection tools (fpocket, or a solvent-accessible-surface-area calculation around the catalytic residues): a competent design has an open, appropriately sized channel from solvent to the catalytic center, not a buried cavity the substrate can never reach.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 18.1 · MD and docking software (version-stamped)"}
**OpenMM** (v8.1+, 2024; Eastman et al.) — GPU-accelerated, Python-native MD; system setup, equilibration, and production in a notebook.  
**GROMACS** (2024 release) — high-throughput C/MPI MD engine for large or batched systems.  
**MDAnalysis** (v2.7+) / **MDTraj** (v1.10+) — trajectory analysis: distances, RMSD, RMSF, contact fractions in Python.  
**AMBER ff19SB / CHARMM36m** — protein force fields; **GAFF2** or **OpenFF Sage (2.x)** — small-molecule ligand parameters; **TIP3P/OPC** water.  
**AutoDock Vina** (v1.2.5) — physics-scored docking with a definable search box.  
**DiffDock** (2023) — diffusion-based ML docking with per-pose confidence.  
**Boltz-2 / AlphaFold3** — co-folded protein–ligand poses (docking via complex prediction).  
**fpocket** (v4) — pocket detection and druggability/accessibility scoring.
:::

## 18.4 Near-Attack-Conformation Analysis

A catalytic distance being short *on average* is necessary but not sufficient; catalysis requires that the reacting atoms are arranged in a geometry from which the reaction can actually proceed. The formalization of this idea is the **near-attack conformation (NAC)** — a configuration in which the substrate's reacting atoms are within bonding distance of, and at the correct *angle* to, the catalytic groups, such that thermal motion can carry the system over the transition state. The concept, due to Bruice and colleagues, makes the geometric prerequisite for reaction explicit: it is not enough for two atoms to be close; the attack trajectory must also be correct.

In practice, NAC analysis turns an MD trajectory into a **fraction of frames in a near-attack conformation**. You define the NAC by two or three criteria drawn from the chemistry: a distance criterion (e.g., nucleophile-to-electrophile < 3.2–3.5 Å) *and* an angle criterion (e.g., the Bürgi–Dunitz angle of ~105 ± 15° for nucleophilic attack on a carbonyl, or a near-linear arrangement for an SN2 displacement). You then count, over the equilibrated trajectory with the substrate docked in place, what fraction of frames satisfy *all* criteria simultaneously. A design that spends a meaningful fraction of frames in a NAC is geometrically poised for catalysis; one whose substrate is close but always at the wrong angle is not, even though its distance time series looks fine.

NAC fraction is the most catalysis-relevant geometric quantity you can extract from MD without simulating the reaction itself (which requires QM/MM, beyond the scope of routine filtering). It integrates distance, angle, and dynamic accessibility into one interpretable number, and it correlates with the right thing — the *probability* that the system samples a reactive geometry — far better than a single static distance does. A practical caution: the substrate must be present in the simulation (docked and parameterized) for NAC analysis to mean anything, which is why docking (18.3) precedes it. Reporting a NAC fraction from an apo trajectory is a category error.

## 18.5 When MD Is Worth It — and When It's Theater

MD is expensive and seductive. A trajectory produces beautiful movies and a wealth of numbers, and it is tempting to run it on everything and to believe that more nanoseconds equal more truth. The graduate skill is to deploy MD where it changes a decision and to recognize when it is **theater** — compute spent to *look* rigorous without improving the shortlist.

MD is **worth it** in a few specific situations. It earns its cost when you must **break a tie** among designs that all passed Chapter 17's static filters: scRMSD and geometry RMSD are snapshots, and a short MD run that shows one design holds its catalytic distance in 90% of frames while another collapses by 8 ns is genuine discriminating information. It is worth it when the active site contains a **flexible element** — a catalytic loop, a lid, a mobile metal site — whose stability cannot be judged from a single structure. And it is worth it as the substrate-aware **NAC check** of Section 18.4, which no static method provides. In all these cases MD answers a question the cheaper filters cannot.

MD is **theater** in the more common case where it is run as a reflex. Running 100 ns on every one of 500 designs before the cheaper filters have culled them is a waste; MD belongs *after* Chapter 17, on the survivors. Running MD on a design that already failed self-consistency or geometry will not rescue it — and that is the deeper point of the Reality Check below: MD is a *filter*, not a *fixer*. It can demote a design that looks good statically but is dynamically unstable; it essentially never promotes a design whose static geometry was wrong, because a site that starts in the wrong place does not wander into the right one. Use MD to remove false positives from a good shortlist, not to manufacture hope for a bad one. And resist the lure of length: for stability filtering, 10–50 ns answers the question; a microsecond on a design you will never make is the purest form of theater.

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 18.1 · MD rarely rescues a bad design"}
MD is a demoter, not a promoter. In practice it removes designs that *looked* good in a static snapshot but fall apart under motion — a real and useful service. It almost never elevates a design that failed the static filters: a catalytic site that is misplaced at t = 0 does not relax into correctness; it relaxes into something else. Treat a clean MD trace as a reason to keep a design you already liked, not as a way to revive one you should have cut. And remember that even a flawless 50-ns trajectory with a 95% NAC fraction is still not turnover — only the assay measures catalysis. MD improves the odds; it does not certify activity.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **18.1** A predicted structure is one snapshot; short MD (10–50 ns) in OpenMM or GROMACS with an explicit-solvent force field tests whether the designed active-site geometry survives thermal motion.
- **18.2** The catalytic-distance time series is the key analysis; read it for a *stable mean* (tight fluctuation, high competent-frame fraction) versus a *drift* (sustained departure), never on the mean alone.
- **18.3** Docking (Vina, DiffDock, or co-folded Boltz-2/AF3) tests substrate fit and pocket accessibility; for designed enzymes it is a consistency-and-access check against the intended pose.
- **18.4** Near-attack-conformation analysis combines distance and angle criteria over a substrate-bound trajectory into a NAC fraction — the most catalysis-relevant geometric quantity short of QM/MM.
- **18.5** MD is worth it for tie-breaking, flexible sites, and NAC checks on survivors; it is theater when run reflexively, run before cheaper filters, or expected to rescue a statically wrong design.

**Quantitative & Computational.**

1. A 20-ns trajectory is saved every 10 ps. How many frames are there? If you discard the first 4 ns as equilibration, over how many frames do you compute statistics?
2. Of 1,600 equilibrated frames, 1,408 have the nucleophile–electrophile distance < 3.5 Å. Compute the competent-frame fraction and judge it against an 80% threshold.
3. Design A: catalytic distance mean 3.1 Å, SD 0.3 Å, 91% competent. Design B: mean 3.0 Å, SD 1.6 Å, 55% competent, bimodal trace. Rank them and justify using the right statistics.
4. A NAC requires distance < 3.3 Å AND Bürgi–Dunitz angle 105 ± 15°. Of 1,000 frames, 700 satisfy the distance, 600 satisfy the angle, and 480 satisfy both. Compute the NAC fraction and explain why it is not simply 0.7 × 0.6.
5. At 2 fs timestep, how many integration steps are in a 30-ns run? With hydrogen-mass repartitioning allowing 4 fs, how many?
6. You have 80 designs surviving Chapter 17. At 30 ns/design and 40 ns/day of GPU throughput, how long to run all 80 on one GPU, and on four?

**Challenge.**

1. Propose a composite "dynamic stability score" from MD observables (backbone RMSD plateau, catalytic-distance SD, competent-frame fraction, NAC fraction). Define each term's normalization and defend the weighting, then state how you would validate it against measured activity.
2. A reviewer asks why you did not run microsecond MD on all 500 raw designs. Write a rigorous reply that defends short MD on survivors and names the specific decisions MD does and does not inform.
