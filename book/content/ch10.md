## 10.1 RFdiffusion2: Functional-Group Conditioning

Recall the two limitations that closed Chapter 9. Classic motif scaffolding forced a designer to (i) pre-specify the residue indices and chain spacing of the catalytic residues, and (ii) translate the chemically meaningful quantity — the placement of catalytic *functional groups* relative to the transition state — into backbone frames by enumerating inverse rotamers. Both are pre-processing burdens that throw away design space and inject geometric guesses. **RFdiffusion2** (Ahern et al., *Nature Methods*, 2026) removes both by changing what the model conditions on.

The key conceptual move is **functional-group conditioning**. Instead of supplying fixed backbone coordinates at fixed indices, the designer supplies the **functional-group geometry** directly: "place a carboxylate here, an imidazole nitrogen there, a hydroxyl oxygen there," each as an atom or small group of atoms in space, drawn straight from a theozyme. RFdiffusion2 then generates a backbone *and* decides, on its own, how many residues to use, where along the chain the catalytic residues fall, and which rotamers reach the specified groups. The residue indices are no longer an input — they are an output. The model is free to discover that the nucleophile is most comfortable at position 47 in a 130-residue chain rather than being told it must be residue 11.

This is what "specify the chemistry; let the protein follow" means operationally. The designer's job collapses to its essence — define the catalytic geometry — and the combinatorial burden of inverse-rotamer enumeration is absorbed into the generative process. The model learned, during training, the joint distribution of where functional groups sit and what backbones can present them; at inference it samples backbones conditioned on the groups you want, with the rotamer choice handled internally rather than enumerated by hand.

![**Figure 10.1** Atom-level conditioning. Left: classic residue-level scaffolding conditions on fixed backbone frames at fixed indices, requiring the designer to enumerate inverse rotamers to realize a target functional-group geometry. Right: atom-level models condition directly on catalytic functional-group atoms (RFD2) or on every atom including H-bond donor/acceptor identity and burial (RFD3), letting the backbone, residue indices, and rotamers emerge from generation.](assets/figures/atom_level_conditioning.png)

The benchmark result is the headline. On a curated panel of enzyme active sites, RFdiffusion2 successfully scaffolded **41 of 41** benchmark sites, versus **16 of 41** for the best prior residue-level methods using inverse-rotamer scaffolding. That is not an incremental gain; it is the difference between "works for the easy quarter" and "works for essentially all of them." Just as important for a wet lab, the original report found active sequences within fewer than **96 tested sequences** for favorable targets — the few-dozens regime promised in Chapter 1, reached by removing the pre-processing bottleneck rather than by screening harder.

:::

::: {.method data-title="Method 10.1 · Running RFdiffusion2 from a functional-group file"}
1. **Build the theozyme.** From the transition-state model (Chapter 6), identify the catalytic functional groups and their geometry relative to the substrate/TS. Decide the chemical identity each group implies (e.g., carboxylate → Asp/Glu, imidazole → His).
2. **Write the functional-group input.** Encode each catalytic group as atoms in space in RFD2's input format, tagging each with its allowed residue identity(ies). Include the substrate/ligand atoms if the model is to place them.
3. **Set generation parameters.** Choose a length range (allow the model latitude), the number of designs (start ≈ 500–1,000), and a fixed seed for the first batch. Do **not** specify residue indices for the catalytic residues — that is the point.
4. **Generate.** Run RFD2 on a GPU. Outputs are backbones with the catalytic residues already assigned to positions chosen by the model and rotamers oriented toward the functional-group targets.
5. **Sequence design.** Run LigandMPNN (the ligand-aware ProteinMPNN variant) to design the remaining sequence while holding catalytic identities and, where supported, the ligand context.
6. **Refold and score geometry.** Predict each sequence's structure with a ligand-capable predictor (e.g., AlphaFold3, Chai-1, or Boltz). Compute the **catalytic-atom RMSD** between the predicted active site and the theozyme — this, not global RMSD, is the gate.
7. **Rank and shortlist.** Keep designs with catalytic-atom RMSD below your tolerance (often < 1.0–1.5 Å on the key atoms), confident global predictions, and no steric clashes with the substrate. Record all seeds and inputs.
:::

## 10.2 RFdiffusion3: All-Atom Design and H-Bond/Burial Control

RFdiffusion2 conditions on the catalytic functional groups but still reasons about the protein largely at the level of residues and their frames; the rest of each side chain and the surrounding chemistry are filled in downstream. **RFdiffusion3** (Butcher et al., 2025) goes further: it models **every atom**, on the order of **~14 atoms per residue** including all side-chain heavy atoms, within the diffusion process itself. The protein is no longer a backbone scaffold awaiting a sequence — it is a full all-atom object generated jointly.

This all-atom representation unlocks conditioning that simply has no expression in a backbone-only model. Three are decisive for enzymes:

- **Specific hydrogen-bond donor/acceptor identity.** You can require that a particular atom act as an H-bond *donor* to a substrate oxygen, or that a backbone amide *accept* from a catalytic water — not merely "a polar residue is nearby" but the directional, chemically typed interaction that actually stabilizes a transition state or orients a substrate.
- **Burial / solvent exposure.** You can require that a catalytic group be buried (excluded from solvent, raising its effective reactivity and pK_a shifts) or, conversely, surface-exposed. Burial control is how a designer builds the desolvated microenvironment that makes general acid/base catalysis and oxyanion stabilization work.
- **Substrate placement.** Because the ligand and protein atoms are co-modeled, RFD3 can place the substrate in the pocket as part of generation rather than docking it afterward.

The practical consequence is that RFD3 lets you specify the *electrostatic and steric microenvironment* of the active site, not just the positions of catalytic side-chain tips. That is the difference between "the histidine is in the right place" and "the histidine is the right place, buried, donating to the leaving group, with the oxyanion hole formed by two backbone amides." RFD3 also reports being roughly **10× faster** than RFD2, which matters when a campaign generates thousands of designs — speed converts directly into a larger, better-filtered shortlist for the same compute budget.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 10.1 · Specifying an atom-level H-bond constraint for RFD3"}
**Problem.** Your theozyme calls for a catalytic histidine that acts as a general base, abstracting a proton from a substrate hydroxyl, while a backbone amide NH stabilizes the developing oxyanion on the substrate carbonyl. Translate this into atom-level constraints that distinguish what RFD2 and RFD3 can each express, and state the geometric quantities you would set.

**Solution.** Identify the interacting atom pairs and their roles. (1) **General base:** the His **Nε2** must sit within hydrogen-bonding distance (≈2.7–3.2 Å) and near-linear angle to the substrate hydroxyl **O–H**, with Nε2 acting as *acceptor*. (2) **Oxyanion stabilization:** a backbone amide **N–H** must *donate* to the substrate carbonyl **O**, again at ≈2.8–3.1 Å with favorable N–H···O angle. In **RFD2** you can place the His imidazole functional group at the target geometry (the functional-group condition), but you cannot directly require that a *specific backbone amide* donate to the oxyanion with a typed, directional H-bond — that interaction involves backbone atoms and donor/acceptor identity that RFD2 does not condition on. In **RFD3**, both constraints are expressible: tag Nε2 as an acceptor at the specified distance/angle to the hydroxyl, and require a buried backbone-amide donor to the carbonyl oxygen, with the catalytic cluster marked buried. Set: the two donor–acceptor distances, the two N/O···H–X angles, and a burial flag on the cluster. The lesson: the *backbone-amide oxyanion hole* and the *typed donor/acceptor roles* are exactly the things that move you from RFD2 to RFD3.
:::

::: {.method data-title="Method 10.2 · Running RFD3 with burial and H-bond constraints"}
1. **Annotate the theozyme atomically.** For each catalytic interaction, record the two atoms, the donor/acceptor role, the target distance and angle, and whether the group is buried.
2. **Write the all-atom constraint file.** Encode catalytic functional groups, typed H-bond donor/acceptor constraints, burial flags, and the substrate atoms to be co-placed.
3. **Configure generation.** Length range, design count (RFD3's speed allows ≈2,000–5,000), seed. Mark the catalytic cluster as buried if the chemistry needs desolvation.
4. **Generate all-atom designs.** Run RFD3 on a GPU; outputs include side-chain atoms and the placed substrate, so the predicted active-site geometry is directly inspectable.
5. **Sequence design (if needed).** RFD3 produces side-chain atoms; use LigandMPNN to finalize/diversify the non-catalytic sequence and to provide a sequence for refold validation.
6. **Validate with a ligand-aware predictor.** Refold with AF3 / Chai-1 / Boltz; score catalytic-atom RMSD, H-bond geometry recovery (distance and angle), and burial (solvent-accessible surface area of the catalytic group).
7. **Rank.** Gate on H-bond geometry and burial recovery, not just Cα-RMSD. Shortlist the designs that reproduce the *microenvironment*, then proceed toward the ~96-design test set.
:::

## 10.3 Riff-Diff: Catalytic-Array Scaffolding

A serine-protease triad is three residues; many real enzymes need more — a metal coordinated by four ligands plus a general base and an oxyanion hole, or a multi-step mechanism with a proton-relay network. As the number of mutually constrained catalytic residues grows, the geometry becomes a tightly coupled **catalytic array**, and the hard part shifts from placing any one group to placing *all of them simultaneously* in a single scaffold without compromise.

**Riff-Diff** (Schnettler et al., *Nature*, 2025) targets exactly this regime: scaffolding **full catalytic arrays**. Its workflow assembles the desired multi-residue active site as a coherent geometric object and then builds — via a diffusion backbone generation step coupled to inverse-rotamer-aware array placement and downstream sequence design — scaffolds that present the entire array at once. The headline biological result is the one that matters most for this book's thesis: Riff-Diff produced de novo enzymes whose **catalytic rates rivaled those of natural enzymes without any directed evolution**. That is a milestone. For most of the field's history, de novo designs needed many rounds of laboratory optimization to climb from marginal to useful activity; reaching natural-like rates straight out of computation, for a multi-residue active site, is the proof that atom-level, array-aware design is qualitatively different from grafting a motif onto a hole.

The conceptual relationship to RFD2/RFD3 is one of emphasis. All three condition on chemistry rather than on a pre-fixed backbone. Riff-Diff's distinctive strength is the *combinatorial* problem of many interdependent catalytic residues — the case where naive inverse-rotamer enumeration is most explosive and where holding the whole geometry together is hardest.

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 10.1 · In silico is not active — still test ~96"}
Atom-level models are a genuine leap: RFD2 scaffolds 41/41 benchmark sites where prior methods reached 16/41, and Riff-Diff has reached natural-like rates without directed evolution. But a backbone that reproduces your theozyme at sub-ångström catalytic-atom RMSD, with correct H-bond geometry and burial *in the prediction*, is still a hypothesis. Predictors share blind spots with generators; a design can refold beautifully and be inert, unexpressed, or insoluble. The honest expectation remains that you **express and assay on the order of ~96 designs** to find robust activity for a favorable chemistry — and more for a hard one. The advance is not that testing disappeared; it is that the ~96 you test are now drawn from a far better-enriched pool. Report designability, geometry recovery, *and* the experimental hit rate as three separate numbers.
:::

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 10.1 · Atom-level generators"}

**RFdiffusion3 (RFD3)** — Butcher et al., 2025; all-atom diffusion (~14 atoms/residue) with typed H-bond donor/acceptor, burial, and substrate-placement conditioning; ≈10× faster than RFD2.

**LigandMPNN** — Dauparas et al.; ligand-aware inverse folding; the default sequence-design partner for active-site work.  
**Ligand-aware predictors** — AlphaFold3, Chai-1, Boltz-1/2 for refold validation with the substrate present.

:::

## 10.4 Choosing Among RFD2 / RFD3 / Riff-Diff

The three models are not a ranking; they are a decision keyed to **active-site complexity** and the kind of control the chemistry demands. A practical rule:

- **Reach for RFdiffusion2** when your active site is defined primarily by the *positions of a few catalytic functional groups* and you want the lightest input — a theozyme's group geometry — without specifying typed H-bonds or burial. It is the cleanest entry point and the right default for a two- to four-group site where placement, not microenvironment, is the crux.
- **Reach for RFdiffusion3** when the chemistry depends on the *microenvironment*: a specific H-bond donor/acceptor pattern (e.g., a backbone-amide oxyanion hole), required burial/desolvation of a catalytic group, or explicit substrate placement. Its all-atom representation is the only one of the three that can *condition* on these, and its ≈10× speed lets you generate the larger pools that hard sites need.
- **Reach for Riff-Diff** when the defining difficulty is a *large, tightly coupled catalytic array* — many interdependent residues (a metal site plus a base plus an oxyanion hole, or a proton-relay network) that must all be presented simultaneously. This is where array-aware scaffolding earns its keep and where it has demonstrated natural-like rates.

These overlap, and the mature move is to *compose* them (Chapter 12): prototype geometry with RFD2, refine the microenvironment with RFD3, and use Riff-Diff when the array count makes simultaneous placement the bottleneck. The selection criterion to internalize is not "which is best" but "what does *this* chemistry need me to control" — group positions, microenvironment, or a coupled array.

:::

## 10.5 Worked Comparison on One Active Site

To make the abstractions concrete, take a single, well-understood target: an **ester-hydrolysis active site** built around a His–Asp dyad (the His as general base, the Asp orienting and tuning the His) plus a backbone-amide oxyanion hole to stabilize the tetrahedral intermediate. The theozyme specifies: the His imidazole Nε2 positioned to abstract the proton from the attacking nucleophile/water; the Asp carboxylate hydrogen-bonded to His Nδ1; and two backbone amide N–H groups forming the oxyanion hole around the substrate carbonyl oxygen.

**Run A — RFdiffusion2.** Supply the His imidazole and Asp carboxylate as functional groups with their relative geometry, tag identities, and include the substrate. RFD2 generates backbones that place a His and an Asp wherever along the chain the model finds them designable, with rotamers oriented to the targets. After LigandMPNN and a ligand-aware refold, you score catalytic-atom RMSD on the imidazole N atoms and the carboxylate O atoms. Typical outcome: the dyad geometry is reproduced well; the **oxyanion hole, however, is not something you could condition on** — it depends on backbone-amide identity and direction — so its formation is left to chance and must be *filtered for* after the fact, discarding the (many) designs that lack it.

**Run B — RFdiffusion3.** Supply the same dyad *plus* the two backbone-amide donor constraints (typed N–H → carbonyl O, with distance and angle) and a burial flag on the catalytic cluster. RFD3 generates all-atom designs in which the oxyanion hole is part of the *specification*, not the filter. Refolding and scoring now includes H-bond geometry recovery for the oxyanion hole and burial of the dyad. Typical outcome: a higher fraction of designs present a *complete* active site — dyad and oxyanion hole and desolvation together — because the microenvironment was conditioned on rather than hoped for. For the same compute, RFD3's speed also yields a larger pool to filter.

The comparison generalizes into a single lesson. RFD2 moved the chemistry from a backbone proxy to the functional groups — a decisive gain over Chapter 9's scaffolding. RFD3 moved it one level deeper, to the *typed interactions and microenvironment* that distinguish a placed residue from a working catalytic site. You feel the difference precisely where catalysis lives: not in whether the histidine is roughly in place, but in whether the oxyanion hole, the desolvation, and the directional hydrogen bonds are all there at once. That is why this chapter sits at the center of the book: it is where "specify the chemistry; let the protein follow" stops being a slogan and becomes a set of constraints you actually write down.

:::

## Hands-On Lab 10 (Flagship) · Scaffold a Real Active Site, RFD2 vs. RFD3

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 10 · Scaffold a Real Active Site with RFdiffusion2 and RFdiffusion3"}
**Goal.** Scaffold one real active site from a theozyme using *both* RFD2 and RFD3, and compare catalytic-geometry preservation and microenvironment recovery.

1. **Define the theozyme.** Choose a documented small active site (e.g., a His–Asp dyad ester hydrolase with a backbone-amide oxyanion hole). Record every catalytic atom, each H-bond's donor/acceptor role, distances, angles, and which groups must be buried. Include the substrate/TS analog.
2. **RFD2 run.** Encode the functional groups (dyad + substrate) without residue indices. Generate ≈500–1,000 backbones. Design sequences with LigandMPNN. Refold with a ligand-aware predictor (AF3/Chai-1/Boltz).
3. **RFD3 run.** Encode the same dyad *plus* the typed oxyanion-hole H-bond constraints and a burial flag. Generate ≈1,000–2,000 all-atom designs (use the speed). Finalize sequences with LigandMPNN; refold with the same predictor.
4. **Score both.** For every surviving design compute: global plausibility (pLDDT/confidence), **catalytic-atom RMSD** to the theozyme, **H-bond geometry recovery** (distance and angle for each catalytic H-bond, including the oxyanion hole), and **burial** (SASA of the catalytic cluster).
5. **Compare.** Build a table and matched figures: what fraction from each model presents the *complete* active site (dyad + oxyanion hole + burial), not just the dyad? Where does RFD2 fall short, and is it where Section 10.5 predicts?
6. **Shortlist.** Rank the top designs across both models by microenvironment recovery; assemble a candidate test set heading toward ~96 (Reality Check 10.1).

**Platform:** HPC or cloud GPU (multi-GB VRAM; RFD3's speed makes the larger pool feasible) · **Deliverable:** a comparative report (RFD2 vs. RFD3 on geometry, H-bond, and burial recovery) plus a ranked list of backbones with active-site figures.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **10.1** RFdiffusion2 conditions on catalytic *functional-group geometry*, making residue indices and rotamers emergent outputs; it scaffolded 41/41 benchmark sites (vs. 16/41 prior) and reached active sequences within < 96 tests.
- **10.2** RFdiffusion3 models every atom (~14/residue), enabling conditioning on typed H-bond donor/acceptor identity, burial/desolvation, and substrate placement, at ≈10× RFD2's speed.
- **10.3** Riff-Diff scaffolds full catalytic *arrays* and produced de novo enzymes at natural-like rates without directed evolution.
- **10.4** Choose by control needed: RFD2 for functional-group placement, RFD3 for microenvironment, Riff-Diff for large coupled arrays — and compose them when sensible.
- **10.5** On one His–Asp + oxyanion-hole site, RFD2 reproduces the dyad but leaves the oxyanion hole to filtering, whereas RFD3 *conditions* on it — the move from "residue placed" to "microenvironment built."
