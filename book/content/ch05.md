## 5.1 The Rosetta Era

Modern de novo enzyme design began in earnest in the mid-2000s with a methodology built around the **theozyme** and the Rosetta software suite. The logic was clean and physics-based. First, quantum chemistry defines the ideal arrangement of catalytic functional groups around an explicit transition state — the **theozyme** (a "theoretical enzyme," a term coined by Houk and Tantillo). Then **RosettaMatch** searches a library of natural protein scaffolds for backbones that can present side chains in that geometry, "matching" the theozyme into a real fold. Finally, **RosettaDesign** optimizes the surrounding sequence to support the placed catalytic residues, build the pocket, and stabilize the fold. Order genes for the top designs, express, and assay.

Three landmark results defined the era. The **Kemp eliminases** (Röthlisberger et al., *Nature*, 2008) targeted a model proton-abstraction reaction — base-catalyzed ring-opening of benzisoxazole — with a minimal catalytic set of a general base (Asp/Glu or His) plus, in some designs, a stabilizing residue for the developing negative charge. Of dozens of designs tested, several showed genuine, if modest, catalysis (the best initial variants reached rate enhancements of ~10²–10⁵ over background, with k_cat/K_M values far below natural enzymes). The **retro-aldolases** (Jiang et al., *Science*, 2008) used a lysine to form a Schiff base and break a carbon–carbon bond, again yielding weak but real de novo activity. The **Diels–Alderase** (Siegel et al., *Science*, 2010) achieved a stereoselective bimolecular cycloaddition with no natural counterpart — a striking proof that wholly abiological chemistry could be designed, even though, as Chapter 4 predicts for pericyclic reactions, the rate enhancement was modest because there is little charge to stabilize electrostatically.

The Rosetta era proved Pauling's hypothesis constructively: arrange the right groups around a transition state and you get catalysis. But it also exposed the gap between "active" and "good." The designs worked, yet their k_cat/K_M values were often 10⁶-fold or more below those of comparable natural enzymes, and the hit rates were sobering — many campaigns screened large numbers of designs for a handful of weak catalysts. The era's enduring lessons were that the theozyme-grafting logic is sound, that the limiting step was the *quality* of the resulting active site (geometry imperfect, second shell unmodeled, dynamics ignored), and that something more was needed to turn a weak hit into a real enzyme.

## 5.2 The Role of Directed Evolution in Early Successes

The weak initial designs became genuinely impressive enzymes only through **directed evolution** — iterative rounds of mutagenesis and screening that optimize what computation got approximately right. The most celebrated case is the Hilvert laboratory's evolution of the Kemp eliminases. Starting from the Baker-lab design **KE07**, and more dramatically from **KE59** and the de-novo-then-evolved **HG3**, directed evolution improved catalytic efficiency by roughly **10⁵-fold**, culminating in the variant **HG3.17**, whose k_cat/K_M reached on the order of 2 × 10⁵ M⁻¹s⁻¹ — comparable to a respectable natural enzyme, and one of the most efficient Kemp eliminases known.

What did evolution actually fix? Strikingly, almost never the catalytic base itself. The activity-enhancing mutations clustered in the **second shell and beyond** (Chapter 3): they rigidified the active site, refined the positioning and pK_a of the catalytic residues, remodeled the pocket to exclude water and better complement the transition state, and improved substrate binding. In other words, evolution corrected exactly the things the physics-based pipeline modeled poorly — fine geometry, dynamics, electrostatic tuning, and the second shell — while leaving the *conceptual* catalytic set (which the design got right) intact.

This is the era's most important methodological lesson, and it frames the modern agenda. The 2008-vintage pipeline was a good hypothesis generator and a poor optimizer; directed evolution was the indispensable finisher. A central promise of the deep-learning era is to *close this gap at design time* — to model the second shell, dynamics, and fine geometry well enough that designs emerge closer to evolution's endpoint, reducing (though rarely eliminating) the reliance on downstream optimization. Directed evolution has not disappeared; it has become a complement to be deployed deliberately (Part VI), not a crutch every design must lean on.

## 5.3 The Deep-Learning Inflection

Between roughly 2018 and 2026 the field's foundations shifted from physics-based search to learned generative modeling. The inflection had several waves. **Structure prediction** matured first: **AlphaFold2** (2021) and **RoseTTAFold** (2021) made accurate structure prediction routine, giving designers a fast, reliable way to *check* whether a sequence would fold as intended — turning prediction into a filter (Part II). Next came **sequence design** by deep learning: **ProteinMPNN** (Dauparas et al., 2022) designs sequences for a given backbone far better and faster than physics-based RosettaDesign, dramatically raising the fraction of designs that fold and express. Then came **backbone generation**: **RFdiffusion** (Watson et al., 2023) and related diffusion models generate novel backbones from scratch, optionally *conditioned* on a functional site — so the scaffold can be grown around the active site rather than searched for in a library, inverting the Rosetta-era logic of Section 5.1.

The most consequential development for *catalysis* specifically is **atom-level, ligand- and transition-state-aware** generation. Tools in the **RFdiffusion All-Atom** / **RFdiffusion2** lineage, and family-based methods such as the 2025 **Riff-Diff** pipeline, can build backbones around an explicit catalytic constellation and ligand at atomic resolution, addressing precisely the fine-geometry and second-shell deficits that crippled the Rosetta era. Reported results have begun to show *higher hit rates from fewer tested designs* for favorable chemistries — a qualitative change from screening thousands for a few hits. The narrative arc, then, is from *match a theozyme into an existing fold and hope the sequence folds* to *generate a bespoke, foldable backbone around the transition state with its second shell modeled in*. The physics of Chapter 2 has not changed; our ability to instantiate it in protein has.

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 5.1 · What 'designed enzyme' meant in 2008 vs. 2026"}
The phrase has quietly raised its bar.

- **2008:** A "designed enzyme" typically meant a theozyme grafted onto a *natural* scaffold by RosettaMatch, yielding weak but measurable activity (often 10⁶-fold below natural efficiency), found among dozens to thousands of tested designs, and usually requiring directed evolution to become competent. "De novo" referred to the *function*, not the *backbone*.
- **2026:** It increasingly means a backbone *generated from scratch* (TM-score < 0.5 to any PDB entry), with the active site and second shell built in at atomic resolution, an explicit transition-state model, and — for favorable chemistries — appreciable activity from a small tested set (tens, sometimes < 96), sometimes without any directed evolution. "De novo" now plausibly refers to *both* the function and the backbone.

The constants across both eras: most designs are still inactive or unexpressed; honest reporting requires stating how many were tested; and hard chemistries (radical, redox, efficient cofactor catalysis) remain frontier in both 2008 and 2026.
:::

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 5.1 · Auditing a historical hit rate"}
**Problem.** A Kemp-eliminase paper reports that of 84 designs ordered, 59 expressed solubly, and 8 showed catalytic activity clearly above the buffer background. After directed evolution, the best variant's k_cat/K_M improved from ~5 M⁻¹s⁻¹ to ~2 × 10⁵ M⁻¹s⁻¹. Compute the meaningful rates and state honestly what was achieved.

**Solution.** Two distinct "hit rates" must be separated.

- *Expression rate:* 59/84 = **70%** expressed solubly. Not a measure of design success, but a real filter — 30% never reached a meaningful assay.
- *Activity hit rate (per ordered design):* 8/84 = **9.5%**. *Per expressed design:* 8/59 = **13.6%**. Always state the denominator; quoting 8/59 flatters the method relative to 8/84.
- *Directed-evolution improvement:* (2 × 10⁵)/5 = **4 × 10⁴-fold**, i.e. ~10⁴·⁶ ≈ a ~4.6-order-of-magnitude gain — most of the final enzyme's competence came from *evolution*, not the initial design. By the Eyring per-decade rule (Chapter 2), 4.6 orders ≈ 4.6 × 5.71 ≈ 26 kJ/mol of additional barrier lowering supplied after design.

**Honest statement:** the *design* produced 8 weak-but-real de novo catalysts from 84 attempts (≈10% hit rate), proving the catalytic hypothesis; *directed evolution* then supplied roughly 10⁴–10⁵-fold of the final activity. Reporting only the post-evolution k_cat/K_M, or only the per-expressed hit rate, would misattribute evolution's work to design and overstate the method. This audit — separate expression from activity, state denominators, and partition design versus evolution contributions — is exactly how a referee should read any such paper.
:::

## 5.4 The Four Questions of Every Campaign

Strip away the changing tools and every campaign — Rosetta-era or deep-learning, 2008 or 2026 — answers the same four questions, in order. They are the spine of Part IV and the operational form of everything in Chapters 2–4. State them explicitly, because they recur on nearly every page that follows.

1. **What reaction?** Define the exact transformation: substrates, products, stoichiometry, conditions, and the success criterion (a target k_cat/K_M or rate enhancement, defined *before* the work begins). This is a Chapter 1 / Chapter 13 question.
2. **What transition state?** Identify the rate-limiting transition state and its geometry and charge distribution — the thing the enzyme must be complementary to (Pauling, Chapter 2). Everything downstream is complementarity to *this* structure.
3. **What catalytic set?** Derive the minimal constellation of functional groups and cofactors the mechanism requires, with their target geometry — the procedure of Chapter 4 (and Worked Example 4.1).
4. **What pocket?** Build (or generate) the scaffold and second shell that hold the catalytic set in geometry, exclude or admit water appropriately, bind and orient the substrate, and fold stably (Chapter 3 and Part III).

The four questions are deliberately ordered: each constrains the next, and skipping forward is the most common cause of failure. A team that starts at question 4 ("we have a nice scaffold") without a clear answer to questions 1–3 builds a beautiful protein that catalyzes nothing. The questions are also the natural rubric for *reading* a design paper — find each answer, and judge whether the authors got it right.

## 5.5 An Annotated Milestone Timeline

![**Figure 5.1** An annotated milestone timeline of de novo enzyme design, from the theozyme concept and the 2008 Kemp eliminases and retro-aldolases, through the 2010 Diels–Alderase and Hilvert's directed-evolution finishing, to the deep-learning wave: AlphaFold2/RoseTTAFold (2021), ProteinMPNN (2022), RFdiffusion (2023), and the 2024–2025 atom-level, transition-state-aware pipelines.](assets/figures/milestone_timeline.png)

Read along the timeline and the field's logic appears as a sequence of bottlenecks removed. The theozyme gave a *target*. RosettaMatch and RosettaDesign gave a way to *instantiate* it, but the resulting sequences often misfolded and the geometry was imperfect — so directed evolution had to finish the job. AlphaFold2 and ProteinMPNN removed the *folding/expression* bottleneck (you could now design sequences that reliably fold and predict whether they will). RFdiffusion removed the *scaffold* bottleneck (generate a bespoke backbone instead of searching for one). The atom-level, ligand-aware generators of 2024–2026 are attacking the *fine-geometry and second-shell* bottleneck — the very deficit that made directed evolution indispensable in 2008. Each milestone moved the field one step closer to designs that are competent at birth. None abolished the core physics, the numbers game, or the value of honest accounting — which is why this history, not just the latest tool, belongs in a designer's working knowledge.

## Chapter Summary

- **5.1** The Rosetta era (theozyme + RosettaMatch + RosettaDesign) produced the first de novo Kemp eliminases, retro-aldolases (2008), and a Diels–Alderase (2010): real but weak activity, modest hit rates, proving Pauling's hypothesis constructively.
- **5.2** Directed evolution (notably Hilvert's ~10⁵-fold improvement to HG3.17) supplied most of the final competence by fixing second-shell geometry, pK_a, dynamics, and water exclusion — not the catalytic residues.
- **5.3** The deep-learning inflection removed bottlenecks in turn: AlphaFold2/RoseTTAFold (folding check), ProteinMPNN (sequence design), RFdiffusion (backbone generation), and atom-level transition-state-aware tools (fine geometry and second shell).
- **5.4** Every campaign answers four ordered questions — What reaction? What transition state? What catalytic set? What pocket? — the spine of Part IV and the rubric for reading design papers.
- **5.5** The milestone timeline reads as a sequence of bottlenecks removed, each milestone bringing designs closer to competence at birth without abolishing the core physics or the numbers game.
