## 9.1 Denoising Diffusion, a Designer's Mental Model

The generative revolution in protein design rests on a single idea borrowed from image synthesis: if you can teach a network to remove a little bit of noise from a corrupted example, you can run that network many times in sequence and walk all the way from pure noise to a clean, structured sample. The clean samples here are not pictures — they are sets of protein backbone coordinates.

Picture the **forward process** first, because it is the easy direction. Take a real protein backbone from the Protein Data Bank and, step by step, jitter its atomic coordinates with small random displacements. After a few steps the fold is still recognizable; after a few hundred, the coordinates are indistinguishable from a random cloud. This destruction is deterministic in its statistics: at every **timestep** *t* we know exactly how much noise has been added relative to the original. The forward process is just a schedule of progressive corruption, and it requires no learning at all.

The **reverse process** is where the network earns its keep. We train a neural network to look at a noisy backbone at timestep *t* and predict the cleaner version at timestep *t*−1 — equivalently, to estimate the noise that was added. Because the forward noising is known, we can manufacture unlimited training pairs from real structures: take any PDB backbone, noise it to a random *t*, and ask the network to denoise. At **inference** time we have no real structure to start from. We start from pure Gaussian noise at the largest timestep *T*, ask the network "what clean structure is hiding under this?", take a small step toward that estimate, and repeat — typically across 50 to 200 steps — until a coherent backbone emerges. The motto at the head of this chapter is literal: start from noise, end at a fold.

![**Figure 9.1** Denoising diffusion for backbones. The forward process (left to right) progressively corrupts a real backbone into noise on a fixed schedule; the learned reverse process (right to left) iteratively denoises a random cloud into a coherent fold. Conditioning information is injected at every reverse step.](assets/figures/diffusion.png)

Two design-relevant facts fall out of this picture. First, diffusion is **iterative**, so we can inject information at every reverse step — this is the hook that makes conditioning possible. Second, the model operates on a representation that respects the geometry of proteins. **RFdiffusion**, the workhorse of this chapter, does not diffuse raw Cartesian coordinates naively; it represents each residue as a rigid **frame** — a translation (the Cα position) plus a rotation (the orientation of the backbone N–Cα–C triad) — and diffuses on the manifold of these frames. The network at the core is a fine-tuned version of **RoseTTAFold**, the structure-prediction network, so the model already "knows" what plausible protein geometry looks like before any diffusion training begins. This reuse of a pretrained structure predictor is the single most important reason RFdiffusion works as well as it does.

## 9.2 Conditional vs. Unconditional Generation

An **unconditional** run asks the model only for "a plausible protein backbone of length *L*." Each call to RFdiffusion with nothing but a length specification samples a fresh fold from the distribution of protein-like geometries. This is useful for exploring what the model considers plausible and for generating novel scaffolds with no constraints, but it is rarely what an enzyme designer wants. Catalysis is a statement about *where specific atoms sit*, and an unconditional sample knows nothing about your reaction.

**Conditional** generation steers the reverse process toward backbones that satisfy a constraint. Because conditioning is applied at every denoising step, the model nudges each partial structure toward agreement with the condition while still keeping it on the manifold of realistic proteins. RFdiffusion exposes several conditioning channels, and it is worth distinguishing them clearly:

- **Length and symmetry.** The simplest conditions: total residue count, chain breaks, and point-group symmetry for assemblies.
- **Fold conditioning.** A specification of secondary-structure and topology — "make me a TIM barrel" — without dictating exact coordinates.
- **Motif scaffolding.** A set of fixed residues whose coordinates are held constant while the model builds new structure around them. This is the channel used to install a catalytic motif and is the focus of Section 9.4.
- **Binder and interface conditioning.** Generating a backbone against a fixed target surface.

The mechanism for motif scaffolding deserves a precise statement because it explains both the method's power and its limits. The fixed motif residues are placed at chosen positions and held at their true coordinates throughout denoising — they are simply not noised. The model treats them as context and "in-paints" the rest of the chain around them, exactly as an image diffusion model can fill in a masked region while respecting the visible pixels. The output is a full backbone in which the motif occupies the geometry you specified and the scaffold is novel.

## 9.3 RFdiffusion: Monomers, Assemblies, Fold Conditioning

RFdiffusion (Watson et al., 2023) was the first diffusion model to generate experimentally validated de novo proteins across a wide range of tasks, and it remains the default starting point for backbone generation. Its range is broad: it produces well-folded **monomers** up to several hundred residues, **symmetric oligomers** and nanocages specified by point group (cyclic, dihedral, tetrahedral, and higher symmetries), **binders** designed against a target protein surface, and **scaffolds** that present a functional motif. In each case the experimental success rates reported in the original work were, for the time, startling — symmetric oligomers and binders that expressed, folded, and behaved as designed at rates one to two orders of magnitude better than prior Rosetta-based pipelines.

For enzyme work the most useful capability beyond raw motif scaffolding is **fold conditioning**. Many catalytic chemistries are historically associated with particular folds: the **TIM barrel** (an eightfold β/α barrel) is the most common enzyme fold in nature and hosts an enormous range of chemistries; the **NTF2-like fold** provides a deep hydrophobic pocket well suited to small-molecule binding and several designed activities; the Rossmann fold cradles dinucleotide cofactors. Fold conditioning lets you ask RFdiffusion for a backbone of a chosen topology by supplying per-residue **secondary-structure** and **block-adjacency** information — which strands pack against which, in what order — without dictating coordinates. The model then generates many distinct backbones that all realize that topology, giving a designer a population of barrels or NTF2 folds to scaffold a motif into.

Fold conditioning is not free. Not every requested topology is equally easy for the model, and the in-silico success rate — the fraction of generated backbones that pass downstream self-consistency filtering — varies substantially by fold. This variation is the subject of Reality Check 9.1, and it is the first quantitative number a designer should internalize before committing GPU hours.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 9.1 · Reading an RFdiffusion in-silico success rate"}
**Problem.** You generate 500 NTF2 backbones with fold conditioning. For each you design 8 sequences with ProteinMPNN and predict each sequence's structure with a single-sequence predictor, then compute the backbone-RMSD between the prediction and the design. You count a backbone as a "self-consistent success" if at least one of its 8 sequences refolds to within 2 Å Cα-RMSD and with predicted confidence (pLDDT) above 80. You observe 270 successes. What is the in-silico success rate, and what does it *not* tell you?

**Solution.** The in-silico success rate is 270 / 500 = **54%**. This is the *designability* of the backbones — the fraction for which a sequence plausibly encodes the intended fold. It is the right number to report for backbone quality and matches the NTF2 figure in Reality Check 9.1. What it does **not** tell you: whether any of these backbones will *express*, fold *in vitro*, or show *catalytic activity*. Self-consistency is necessary but nowhere near sufficient for function. A 54% designability rate paired with a 1–5% experimental hit rate is entirely normal; the two numbers measure different things, and conflating them is a classic reporting error.
:::

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 9.1 · RFdiffusion"}
**RFdiffusion** (Watson et al., Nature 2023) — open source, MIT-licensed; repo `github.com/RosettaCommons/RFdiffusion`. Core network is a fine-tuned RoseTTAFold; runs on a single GPU (≈8–16 GB VRAM for typical monomers). Outputs backbone-only PDBs plus a `.trb` metadata file recording the contig map and fixed-residue indices.  
**ProteinMPNN** (Dauparas et al., Science 2022) — message-passing inverse-folding network for sequence design on a fixed backbone; the standard partner stage; repo `github.com/dauparas/ProteinMPNN`.  
**Self-consistency predictor** — AlphaFold2 (single-sequence/initial-guess mode), ESMFold, or Boltz/Chai for refolding the designed sequence.  
Pin versions and record the random seed; diffusion outputs are seed-dependent and must be reproducible for a design notebook.
:::

## 9.4 Motif Scaffolding for Simple Catalytic Motifs

A **catalytic motif** is the minimal set of residues whose precise relative geometry produces a chemistry. The classic teaching example is the **serine protease catalytic triad** — Ser, His, Asp — but for a first scaffolding exercise a two- or three-residue motif is the right scope: an oxyanion-stabilizing pair, a metal-binding His–His–Asp cluster, or a single nucleophile flanked by a general base.

Motif scaffolding takes the *coordinates* of that motif — typically lifted from a natural enzyme or built as a **theozyme** (Chapter 6) — and asks RFdiffusion to construct a novel scaffold that holds those residues in exactly that geometry. The user supplies a **contig map**, a compact grammar that interleaves fixed segments (drawn from the input PDB by chain and residue range) with newly generated segments of specified length. A contig like `10-20/A50-52/10-30` means "generate 10–20 new residues, then place input residues A50–A52 at their fixed coordinates, then generate 10–30 more." The model fills in the generated segments around the fixed triad.

The output is a backbone, not a finished enzyme. Two stages follow immediately. First, **sequence design** with ProteinMPNN assigns an amino-acid sequence to the generated portion while (optionally) holding the motif identities fixed. Second, a **self-consistency filter** refolds each designed sequence and keeps only those whose predicted structure recapitulates both the global fold and — critically — the catalytic geometry, measured as the motif-atom RMSD between design and prediction. Backbones that refold globally but let the catalytic residues drift are discarded; geometry at the active site is the entire point.

::: {.method data-title="Method 9.1 · Scaffolding a small catalytic motif with RFdiffusion"}
1. **Prepare the motif.** Extract the catalytic residues' coordinates into a clean input PDB (one chain, contiguous numbering noted). For a theozyme, embed the idealized residues as a pseudo-PDB. Record exactly which atoms define the catalytic geometry.
2. **Write the contig map.** Specify generated and fixed segments, e.g. `inpaint` the scaffold around `A10-12` (your three motif residues). Set total length and allow ranges so the model can choose comfortable linker lengths.
3. **Generate backbones.** Run RFdiffusion with a fixed seed for the first batch (e.g. 200 designs), 50 denoising steps, monomer mode. Inspect a few outputs for chain breaks or clashes before scaling up.
4. **Design sequences.** Run ProteinMPNN on each backbone (e.g. 8 sequences each, sampling temperature ≈ 0.1–0.2), fixing the motif residue identities.
5. **Refold and filter.** Predict each sequence's structure (AF2 initial-guess or ESMFold). Compute global Cα-RMSD, pLDDT, and — separately — **motif-atom RMSD** at the catalytic residues.
6. **Rank and select.** Keep backbones with at least one sequence at global RMSD < 2 Å, pLDDT > 80, **and** motif RMSD < 1 Å. Rank survivors by motif RMSD, then pLDDT.
7. **Record provenance.** Save seeds, contig maps, `.trb` files, and the filter thresholds in the design notebook.
:::

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 9.1 · Designability varies by fold"}
In-silico (self-consistency) success rates are **fold-dependent**. With standard fold conditioning and self-consistency filtering, a **TIM barrel** typically passes at roughly **42%**, while an **NTF2 fold** passes at roughly **54%** — the NTF2's simpler, more compact topology is easier for the model to realize designably. These are *designability* numbers, not activity numbers. Even a backbone that scaffolds your motif at sub-ångström RMSD and refolds confidently may be catalytically inert: nothing in residue-level diffusion reasons about the *substrate*, the transition state, or whether a real side-chain rotamer can reach the geometry you drew. Budget for testing on the order of dozens of designs to find activity, exactly as in Chapter 1.
:::

## 9.5 Where Classic Motif Scaffolding Breaks

Residue-level diffusion was a breakthrough, and for binders and structural motifs it is often enough. For catalysis it has two structural limitations that no amount of additional sampling fixes — and naming them precisely is the bridge to Chapter 10.

**Limitation 1: residue-index pre-specification.** The contig map forces you to commit, *before generation*, to how many residues separate your catalytic residues and roughly where each will sit along the chain. You are telling the model "the nucleophile is residue 11 and the base is residue 12 in the final protein." But the natural question in enzyme design is the inverse: *given a target active-site geometry, what backbone — of any length, with the catalytic residues at any positions — can present it?* Pre-committing the indices throws away most of the search space and bakes in a topology guess that may be incompatible with the chemistry.

**Limitation 2: the inverse-rotamer problem (rotamer explosion).** A theozyme specifies the geometry of catalytic *side-chain functional groups* — the imidazole nitrogen of a histidine, the carboxylate of an aspartate — relative to the substrate's transition state. But RFdiffusion scaffolds **backbone frames**. To translate "this carboxylate must sit here" into "this Cα–N–C frame must sit there," a designer must enumerate **inverse rotamers**: every backbone placement from which a given side-chain could, after choosing a rotamer, reach the desired functional-group position. For a single residue this is a handful of options; for a three- or four-residue array it is a combinatorial explosion that must be sampled *before* diffusion even runs, and most sampled combinations are geometrically infeasible or mutually incompatible. The model never reasons about the functional-group atoms directly; it only ever sees backbone frames, so the chemically meaningful quantity is laundered through a brittle, manual pre-processing step.

Together these limitations mean that residue-level diffusion treats the active site as an afterthought stapled onto a backbone-shaped hole, rather than as the specification from which the protein is grown. The fix is conceptually simple to state and was hard to build: let the model see and condition on the **functional-group atoms** and, ultimately, on **every atom**, so that the chemistry — not a backbone proxy of it — drives generation. That is precisely what RFdiffusion2, RFdiffusion3, and Riff-Diff do, and it is the subject of the central chapter that follows.

## Hands-On Lab 9 · Scaffold a Simple Catalytic Motif

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 9 · Scaffold a Simple Catalytic Motif with RFdiffusion"}
**Goal.** Scaffold a two- or three-residue catalytic motif into novel backbones, filter by self-consistency, and visualize the survivors.

1. **Choose a motif.** Use a serine-protease-style His–Asp pair or a simple His–His metal-binding motif. Extract the residues' coordinates from a PDB structure into a clean single-chain input file; note the atoms that define the catalytic geometry.
2. **Set up RFdiffusion.** Install from the RosettaCommons repo (or open the provided Colab). Confirm GPU access and that a trivial unconditional monomer run completes.
3. **Generate.** Write a contig map that places your motif residues at fixed coordinates and generates a scaffold of total length ≈ 100–140 around them. Run 200 backbones, 50 steps, recording the seed.
4. **Design sequences.** Run ProteinMPNN, 8 sequences per backbone, motif identities fixed, temperature ≈ 0.1.
5. **Filter.** Refold every sequence (ESMFold or AF2 initial-guess). Compute global Cα-RMSD, pLDDT, and motif-atom RMSD. Keep backbones with ≥1 sequence at global RMSD < 2 Å, pLDDT > 80, motif RMSD < 1 Å.
6. **Visualize.** In PyMOL or ChimeraX, superimpose each surviving design on the input motif; render a figure showing the scaffold and the preserved catalytic geometry.
7. **Reflect.** In one paragraph, state which limitation from Section 9.5 most constrained your motif choice.

**Platform:** Google Colab (GPU) or an HPC node with a single GPU · **Deliverable:** five filtered backbones, each with a refold figure, a table of RMSD/pLDDT/motif-RMSD, and recorded seeds and contig maps.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **9.1** Diffusion learns to reverse a known noising process; RFdiffusion diffuses residue *frames* on a manifold using a fine-tuned RoseTTAFold, so it starts from realistic protein geometry.
- **9.2** Unconditional generation samples plausible folds; conditional generation steers every denoising step, with channels for length/symmetry, fold, motif scaffolding, and binders.
- **9.3** RFdiffusion builds monomers, symmetric assemblies, binders, and motif scaffolds; fold conditioning targets enzyme-relevant topologies like the TIM barrel and NTF2 via secondary-structure and block-adjacency input.
- **9.4** Motif scaffolding holds catalytic residues fixed and in-paints a novel scaffold; ProteinMPNN designs the sequence and a self-consistency refold filters on global *and* motif-atom RMSD.
- **9.5** Residue-level diffusion is limited by residue-index pre-specification and the inverse-rotamer explosion — it conditions on backbone frames, not functional-group atoms — motivating the atom-level models of Chapter 10.
