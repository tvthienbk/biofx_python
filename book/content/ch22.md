## 22.1 Assay Design

To measure catalysis you need a **continuous, quantitative readout** of substrate consumed or product formed, ideally in a **96-well microplate** so you can run a substrate-concentration series with all controls in parallel on one plate reader. Two detection modes dominate.

**Colorimetric** assays follow absorbance change. The archetype for de novo hydrolase work is a *para*-nitrophenyl (pNP) ester or phosphate substrate: hydrolysis releases **para-nitrophenolate**, which absorbs strongly at **405–410 nm** (extinction coefficient ε ≈ 18,000 M⁻¹cm⁻¹ at pH ≥ 8, where the phenol is deprotonated and yellow). The rate of A₄₀₅ increase, divided by ε and the path length, gives the rate of product formation in molar units. **Fluorometric** assays follow fluorescence and are typically 10–1,000× more sensitive: 4-methylumbelliferyl (4-MU) esters release the fluorophore **4-methylumbelliferone** (ex ~360 nm, em ~450 nm), and coupled assays can tie almost any chemistry to a fluorescent or NAD(P)H (340 nm) readout.

Three design choices make or break the assay. First, **path length**: in a 96-well plate, the path length is not 1 cm — it depends on fill volume (≈ 0.29 cm for 100 µL in a standard well) and must be measured or corrected, or the assay run on a reader that normalizes to path length. Second, **the initial-rate (linear) regime**: kinetic parameters are defined from *initial velocities*, measured while less than ~10% of substrate is consumed and the product-vs-time trace is still linear; rates taken after curvature has set in are corrupted by substrate depletion and product inhibition. Third, **a product standard curve**: convert your raw signal (absorbance or RFU) to molar product using a dilution series of authentic product in the assay buffer, on the same plate — never assume the textbook ε holds in your exact conditions.

::: {.method data-title="Method 22.1 · A 96-well steady-state activity screen"}
1. **Buffer and temperature.** Choose an assay buffer at the design's intended pH (e.g., 50 mM HEPES or phosphate, pH 7.5–8.0, with NaCl to maintain ionic strength). Equilibrate plate reader to assay temperature (commonly 25 °C; confirm the design's Tm safely exceeds it).
2. **Substrate series.** Prepare a substrate dilution series spanning ~0.2·KM to ~5·KM if KM is roughly known; otherwise span a wide range (e.g., 7–8 concentrations from ~5 µM to ~2 mM). Keep organic co-solvent (DMSO to dissolve the substrate) constant and low (≤2–5% v/v) across all wells.
3. **Lay out controls on the same plate.** For each substrate concentration include: **enzyme** (the design), **catalytically-dead mutant**, **heat-killed enzyme**, **empty-vector mock-purified prep**, **buffer-only (no enzyme)**, and **substrate-background (no enzyme, to capture spontaneous hydrolysis)**. (See 22.4.)
4. **Start the reaction.** Pre-warm substrate and enzyme separately; initiate by adding enzyme (multichannel pipette or injector) to a fixed final concentration (e.g., 0.1–1 µM), final volume 100 µL/well. Mix briefly.
5. **Read kinetically.** Acquire absorbance (405 nm) or fluorescence (ex 360 / em 450 nm) every 10–30 s for 5–20 min at constant temperature.
6. **Extract initial rates.** For each well, fit the slope of the *linear* early region (≤10% conversion) of signal vs. time; convert to molar rate using the same-plate product standard curve and the corrected path length.
7. **Subtract backgrounds and tabulate.** Subtract the substrate-background (spontaneous) rate from each enzyme-containing well at the matching substrate concentration. Tabulate v₀ vs. [S] for fitting (22.2).
:::

## 22.2 Steady-State Michaelis–Menten

The dominant framework for a single-substrate enzyme is the **Michaelis–Menten model**, derived under the steady-state assumption that the enzyme–substrate complex ES is formed and broken down at equal rates after a brief transient. It predicts a hyperbolic dependence of initial velocity v₀ on substrate concentration [S]:

<p class="eqn">v₀ = (Vmax · [S]) / (KM + [S])</p>

Two parameters define the curve. **Vmax** is the maximal velocity, approached as [S] → ∞ when the enzyme is saturated. **KM** (the Michaelis constant) is the substrate concentration at which v₀ = Vmax/2; operationally it is the half-saturation point, and under simple assumptions it approximates the substrate concentration at which the active sites are half-occupied.

Fit the parameters by **nonlinear regression** of v₀ vs. [S] directly to the hyperbola — *not* by the historical linearizations (Lineweaver–Burk double-reciprocal, Eadie–Hofstee, Hanes–Woolf). Those linear transforms distort the error structure: the double-reciprocal plot, in particular, hugely amplifies errors at low [S], biases the fit, and is unacceptable as a primary analysis today. Use it only as a visual sanity check, if at all. A good fit requires data both well below and well above KM; if your highest [S] is still ≪ KM you will fit only the initial slope (which equals Vmax/KM) and cannot separate Vmax from KM individually — report that limitation honestly.

The dashed asymptote marks Vmax; the dotted line marks the half-saturation point.](assets/figures/kinetics.png)

## 22.3 kcat, KM, and kcat/KM

Vmax is an extensive quantity — it scales with how much enzyme you added — so it is converted to an intensive one. The **turnover number kcat** is the maximal number of catalytic cycles each active site completes per unit time:

<p class="eqn">kcat = Vmax / [E]ₜ</p>

where [E]ₜ is the total active-site concentration (which is why an accurate enzyme concentration, by A₂₈₀ with the correct ε or by amino-acid analysis, matters). kcat has units of s⁻¹.

The **specificity constant kcat/KM** is the single most important number for a designed enzyme. With units of M⁻¹s⁻¹, it is the apparent second-order rate constant for the reaction of free enzyme with free substrate at low [S], and it governs catalytic efficiency under physiological (sub-saturating) conditions. It is also the right figure of merit for comparing a weak design against background and against natural enzymes: natural enzymes span roughly 10²–10⁸ M⁻¹s⁻¹, with the "catalytically perfect" diffusion-limited ceiling near 10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹s⁻¹. A first-generation de novo enzyme commonly lands many orders of magnitude below that — kcat/KM of 10⁰–10² M⁻¹s⁻¹ is a *real but weak* catalyst, and that is the honest place most designs start before optimization (Part VI).

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 22.1 · Fitting Michaelis–Menten and computing kcat/KM"}
**Problem.** You assay a designed esterase at [E]ₜ = **0.50 µM** against pNP-acetate, subtract the spontaneous-hydrolysis background, and measure these background-corrected initial rates:

| [S] (µM) | v₀ (µM·s⁻¹) |
|---|---|
| 25 | 0.044 |
| 50 | 0.080 |
| 100 | 0.133 |
| 200 | 0.200 |
| 400 | 0.267 |
| 800 | 0.320 |

Nonlinear fit returns **Vmax = 0.40 µM·s⁻¹** and **KM = 200 µM**. Compute kcat and kcat/KM.

**Solution.** Check the fit: at [S] = KM = 200 µM the model predicts v₀ = Vmax/2 = 0.20 µM·s⁻¹, matching the measured 0.200 — consistent. Then

<p class="eqn">kcat = Vmax / [E]ₜ = (0.40 µM·s⁻¹) / (0.50 µM) = 0.80 s⁻¹</p>

<p class="eqn">kcat/KM = (0.80 s⁻¹) / (200 × 10⁻⁶ M) = 4.0 × 10³ M⁻¹s⁻¹</p>

So each active site turns over ~0.8 substrate molecules per second, and the specificity constant is 4.0 × 10³ M⁻¹s⁻¹ — a modest but real catalyst, ~4–5 orders below diffusion-limited. **Caveat:** kcat is only as accurate as [E]ₜ; if your A₂₈₀-based concentration is 20% high, kcat is 20% low. And none of this is a *catalysis* claim until the control suite (22.4) passes — Vmax and KM can be fit to an artifact just as easily as to an enzyme.
:::

## 22.4 The Control Suite

This section *is* the chapter. A hyperbolic v₀-vs-[S] curve and a clean kcat/KM are necessary but nowhere near sufficient to claim catalysis. Five controls, run on the same plate as the enzyme, convert a number into a defensible claim.

1. **Catalytically-dead mutant.** Mutate the proposed catalytic residue(s) to a non-functional substitute — the nucleophile of a designed serine hydrolase to alanine (Ser→Ala), a catalytic His→Ala, a metal-coordinating residue removed. This is the *single most important* control. If activity tracks the designed mechanism, the dead mutant must lose essentially all of it. A dead mutant that *retains* activity proves the "catalysis" does not come from the designed active site — it is contamination, adsorption, or an artifact (Worked Example 22.2).
2. **Heat-killed enzyme.** Boil the purified design (e.g., 95 °C, 10 min) and assay it. A heat-denatured protein cannot catalyze; residual activity indicates a heat-stable contaminant or a non-protein artifact.
3. **Empty-vector mock prep.** Carry an empty-vector (no-insert) culture through the *identical* expression and purification (IMAC + SEC). This captures any **host enzyme** that co-purifies on the Ni resin — *E. coli* has esterases and phosphatases that elute with His-tagged proteins and that have ruined many "designed hydrolase" claims. Activity in the mock prep is contamination, full stop.
4. **Buffer-only (no enzyme).** Confirms the reagents, plate, and reader give no signal change without enzyme.
5. **Substrate-background (spontaneous reaction).** Many activated substrates (pNP esters especially) hydrolyze spontaneously, and the rate rises with pH and temperature. Measure this no-enzyme rate at every [S] and subtract it; an "enzyme" rate that is merely the spontaneous rate is no enzyme at all.

The logic is subtractive and conjunctive: the claim "this design catalyzes the reaction" survives only if the **enzyme rate exceeds substrate-background**, the **dead mutant and heat-killed and empty-vector controls are at background**, and the activity **scales with enzyme concentration** in the expected first-order way. Any one failure kills the claim.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 22.2 · Interpreting a dead-mutant control"}
**Problem.** Your designed hydrolase shows a background-corrected rate of 0.18 µM·s⁻¹ at 200 µM substrate. The Ser→Ala "dead" mutant, prepared and purified identically, shows **0.16 µM·s⁻¹** under the same conditions. The empty-vector mock prep shows 0.15 µM·s⁻¹. What do you conclude?

**Solution.** The dead mutant retains ~89% of the wild-type design's activity, and the empty-vector prep shows ~83% — both essentially indistinguishable from the design within error. **Conclusion: the observed "activity" is not catalysis by the designed active site.** Because the empty-vector prep is also active, the most likely culprit is a **co-purifying host esterase** that rides the Ni column with any His-tagged eluate. The designed nucleophile is irrelevant to the signal — exactly what the dead-mutant control exists to expose. The correct action is *not* to report a kcat; it is to improve purification (extra wash steps, ion exchange, or a different tag), re-run with the full control suite, and only claim catalysis if the dead mutant and mock prep drop to background while the design does not. This is precisely the failure the chapter epigraph warns against.
:::

## 22.5 Real Catalysis vs. Binding, Adsorption, and Artifact

Even with controls passing, two seductive artifacts mimic catalysis and must be excluded explicitly.

**Stoichiometric (single-turnover) "activity."** A true catalyst is *regenerated*: it performs many cycles, so total product formed exceeds the enzyme's molar amount. The hallmark test is **turnover number > 1**: if you add 1 µM enzyme and form, over the run, far more than 1 µM product, the enzyme cycled. A reaction that produces a "burst" of product up to roughly the enzyme concentration and then **stops** is not catalysis — it is a stoichiometric reaction (e.g., the enzyme is covalently modified or acylated once and never released, or a tightly bound contaminant is consumed). Burst-then-plateau at ~[E]ₜ is a classic false positive.

**Adsorption and depletion.** A protein surface can *bind* a chromogenic substrate or sequester product, changing the optical signal without any chemistry. Designed proteins, often with engineered pockets and exposed hydrophobic patches, are prone to nonspecific adsorption. Distinguish binding from turnover by: confirming product is genuinely formed (LC-MS or HPLC detection of the authentic product, not just an absorbance change); checking that signal scales linearly with enzyme concentration as a *rate*, not as a one-time jump; and verifying the reaction continues well past the point where stoichiometric binding would saturate. A real enzyme makes product proportional to time and to enzyme amount, far in excess of its own concentration.

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 22.1"}
- Reporting kcat/KM without the dead-mutant, heat-killed, and empty-vector controls on the same data.
- A "burst then plateau" at product ≈ [E]ₜ presented as turnover — that is a single stoichiometric event, not catalysis.
- An absorbance/fluorescence change attributed to chemistry without independent (LC-MS/HPLC) confirmation that the authentic product formed (adsorption and inner-filter effects mimic activity).
- Using a Lineweaver–Burk fit as the primary parameter estimate (it distorts the error structure); always fit the hyperbola directly.
- Not subtracting the spontaneous substrate-hydrolysis background, especially for pNP esters at high pH.
- Reporting kcat without an accurately measured active-site concentration [E]ₜ.
- Activity that survives in the empty-vector mock prep — that is a host enzyme, not your design.
:::

## 22.6 Substrate Scope and Mechanism Checks

Once catalysis is established against the controls, two further lines of evidence connect the activity to the *designed mechanism*. **Substrate scope** probes selectivity: a designed enzyme built around a specific transition state should show a rational preference across a panel of related substrates (e.g., a series of pNP esters of varying acyl chain length), and the trend should make chemical sense given the modeled pocket. A flat, indiscriminate profile across chemically diverse substrates is a hint of a nonspecific surface effect rather than a defined active site.

**Mechanism checks** test the proposed chemistry directly. **pH–rate profiles** reveal the pKa of catalytic groups: a bell-shaped or sigmoidal log(kcat/KM)-vs-pH curve with an inflection near the expected pKa supports the involvement of a specific ionizable residue (e.g., a catalytic histidine near pH 6–7). **Mutagenesis beyond the dead mutant** — alanine-scanning the rest of the proposed catalytic constellation (the oxyanion-hole residues, the general base, a metal ligand) — tests whether each designed element contributes as intended; loss of activity tracking the designed residues is strong evidence the design works *as designed*. **Inhibitors** that target the proposed mechanism (a serine-hydrolase covalent inhibitor such as PMSF or a fluorophosphonate for a designed Ser nucleophile) should abolish activity. Where the chemistry permits, **isotope effects** and direct **LC-MS detection of a covalent intermediate** (e.g., the acyl-enzyme) close the loop from a measured rate to a validated mechanism — the bridge to structural validation in Chapter 23.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 22.1 · Kinetics-fitting tools"}
**GraphPad Prism** (v10) — the standard GUI for nonlinear regression; built-in Michaelis–Menten, substrate-inhibition, and allosteric models with proper weighting and confidence intervals.
**Python + lmfit / scipy.optimize.curve_fit** (lmfit 1.3) — scriptable, reproducible, version-controllable fitting; define the MM model, fit with weights, report parameter uncertainties — ideal in a pipeline.
**SciPy/`scipy.stats`** — for the 95% CI on parameters and for global fits across replicates.
**Always** fit the hyperbola directly (never Lineweaver–Burk as primary), weight appropriately, report parameter ± SE/CI, and overlay the fit on the raw v₀-vs-[S] data.
:::

::: {.conceptcheck data-title="Concept Check 22.1"}
A reviewer accepts your hyperbolic fit and clean kcat/KM but rejects the catalysis claim. You realize you ran only buffer-only and substrate-background controls. Which two controls did you omit that most directly distinguish *designed-active-site catalysis* from *co-purifying host enzyme*, and what result from each would rescue the claim?
:::

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 22 · Fitting kinetics and adjudicating hits against the control suite"}
You are given raw 96-well kinetic exports (signal vs. time) for **six candidate designs**, each with a full substrate series and the five-member control suite (dead mutant, heat-killed, empty-vector mock, buffer-only, substrate-background), plus a same-plate product standard curve and the measured [E]ₜ for each.

Produce a kinetics report that, for each design:

- Converts raw slopes to molar initial rates using the standard curve and corrected path length; subtracts the spontaneous-hydrolysis background.
- Fits v₀ vs. [S] to the Michaelis–Menten hyperbola by nonlinear regression (lmfit or Prism); reports Vmax, KM, kcat, and **kcat/KM** with 95% confidence intervals; overlays fit on data.
- Adjudicates against the control suite with an explicit **hit call**: *confirmed catalyst* only if the design rate exceeds background, the dead-mutant/heat-killed/empty-vector controls are at background, and total product exceeds [E]ₜ (turnover > 1). Otherwise classify as *artifact — host enzyme*, *artifact — stoichiometric/burst*, or *artifact — adsorption*, citing the specific failing control.
- Flags at least one design whose dead mutant retains activity (host-enzyme artifact) and one showing burst-then-plateau (stoichiometric, not catalytic).

**Platform:** data analysis (Python/lmfit or GraphPad Prism) · **Deliverable:** a six-design kinetics report with parameters, CIs, and control-adjudicated hit calls.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **22.1** Build a continuous, quantitative, 96-well assay (colorimetric pNP at 405 nm or fluorometric 4-MU); measure *initial rates* in the linear (<10% conversion) regime, with a same-plate product standard curve and correct path length.
- **22.2** Fit v₀ vs. [S] to the Michaelis–Menten hyperbola by nonlinear regression — never primarily by Lineweaver–Burk — to extract Vmax and KM.
- **22.3** Convert to intensive parameters: kcat = Vmax/[E]ₜ (turnover number, s⁻¹) and the all-important specificity constant kcat/KM (M⁻¹s⁻¹); first-generation designs are commonly weak (10⁰–10² M⁻¹s⁻¹) but real.
- **22.4** The five-member control suite — dead mutant, heat-killed, empty-vector mock, buffer-only, substrate-background — *is* the catalysis claim; the dead-mutant control is paramount.
- **22.5** Exclude stoichiometric "burst" reactions (turnover must exceed 1) and adsorption/optical artifacts (confirm authentic product by LC-MS/HPLC).
- **22.6** Substrate scope, pH–rate profiles, full active-site mutagenesis, mechanism-specific inhibitors, and intermediate detection connect the measured rate to the *designed mechanism*.

## Problem Set

**Review.**

1. Define initial velocity and explain why kinetic parameters must be measured in the <10% conversion regime.
2. State the Michaelis–Menten equation and give the physical meaning of Vmax and KM.
3. Why is nonlinear regression preferred over the Lineweaver–Burk plot for primary parameter estimation?
4. Define kcat and kcat/KM with units, and explain why kcat/KM is the better figure of merit for a weak designed enzyme.
5. List the five controls in the suite and state, for each, what artifact it excludes.
6. Why is the catalytically-dead mutant described as the single most important control?
7. Distinguish a true (multi-turnover) catalyst from a stoichiometric burst reaction operationally.

**Quantitative & Computational.**

1. An assay at [E]ₜ = 0.25 µM gives a fitted Vmax = 0.15 µM·s⁻¹ and KM = 80 µM. Compute kcat and kcat/KM.
2. para-Nitrophenolate has ε₄₀₅ ≈ 18,000 M⁻¹cm⁻¹. In a well with 100 µL (path length 0.29 cm), the absorbance rises at 0.012 min⁻¹. Compute the molar rate of product formation (M·s⁻¹).
3. Convert v₀-vs-[S] data {(20, 0.030), (40, 0.052), (80, 0.080), (160, 0.110), (320, 0.133)} (µM, µM·s⁻¹) to a double-reciprocal table and estimate Vmax and KM from the linear fit; then state why the nonlinear fit of the same data would be more trustworthy.
4. A design with kcat/KM = 50 M⁻¹s⁻¹ is improved 1,000-fold by directed evolution. Where does it now sit relative to the 10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹s⁻¹ diffusion limit (give orders of magnitude)?
5. You add 1.0 µM enzyme and observe 25 µM total product formed over 20 min before the curve plateaus. Compute the minimum turnover number and state whether this is consistent with catalysis.
6. The spontaneous substrate-hydrolysis rate at 200 µM is 0.05 µM·s⁻¹ and the enzyme-containing well gives 0.21 µM·s⁻¹. Compute the background-corrected rate and the fold-enhancement over background.
7. A dead mutant retains 8% of the design's background-corrected activity, and the empty-vector mock shows 3%. Is the catalysis claim supportable? Quantify your reasoning and state the threshold you would require.
8. Given Vmax = 0.40 µM·s⁻¹ with 95% CI [0.36, 0.44] and KM = 200 µM with 95% CI [170, 235] at [E]ₜ = 0.50 µM, propagate the uncertainty to a 95% interval on kcat/KM (approximate; state your method).

**Challenge.**

1. Design a complete experimental plan to prove that a designed serine hydrolase works by the intended acyl-enzyme mechanism — listing every control, mutant, inhibitor, and spectroscopic/MS measurement, and the specific result that would *falsify* the mechanism at each step.
2. A design shows a hyperbolic curve, kcat/KM = 2 × 10³ M⁻¹s⁻¹, a dead mutant at background, and an empty-vector mock at background — but the rate does *not* scale linearly with enzyme concentration above ~0.5 µM, and product plateaus near 3× [E]ₜ. Propose at least two mechanistic explanations and the experiments to distinguish them, and state what you would and would not claim.
3. Critique the following sentence from a manuscript and rewrite it to a defensible standard: "The designed enzyme hydrolyzed the substrate with kcat/KM = 1.2 × 10⁴ M⁻¹s⁻¹, demonstrating de novo catalytic activity."
