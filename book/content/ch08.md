## 8.1 The Inverse Problem

The forward problem of structural biology — sequence to structure — is what Chapter 6's predictors solve. Design needs the **inverse**: given a backbone, find a sequence that folds to it. Stated baldly, *given a shape, what words spell it?* The two directions are not symmetric, and the asymmetry is the reason inverse folding is its own field.

The first difficulty is that the mapping is **many-to-one**. Vast numbers of sequences fold to essentially the same backbone — natural homologs sharing a fold often have well under 30% sequence identity — so the inverse problem has no unique answer but a whole distribution of answers. That is good news (you have freedom) and bad news (you need a principled way to choose). The second difficulty is **combinatorial**: a 200-residue protein has 20²⁰⁰ possible sequences, a space no enumeration will ever touch. The third is **context-dependence**: the best amino acid at one position depends on its neighbors in three dimensions, so positions cannot be chosen independently. A buried position wants a hydrophobic residue; a position lining a charged pocket wants a complementary charge; the choices are coupled through the structure.

Classical methods (Rosetta's `fixbb`) attacked this with a physics-based energy function and stochastic search over rotamer libraries — powerful but slow and only as good as the energy function. The deep-learning reframing is cleaner and faster: train a neural network on the PDB to predict, for each position on a given backbone, a probability distribution over the 20 amino acids *conditioned on the surrounding structure*. Sequence design then becomes sampling from those learned conditional distributions. This is the **message-passing inverse-folding** paradigm, and ProteinMPNN is its canonical instance.

## 8.2 ProteinMPNN

**ProteinMPNN** (Dauparas et al., 2022) is the workhorse of modern sequence design and one of the most widely used tools in the field. It represents the fixed backbone as a **graph**: each residue is a node, and edges connect each residue to its nearest spatial neighbors, carrying geometric features (inter-atomic distances and orientations between backbone atoms). A **message-passing neural network** updates node representations by exchanging information along edges, so each position "learns about" its three-dimensional environment. A decoder then emits, for each position, a distribution over the 20 amino acids.

Two design choices make ProteinMPNN both fast and robust. First, it conditions only on **backbone** geometry (N, Cα, C, O, and a virtual Cβ) — it does not need or use side-chain atoms as input, which is exactly right for designing a sequence onto a side-chain-free generated backbone. Second, it decodes **autoregressively** in a random order: having committed an amino acid at some positions, it conditions the remaining choices on them, capturing the coupling between neighbors that the inverse problem demands. The original network is tiny by modern standards (a few million parameters) and runs in well under a second per sequence on a GPU, so a designer routinely samples dozens of sequences per backbone.

The headline result that earned ProteinMPNN its place: on native backbones it achieves a **sequence recovery of about 50–53%** (versus ~33% for Rosetta), and — far more importantly — its designed sequences **refold** to the target backbone in AF2/ESMFold at much higher rates and express more solubly in the lab than Rosetta designs. It is the default first move once a backbone exists. But note the limitation that motivates the next section: vanilla ProteinMPNN sees *only the protein backbone*. It does not see the substrate, the metal, or the cofactor — and an enzyme is defined by those.

## 8.3 LigandMPNN: Substrate-, Metal-, and Cofactor-Aware Design

For enzyme design, the central tool of this chapter is **LigandMPNN** (Dauparas et al., 2023). It extends ProteinMPNN's graph with the one thing an enzyme cannot do without: the **non-protein context**. Ligand atoms, metal ions, and cofactor atoms become additional nodes in the graph, with edges to nearby protein residues carrying the same kind of geometric and chemical features. When LigandMPNN chooses an amino acid for a pocket-lining position, it is now conditioning on the actual atoms of the bound substrate or the coordinating metal — not on an empty cavity.

Why this matters is concrete and chemical. A position 3 Å from a substrate's carboxylate "wants" a different residue (an arginine or lysine to complement the charge) than the same position next to a hydrophobic ring (which wants a phenylalanine or leucine). A residue completing a Zn²⁺ coordination sphere must be a histidine, cysteine, aspartate, or glutamate placed to point its coordinating atom at the metal. Vanilla ProteinMPNN, blind to the ligand, will pack the pocket for *generic* stability and routinely choose residues that are wrong for the chemistry — often filling a catalytic site with hydrophobics because that lowers the apparent backbone energy. LigandMPNN designs the pocket *for the substrate it must act on*, and on native protein–ligand interfaces it both recovers interface residues better and produces sequences whose predicted binding and catalytic geometry hold up. For any campaign with a bound substrate, metal, or cofactor — which is to say essentially every enzyme — LigandMPNN, not ProteinMPNN, is the correct default.

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 8.1 · Sequence recovery is not function"}
Sequence recovery (~50% for ProteinMPNN on native backbones) is a *benchmark* metric: it measures how often the model re-picks the wild-type residue. It is **not** a measure of activity, and a designer who optimizes it is optimizing the wrong thing.

- High recovery on a native backbone can mean the model is simply rediscovering the natural sequence — useful as a sanity check, useless as a claim of design.
- A designed enzyme that *works* may share little sequence identity with any natural protein; low recovery is not failure.
- The metric that matters is downstream: does the sequence **refold** to the intended backbone with the catalytic geometry intact (Chapter 6), express, and **turn over substrate** in the assay (Part V)?

Treat recovery as a model-development number, not a design objective.
:::

## 8.4 FAMPNN and ESM-IF

Two further tools widen the toolkit. **FAMPNN** (Full-Atom MPNN, 2025) addresses a gap in the MPNN line: ProteinMPNN and LigandMPNN predict the *identity* of each residue but not its **side-chain conformation**, leaving rotamer packing to a separate step. FAMPNN models sequence and full side-chain atoms *jointly*, sampling both the amino-acid identity and its rotamer in one explicit all-atom representation. For enzyme design this is attractive because, as Chapter 7 argued, catalysis is an atomic phenomenon — the orientation of a catalytic side chain, not merely its identity, is what positions chemistry — and a model that places those atoms during design can score the catalytic geometry directly rather than hoping a later packing step recovers it.

**ESM-IF** (ESM Inverse Folding, Hsu et al., 2022) takes a different route. It is a sequence-to-structure inverse model trained at scale — crucially, augmented with millions of **AlphaFold-predicted** structures, not just experimental ones, which vastly expanded its training data. ESM-IF brings the representational power of protein language models to inverse folding and is a useful orthogonal designer: where it and LigandMPNN agree on a position, confidence is higher; where they diverge, the position deserves scrutiny. As with structure predictors (Chapter 6), methodological independence is the value — do not simply pick whichever model gives the answer you wanted.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 8.1 · Inverse folding (version-stamped)"}
**ProteinMPNN** (Dauparas et al., 2022) — backbone-only graph message-passing; ~50% native recovery; the default once a side-chain-free backbone exists. Key knobs: `--sampling_temp`, `--backbone_noise`, `--fixed_positions`/`--chain_id_jsons`.  
**LigandMPNN** (Dauparas et al., 2023) — ProteinMPNN plus ligand/metal/cofactor context nodes; the correct default for enzymes; same sampling knobs, plus parsed ligand input and side-chain packing for the designed sequence.  
**FAMPNN** (2025) — joint sequence + full-atom side-chain (rotamer) sampling in one model; designs and scores catalytic side-chain geometry directly.  
**ESM-IF** (Hsu et al., 2022) — language-model inverse folding trained on experimental + predicted structures; useful orthogonal check.  
Record the model, weights, sampling temperature, backbone noise, seed, and the exact set of fixed positions for every design batch.
:::

## 8.5 Temperature, Noise, and Fixed Residues

Three controls turn an inverse-folding model from a black box into a design instrument.

**Sampling temperature** rescales the per-position amino-acid distribution before sampling. Low temperature (≈ 0.1) sharpens the distribution toward the model's single most confident residue at each position: designs are conservative, similar to each other, high in sequence recovery, and very likely to refold — but lacking diversity, which means lacking the variety you need to find a rare functional hit. High temperature (≈ 0.3 and above) flattens the distribution: designs become diverse and explore more of sequence space, but each individual sequence is riskier and refolds less reliably. The craft is to sweep temperature deliberately — generate at several values, then filter survivors by refold quality (Chapter 6) — rather than guessing one value. A common practical regime is to sample broadly at moderate temperature and let the downstream AF3/ESMFold filter do the culling.

**Backbone noise** adds small Gaussian perturbations to the input backbone coordinates during design. A generated backbone is never perfect, and a model that trusts every coordinate exactly will overfit to its flaws; a little noise makes the design robust to backbone imprecision and often improves experimental success. Modest noise (a few tenths of an Å) is common when designing onto freshly generated, un-relaxed backbones.

**Fixed positions** are the control that makes enzyme design possible at all. You instruct the model to *hold certain residues constant* — most importantly the catalytic residues, whose identity and placement came from your theozyme and must not be redesigned — while it designs everything around them. The model then conditions its choices for the surrounding positions on the fixed catalytic set. Without this, the inverse folder would happily redesign your catalytic histidine into a leucine because that lowers the backbone energy. With it, the active site is preserved and the rest of the protein is built to support it.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 8.1 · Choosing a sampling temperature"}
**Problem.** You have one validated backbone with a bound substrate and a fixed catalytic triad. You can afford to test ~48 sequences in the lab. You want diversity (to hit a rare active design) but also high refold rates (to not waste the 48 slots). How do you set temperature?

**Solution.** Do not pick one value — sweep. A defensible protocol:

1. Generate ~200 LigandMPNN sequences at each of T = 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 (catalytic residues fixed), 600 total.
2. Refold every sequence with AF3 or ESMFold; keep those with scRMSD to the target backbone < 2 Å and the catalytic-residue geometry preserved.
3. Inspect refold yield per temperature. Typically T = 0.1 yields the highest fraction passing but the lowest diversity (many near-identical sequences); T = 0.3 yields more diverse survivors but a lower pass fraction.
4. Build the test set of 48 from the *survivors*, deliberately spanning the temperature range and maximizing sequence diversity among passers — so you sample functional space without spending slots on sequences that already failed the refold filter.

The principle: temperature controls the diversity/refold trade-off, and the downstream refold filter — not the temperature alone — decides what reaches the bench.
:::

::: {.method data-title="Method 8.1 · Fixing catalytic and second-shell residues"}
1. **Identify the catalytic set** from the theozyme: the residues whose side-chain atoms make the chemistry (e.g., the Ser–His–Asp triad, the metal-coordinating His/Cys, the oxyanion-hole donors). Record their residue indices.
2. **Identify the second shell:** residues that orient or tune the catalytic set — the aspartate that fixes a histidine's tautomer, residues that set a key pK_a, or that complete the metal geometry. Decide which to fix and which to let the model optimize.
3. **Write the fixed-positions specification** in the tool's format (LigandMPNN `fixed_residues` / per-chain JSON), listing exactly the indices to hold constant. Double-check the numbering matches the input PDB.
4. **Provide the ligand/metal context** so the model designs the remaining positions *in the presence of* the substrate and the fixed catalytic atoms.
5. **Generate** with chosen temperature and backbone noise; the fixed residues are emitted unchanged and the rest are designed conditioned on them.
6. **Verify** in the output that the fixed residues are byte-for-byte preserved and that refolding reproduces both the backbone and the catalytic geometry. A fixed residue that "moves" in the refold signals a backbone that cannot support it.
:::

## 8.6 Sequence Recovery versus Functional Novelty

The chapter closes on the trap that catches careless campaigns. **Sequence recovery** — the fraction of positions where the model re-picks the wild-type residue on a native backbone — is the field's standard benchmark, and ProteinMPNN's ~50% is genuinely impressive. But recovery is a measure of *agreement with nature*, and de novo design is, by definition, the attempt to do something nature did not. Optimizing for recovery optimizes for *recapitulating evolution's answer*, which is the opposite of the goal.

The **novelty trap** has two faces. The first is self-deception: a design that scores beautifully on recovery and refolds confidently may simply be a slightly perturbed natural protein — you have rediscovered an enzyme, not designed one (recall Chapter 1's operational novelty thresholds: low TM-score *and* low sequence identity to the PDB). The second is the misuse of recovery as a *design objective*: because high temperature lowers recovery, a designer chasing the benchmark will drive temperature down, collapse diversity, and produce a batch of near-identical conservative sequences — exactly the wrong move when you need variety to find a rare functional hit.

The honest resolution is to demote recovery to a development diagnostic and promote the metrics that track function. A design is judged by whether it **refolds** to the intended backbone with the catalytic geometry intact (scRMSD < 2 Å, catalytic atoms in place — Chapter 6), whether it is **novel** by structure and sequence (Chapter 1), and ultimately whether it **turns over substrate** in the assay (Part V). Sequence recovery answers "does this look like a known protein?" — a question whose desired answer, for de novo enzyme design, is often *no*. Keep your eye on refold and turnover; let recovery stay where it belongs, in the model-builder's report.

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 8.1"}
- **Reporting sequence recovery as evidence of a good *design*.** Recovery measures similarity to nature; it certifies neither novelty nor function.
- **Redesigning catalytic residues by forgetting to fix them.** Always verify the fixed set is preserved byte-for-byte in the output.
- **Designing an enzyme with vanilla ProteinMPNN** when a substrate/metal/cofactor is present — use LigandMPNN so the pocket is designed *for* the ligand.
- **Picking the single temperature (or the single inverse-folder) that gave the answer you wanted** instead of sweeping and filtering by refold quality.
- **Calling a confident refold a success** — refold is necessary, turnover (Part V) is the proof.
:::

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 8 · LigandMPNN design around a bound substrate"}
Start from a backbone with a bound substrate and a defined catalytic set (use the holo structure validated in Lab 6, or an instructor-provided design with a ligand).

1. **Fix the catalytic set** following Method 8.1; provide the parsed ligand (and metal/cofactor) to LigandMPNN.
2. **Temperature sweep.** Generate ~100 sequences each at T = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 (backbone noise ~0.1 Å), catalytic residues fixed.
3. **Recapitulation / refold.** Predict each design's holo structure with AF3 (or Boltz-2). Record per-design scRMSD to the target backbone, active-site RMSD, pLDDT/PAE at the catalytic residues, and ligand-pose RMSD.
4. **Novelty.** For the best designs, run Foldseek + a sequence search; record TM-score and sequence identity to the PDB (Chapter 1 thresholds).
5. **Analyze the trade-off.** Plot refold pass-rate and sequence diversity against temperature; identify the temperature that best balances the two for your target.

**Platform:** Google Colab or local GPU (LigandMPNN + AF3/Boltz-2) · **Deliverable:** a table of designs — design ID, temperature, scRMSD, active-site RMSD, catalytic pLDDT/PAE, ligand-pose RMSD, TM-score and identity to PDB, pass/fail — with a one-paragraph reading of how temperature traded diversity against refold quality and which designs you would carry to the bench.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **8.1** Inverse folding seeks a sequence for a fixed backbone; it is hard because the map is many-to-one, the space is combinatorial (20²⁰⁰), and positions are coupled through 3D context.
- **8.2** ProteinMPNN encodes the backbone as a graph and decodes amino-acid distributions autoregressively; ~50% native recovery and strong refold/expression make it the default — but it is blind to the ligand.
- **8.3** LigandMPNN adds substrate, metal, and cofactor nodes so the pocket is designed *for the chemistry*; it is the correct default for essentially every enzyme.
- **8.4** FAMPNN designs sequence and side-chain rotamers jointly (catalytic geometry directly); ESM-IF brings language-model power and serves as an orthogonal check.
- **8.5** Temperature trades diversity against refold reliability (low ≈ 0.1 conservative, high ≈ 0.3+ diverse); backbone noise adds robustness; fixing catalytic residues preserves the active site.
- **8.6** Sequence recovery measures agreement with nature, not function or novelty; judge designs by refold (scRMSD, catalytic geometry), novelty (TM-score + identity), and ultimately turnover.
