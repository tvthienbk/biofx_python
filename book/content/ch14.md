## 14.1 Transition-State Modeling with DFT

Every claim a designed enzyme makes about catalysis is, ultimately, a claim about a **transition state** — the highest-energy configuration along the reaction coordinate, the fleeting arrangement of nuclei at which old bonds are half-broken and new bonds half-formed. Pauling's principle says that an enzyme accelerates a reaction by binding this transition state more tightly than the ground state. To design a protein complementary to the transition state, you must first know the transition state's geometry and charge distribution. That is a quantum-chemistry problem, and for molecules of catalytic interest the practical tool is **density functional theory (DFT)**.

A transition state is, formally, a **first-order saddle point** on the potential-energy surface: a stationary point (the gradient of the energy with respect to every nuclear coordinate is zero) at which the energy is a *maximum* along exactly one direction — the reaction coordinate — and a *minimum* along all others. That single direction of negative curvature is the signature you will verify computationally: when you diagonalize the matrix of second derivatives (the **Hessian**), a true transition state has **exactly one negative eigenvalue**, which corresponds to **exactly one imaginary vibrational frequency**. A geometry with zero imaginary frequencies is a minimum (a reactant, product, or intermediate, not a transition state); a geometry with two or more is a higher-order saddle that does not correspond to a physical reaction path.

Locating a saddle point is harder than minimizing to a well, because you are climbing along one coordinate while descending along all others. The workhorse algorithm is the **eigenvector-following / quasi-Newton** TS optimizer (in many codes a partitioned rational-function optimization, P-RFO), which uses the Hessian to identify the mode to maximize along. It needs a starting geometry already close to the saddle. You obtain that guess in one of three ways: build it by hand from chemical intuition (stretch the breaking bond to ~1.3–1.5× its equilibrium length, partially form the new one); run a **relaxed surface scan** along the presumed reaction coordinate and take the highest point as the guess; or use a **chain-of-states** method — the **nudged elastic band (NEB)** or the more robust **growing-string method** — which optimizes a string of images between reactant and product and locates the highest-energy image near the saddle. For a clean single-step reaction like the Kemp elimination, a scan along the C–H···base distance gives an excellent initial guess.

After the optimizer converges, you must **validate** the result, and this validation is non-negotiable. Compute the **analytic Hessian** (a frequency calculation) at the optimized geometry and confirm one and only one imaginary frequency. Then **animate that imaginary mode**: its atomic displacements must visibly correspond to the chemistry you intended — the proton moving from carbon to base, the ring opening — and not to some spurious motion like a methyl rotation. Finally, to prove that this saddle actually connects *your* reactant and product, run an **intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC)** calculation, which follows the steepest-descent path downhill from the saddle in both directions; it should terminate at the intended reactant complex on one side and the intended product on the other. A transition state that passes the frequency, mode-animation, and IRC checks is a transition state you can build a protein around. One that skips them is a guess wearing a lab coat.

::: {.method data-title="Method 14.1 · DFT transition-state optimization"}
1. **Draw the mechanism** and identify the bond(s) made/broken and the reaction coordinate. Decide the **QM model**: the substrate plus truncated catalytic groups (e.g., acetate for a carboxylate base, methylamine/imidazole for a base, methylindole for π-stacking). Cap truncations at sp³ carbons to avoid artifacts.
2. **Set charge and multiplicity** for the whole QM system; an error here invalidates everything downstream.
3. **Generate a TS guess**: a relaxed scan along the reaction coordinate (e.g., the C–H distance to the base), or an NEB/growing-string path between optimized reactant and product complexes. Take the highest-energy structure as the guess.
4. **Optimize to the saddle** with a TS optimizer (eigenvector following / P-RFO). Supply or compute an initial Hessian (`calc_hess true` in ORCA) — a good initial Hessian is the difference between convergence and chaos.
5. **Frequency calculation** at the converged geometry. Confirm **exactly one imaginary frequency**.
6. **Animate the imaginary mode** and confirm it is the intended bond-breaking/forming motion.
7. **IRC** in both directions; confirm it connects the intended reactant and product complexes.
8. **Refine if needed**: re-optimize at a larger basis set; add an implicit solvent model (CPCM/SMD) if the active site is polar; recompute frequencies. Record the final energy, the imaginary frequency (cm⁻¹), and the geometry.
:::

## 14.2 Functionals and Basis Sets You Can Trust

DFT replaces the intractable many-electron wavefunction with the electron *density* and an approximate **exchange–correlation functional**. The functional is the one ingredient that is not derived from first principles — it is chosen — so a defensible calculation rests on choosing a functional with a track record for your kind of chemistry, and stating it. You do not need to invent a functional; you need to pick a good one and report it honestly.

For organic and enzymatic transition states, three families cover almost all needs. **B3LYP** is the long-standing hybrid functional and remains a reasonable default for geometries; its great weakness is that it omits long-range dispersion (London/van der Waals attraction), so it should essentially always be used with **Grimme's D3 dispersion correction** (with Becke–Johnson damping), written **B3LYP-D3(BJ)**. **ωB97X-D** is a range-separated hybrid with dispersion built in; it is robust for both geometries and barrier heights and is an excellent general choice for design work. **M06-2X**, a meta-hybrid from the Minnesota family, was parameterized with thermochemistry and kinetics in mind and is particularly good for **barrier heights** and non-covalent interactions, which is exactly what a theozyme cares about. A pragmatic protocol: optimize the geometry with ωB97X-D or B3LYP-D3(BJ), then take a single-point energy at a higher level (a larger basis, or a double-hybrid such as **B2PLYP-D3**, or DLPNO-CCSD(T) for a benchmark) on the optimized geometry.

The **basis set** is the set of functions used to expand the molecular orbitals; larger basis sets approach the complete-basis limit at rapidly rising cost. The Karlsruhe **def2** family is the modern standard. Use **def2-SVP** (split-valence polarized) for the cheap, exploratory steps — the relaxed scan, the initial TS optimization, the geometry — where speed matters and small basis-set error cancels. Then refine geometry and especially energies with the larger **def2-TZVP** (triple-zeta valence polarized) or def2-TZVPP. The common, defensible recipe is *optimize and do frequencies at the smaller basis, single-point energy at the larger basis*: for example, optimize at ωB97X-D/def2-SVP, frequencies at the same level, then a def2-TZVP single point. Anions (like the developing phenolate in the Kemp product) benefit from **diffuse functions** (def2-SVPD/def2-TZVPD or a "ma-" minimally-augmented set), because diffuse charge density is poorly described by compact basis functions. For heavy atoms (beyond ~Kr) the def2 sets carry matched effective core potentials automatically.

Two practical accelerators are worth knowing because everyone uses them. The **RIJCOSX** approximation (resolution-of-identity for Coulomb, chain-of-spheres for exchange) speeds hybrid-functional calculations by an order of magnitude with negligible error, given the matching auxiliary basis (e.g., def2/J). And an **implicit solvent model** — **CPCM** or **SMD** — wraps the QM system in a polarizable continuum with the dielectric of water (or a lower dielectric to mimic a partly buried active site). Solvent matters enormously for charged transition states: gas-phase barriers for the Kemp elimination are qualitatively misleading because they ignore how the developing charge is stabilized. Report the functional, dispersion correction, basis set(s), and solvent model together as a single method string, e.g. *ωB97X-D/def2-TZVP//ωB97X-D/def2-SVP, SMD(water)*.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 14.1 · Quantum-chemistry packages (version-stamped)"}
**ORCA** (v6.0, 2024) — free for academic use; excellent TS optimization, DLPNO-CCSD(T), RIJCOSX, NEB-TS built in. The de facto workhorse for this kind of work. Keyword line example: `! wB97X-D3 def2-TZVP def2/J RIJCOSX OptTS Freq TightOpt CPCM(water)`.
**Psi4** (v1.9, 2024) — open-source, Python-scriptable; strong for method development and reproducible workflows; geometric/`optimize` with a TS flag via the `geomeTRIC` engine.
**Gaussian** (g16, rev. C.02) — commercial, ubiquitous in legacy literature; `Opt=(TS,CalcFC,NoEigenTest)` and `Freq`, `IRC`. Cite it if you use it; many older theozymes were built here.
**xtb / GFN2-xTB** (v6.7) — semiempirical tight-binding; not for final energies but superb for cheap conformer searches and initial-Hessian guesses (`crest` for conformers, `--hess`).
Always report code, version, method, basis, dispersion, and solvent model.
:::

## 14.3 QM/MM When Environment Matters

A truncated gas-phase (or implicit-solvent) transition state is enough to *build* a theozyme, and for many teaching campaigns it is all you need. But the central premise of enzyme catalysis is that the **protein environment** does work on the transition state — through hydrogen bonds, an oriented electrostatic field, and steric preorganization — that an implicit continuum cannot capture. When you need to compute a barrier *inside* a real or modeled active site, the method is **QM/MM**.

QM/MM partitions the system: the small region where chemistry happens (substrate, the bonds being made and broken, the catalytic side chains) is treated with quantum mechanics, while the surrounding protein and solvent are treated with a classical molecular-mechanics **force field** (AMBER, CHARMM). The two regions are coupled, most importantly through **electrostatic embedding**, in which the MM point charges enter the QM Hamiltonian so that the protein's electrostatic field polarizes the QM electron density — this is precisely the preorganization effect that distinguishes an enzyme from a beaker. Where the QM/MM boundary cuts a covalent bond, a **link atom** (usually hydrogen) or a frozen-orbital scheme caps the dangling valence; the boundary should always be placed across a nonpolar C–C bond, never across a polar or conjugated bond.

For design, QM/MM plays two roles. First, as a **refinement**: take a candidate enzyme model from later in the pipeline (a designed sequence threaded onto a backbone, Chapters 15–16) and recompute the reaction barrier in its actual pocket, which is a far stronger filter than shape complementarity alone. Second, as a **diagnostic**: when a design is inactive in the assay, a QM/MM barrier can tell you whether the geometry is wrong, the electrostatics are unfavorable, or a key water is missing. Common engines are **ORCA in combination with a force-field driver**, **Q-Chem/CHARMM**, **NWChem**, and the free **CP2K** for larger or QM/MM-MD work; the open **QMMM** tooling in modern packages has made the method routine. Be honest about its cost and its sensitivities: QM/MM barriers depend strongly on the starting structure, the choice of QM region (it must be large enough that nothing chemically important sits in the MM region), and conformational sampling — a single snapshot can mislead, so where the budget allows, average over an ensemble.

::: {.conceptcheck data-title="Concept Check 14.1"}
You optimize a Kemp-elimination transition state in the gas phase and obtain a barrier of 34 kcal/mol. Repeating the calculation with an SMD(water) continuum gives 24 kcal/mol, and a QM/MM calculation inside a designed pocket gives 18 kcal/mol. What physical effect does each successive drop most likely capture, and which number is the most relevant to predicting catalysis?
:::

## 14.4 From Transition State to Theozyme

A **theozyme** ("theoretical enzyme," a term coined by Tantillo, Chen, and Houk in 1998) is the transition state together with the minimal set of catalytic functional groups, each frozen in the position and orientation that optimally stabilizes that transition state. It is an idealized active site stripped of its protein scaffold — pure catalytic geometry. Constructing it is the act of converting the chemistry of §13.3's minimal catalytic set into explicit coordinates, and it is the single highest-leverage step in the entire campaign: every backbone you generate, every sequence you design, and every constraint you filter against descends from this geometry. A theozyme that is right by half an ångström can be realized; a theozyme that is wrong by two ångströms or thirty degrees cannot be rescued by any amount of downstream compute.

The procedure builds outward from the validated transition state of §14.1. For each functional group in the minimal catalytic set, choose a truncated chemical model (a carboxylate base as acetate or propanoate; a backbone or side-chain amide, or a guanidinium/hydroxyl, as the H-bond donor; an indole or benzene ring for π-stacking). Place each group near the substrate atom it acts on, then **optimize the full assembly with the same DFT method** — but with the transition-state core held at its validated geometry (often by freezing the key internal coordinates, or by re-running a constrained TS optimization of the whole complex). The optimization finds the energetically preferred distance and angle of each catalytic group relative to the TS: the base settles at the O···H or N···H distance and the C–H···base angle that best stabilizes the proton in flight; the H-bond donor settles where it best caps the developing negative charge; the aromatic ring settles at the stacking distance and offset that best preorganizes the planar substrate.

What you extract from the optimized theozyme are not just atomic positions but **catalytic geometric constraints**: a small set of distances, angles, and dihedrals relating the catalytic-group atoms to the substrate/TS atoms, each with a *tolerance*. The tolerance is a design decision — too tight and no backbone can satisfy it; too loose and the geometry is no longer catalytic. A defensible practice is to set tolerances from the curvature of the energy surface: scan each constraint and allow the range over which the stabilization energy stays within ~1–2 kcal/mol of its optimum. These constraint definitions — base oxygen 2.7 ± 0.3 Å from the abstracted proton, O···H–C angle 165 ± 15°, donor 2.9 Å from the phenolate oxygen, ring centroid 3.6 Å from the substrate plane at a defined offset — are the deliverable that the rest of Part IV consumes.

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 14.1 · Garbage theozyme, guaranteed failure"}
No downstream method is more accurate than the theozyme it is given. If the transition state is wrong — wrong protonation state, a saddle that the IRC shows connects the wrong species, a barrier computed in vacuum for a charged TS — then every backbone, every sequence, and every filter is faithfully optimizing toward a structure that does not catalyze the reaction. The pipeline will still produce confident-looking, well-folded proteins; they will simply be inactive. This is the most expensive failure mode in the field precisely because it is invisible until the assay. Spend the QM effort here. A week of careful DFT is cheaper than a failed gene-synthesis order and a month of negative kinetics. The frequency check, the mode animation, and the IRC are the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.
:::

## 14.5 Extracting Model-Ready Inputs (RFD2/RFD3/Riff-Diff)

The theozyme is a small-molecule QM construct; the backbone generators of Chapter 15 need it in their own formats. Extraction is a careful, error-prone bookkeeping step — atom-name mismatches and coordinate-frame errors here silently corrupt the whole campaign — so it deserves explicit attention.

First, write the theozyme to a **PDB/SDF pair**: the substrate/transition-state model as a ligand (SDF or a PDB `HETATM` block with an accompanying parameter/`.params` file or CCD-style definition), and each catalytic functional group as a residue or fragment with standard atom names so that distances can be defined unambiguously. Keep a single, documented coordinate frame; every constraint references it.

The three generators consume the geometry differently. **RFdiffusion2 (RFD2)** and its successor **RFdiffusion3 (RFD3)** are **atomized-motif** generators: you provide the ligand (the TS model) and the catalytic side chains as an *enzyme motif*, and the model diffuses a backbone that places those atomized residues around the ligand. The input is the motif coordinates plus a specification of which residues are catalytic and which atoms must be presented to the ligand; in practice this is a templated **PDB of the motif** plus a config (YAML/JSON or command-line flags) declaring the ligand, the catalytic residues, and the contacts. **Riff-Diff** (Höcker and coworkers) takes a complementary route: it assembles a **theozyme of discrete rotamers** into a consistent ensemble and then builds backbones to support it, so its input is most naturally the set of catalytic rotamers with their geometric relationships to the ligand. In all three, the same physical constraints from §14.4 are re-expressed in the generator's language — but the *numbers* (the distances and angles from your DFT optimum) are identical. Export them once, then translate.

The historical lineage is worth keeping in view, because it explains the file formats. The classic Rosetta route used **RosettaMatch** with a **constraint (`.cst`) file** — a text specification of the distance/angle/dihedral relationships between catalytic atoms and the ligand, with their tolerances — to search natural scaffolds for backbones that could host the theozyme (Zanghellini et al., 2006). The modern diffusion generators replaced *searching* with *generating*, but the constraint vocabulary survives almost unchanged: a `.cst`-style table of `distanceAB`, `angle_A`, `angle_B`, `torsion_A`, `torsion_AB`, `torsion_B`, each with a target value and a tolerance/standard deviation. Whether you feed RFD2, RFD3, or Riff-Diff, you will write that table once from your theozyme and adapt its syntax to the tool.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 14.2 · Extracting functional-group coordinates for RFD2"}
**Problem.** From an optimized Kemp-eliminase theozyme, produce the inputs RFdiffusion2 needs to diffuse a backbone around the catalytic base and the substrate.

**Solution.**
1. **Define the ligand.** Write the 5-nitrobenzisoxazole substrate (or, more rigorously, the TS model with the partially broken C3–H bond) as an SDF and generate a parameter file with standard atom names: `C3`, `H3`, `N1`, `O1`, `O_phenolate`, etc.
2. **Choose the motif residues.** The general base = a glutamate (atom `OE2` as the abstracting oxygen). The H-bond donor = a backbone amide or a serine/tyrosine `OH`. (For a minimal first pass, RFD2 can be given the base alone and the rest left to design.)
3. **Build the motif PDB.** Place the Glu side chain so that `OE2` sits at the theozyme-optimized distance from `H3` (2.7 Å) and the `OE2···H3–C3` angle is 165°. Use the *exact* coordinates from the DFT optimization, transformed into one frame shared with the ligand.
4. **Write the contact/constraint spec.** In the RFD2 config, declare the ligand, mark the Glu as catalytic, and specify the `OE2`–`H3` contact and its tolerance (e.g., 2.7 ± 0.3 Å) and the angle (165 ± 15°). RFD2 will atomize this residue and diffuse a backbone presenting it to the ligand.
5. **Sanity-check round-trip.** Reload the written PDB + config and re-measure `OE2`–`H3` and the angle; they must match the DFT values to within rounding. A failed round-trip means an atom-name or frame error — fix it before launching any generation.
:::

## 14.6 Worked Theozyme: the Kemp Eliminase

We now carry the model reaction of §13.5 all the way to an exportable theozyme. The Kemp elimination opens 5-nitrobenzisoxazole: a base removes the C3 proton, the N–O bond breaks, and 2-cyano-4-nitrophenolate forms as negative charge develops on the phenolate oxygen.

![**Figure 14.1** A Kemp-eliminase theozyme: the 5-nitrobenzisoxazole transition state surrounded by the three catalytic functional groups — a carboxylate general base abstracting the C3 proton, an H-bond donor stabilizing the developing phenolate, and an aromatic ring π-stacking against the planar substrate. The labeled distances and angles become the exported constraints.](assets/figures/theozyme_kemp.png)

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 14.1 · A full Kemp-eliminase theozyme"}
**Problem.** Construct and export a complete theozyme for the Kemp elimination of 5-nitrobenzisoxazole, with the minimal three-group catalytic set justified by the transition-state charge distribution.

**Solution.**
1. **Transition state.** Build the QM model as the substrate plus an acetate (truncated carboxylate base) positioned to abstract H3. Run a relaxed scan along the C3–H···O(acetate) coordinate at ωB97X-D/def2-SVP with SMD(water); take the highest point as the TS guess. Optimize to the saddle (`OptTS`, with a computed initial Hessian), then run a frequency calculation — confirm a single imaginary frequency (roughly 1000–1500 cm⁻¹) whose normal mode shows the proton transferring from C3 to acetate while the N–O bond elongates. Run the IRC: downhill one way is the bound substrate + acetate, the other the ring-opened phenolate + acetic acid. Refine with a def2-TZVP single point. This is the validated transition state.
2. **General base.** The acetate carboxylate (a buried Glu/Asp in the protein) abstracts the C3 proton; its `OE2/OD2` sits ≈ 2.7 Å from H3 along a near-linear C3–H···O path. This is the group whose mutation gives the catalytically-dead control of Chapter 13.
3. **H-bond donor.** A hydroxyl (Ser/Tyr/Thr) or a backbone amide stabilizes the developing phenolate oxygen, ≈ 2.8–3.0 Å from `O_phenolate` — the charge the TS develops.
4. **π-stacking aromatic.** A Trp/Phe/Tyr ring stacks against the planar nitrobenzisoxazole, ≈ 3.5–3.8 Å face-to-face with a modest offset, providing binding, desolvation of the base, and electrostatic preorganization.
5. **Optimize the assembly** with the TS core constrained and read off the converged geometry.
6. **Tabulate constraints and export.** base `O`···`H3` = 2.7 ± 0.3 Å, `O`···`H3`–`C3` angle = 165 ± 15°; donor `O/N`···`O_phenolate` = 2.9 ± 0.3 Å; aromatic centroid-to-substrate-plane = 3.6 ± 0.4 Å with a defined offset and ring-plane dihedral. Write these once as a constraint table, then translate into the RFD2/RFD3 motif config and the Riff-Diff rotamer ensemble as in §14.5. Record the DFT method string (*ωB97X-D/def2-TZVP//def2-SVP, SMD(water), ORCA 6.0*), the imaginary frequency, and the IRC endpoints in the design notebook alongside the constraint table.
:::

Two honest caveats close the example. First, the *real* HG-series eliminases placed the base as an aspartate/glutamate in a hydrophobic pocket whose precise electrostatics — not just the three labeled distances — set the activity; this is why QM/MM (§14.3) and the electrostatic filters of Chapter 18 matter, and why directed evolution improved HG3 to HG3.17. Second, a theozyme specifies *geometry*, not *foldability*: it is entirely possible to write a beautiful three-group theozyme that no soluble protein backbone can present, which is exactly why the next chapter generates hundreds of candidate backbones and triages them by motif fidelity.

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 14 · Compute a transition state and build a theozyme"}
Carry your capstone reaction from a TS guess to exported, model-ready constraint files. Run on an HPC cluster or cloud GPU/CPU node with a quantum-chemistry package installed.

1. **Build the QM model** (Method 14.1, steps 1–2): substrate/TS core plus truncated catalytic groups; set charge and multiplicity.
2. **Locate and validate the TS** (Method 14.1, steps 3–7): relaxed scan → `OptTS` → frequency calculation (confirm one imaginary frequency) → animate the mode → IRC connecting reactant and product. Use ωB97X-D/def2-SVP with SMD(water) for optimization; refine with a def2-TZVP single point.
3. **Assemble the theozyme** (§14.4): attach each catalytic group, optimize the assembly with the TS core constrained, and read off the optimized distances/angles.
4. **Tabulate constraints** with tolerances set from the energy-surface curvature (≈ within 1–2 kcal/mol of optimum).
5. **Export** (§14.5, Worked Example 14.2): write the theozyme PDB + ligand parameters, then translate the constraint table into RFD2, RFD3, and Riff-Diff input formats. Round-trip-check every coordinate.
6. **Record** the full method string, the imaginary frequency, and the IRC endpoints in the design notebook.

**Platform:** HPC / cloud (ORCA, Psi4, or Gaussian) · **Deliverable:** a theozyme PDB, RFD2/RFD3/Riff-Diff constraint files, and the DFT log (with the validated frequency and IRC).
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **14.1** A transition state is a first-order saddle point; validate every TS with a frequency calculation (exactly one imaginary frequency), mode animation, and an IRC that connects the intended reactant and product.
- **14.2** Choose and report a defensible method: ωB97X-D or B3LYP-D3(BJ)/M06-2X for geometries and barriers, def2-SVP for optimization and def2-TZVP for energies, with dispersion, RIJCOSX, and an implicit solvent model for charged TSs.
- **14.3** QM/MM embeds a QM reactive core in an MM protein with electrostatic embedding, capturing the preorganized field that distinguishes an enzyme from a beaker; use it to refine and to diagnose.
- **14.4** A theozyme is the validated TS plus the minimal catalytic groups frozen in optimal geometry; from it you extract distance/angle/dihedral constraints with tolerances set by the energy-surface curvature.
- **14.5** Export the constraints once as a `.cst`-style table, then translate into the atomized-motif configs of RFD2/RFD3 and the rotamer ensemble of Riff-Diff; round-trip-check every coordinate.
- **14.6** The Kemp eliminase theozyme — carboxylate base, H-bond donor, π-stacking ring around the nitrobenzisoxazole TS — is the blueprint the rest of Part IV builds on.

2. Argue, with reference to the Reality Check, why investing in QM/MM refinement of a *designed* pocket (rather than only an implicit-solvent theozyme) can change which backbones you advance — and identify the conformational-sampling pitfall that can make a single QM/MM barrier misleading.
3. The HG3 → HG3.17 lineage improved a designed Kemp eliminase by orders of magnitude through directed evolution. Propose which theozyme assumptions the original design most likely got *approximately* right and which it got wrong, and explain how you would test your hypothesis computationally before any wet-lab work.

## Further Reading

- Zanghellini, A., et al. (2006). *New algorithms and an in silico benchmark for computational enzyme design*. **Protein Science** 15, 2785–2794. — The theozyme + RosettaMatch constraint framework that the modern generators inherit.
- Tantillo, D. J., Chen, J., & Houk, K. N. (1998). *Theozymes and compuzymes: theoretical models for biological catalysis*. **Curr. Opin. Chem. Biol.** 2, 743–750. — The origin of the theozyme concept.
- Senn, H. M., & Thiel, W. (2009). *QM/MM methods for biomolecular systems*. **Angew. Chem. Int. Ed.** 48, 1198–1229. — The standard QM/MM review for enzymology.
- Mardirossian, N., & Head-Gordon, M. (2017). *Thirty years of density functional theory in computational chemistry*. **Mol. Phys.** 115, 2315–2372. — A grounded guide to choosing functionals.
- Blomberg, R., et al. (2013). *Precision is essential for efficient catalysis in an evolved Kemp eliminase*. **Nature** 503, 418–421. — Why sub-ångström theozyme geometry matters, shown by the HG3.17 structure.
