Catalysis is geometry, and geometry is hosted by a fold. This chapter surveys the small number of protein architectures that recur across the catalytic universe (3.1–3.2), explains why a few of them are the workhorses of de novo design, then zooms into the active site to separate the residues that do chemistry from those that merely bind or support (3.3–3.4). ## 3.1 Folds That Host Catalysis

A natural enzyme is a marriage of two things: a **catalytic constellation** — a few functional groups held in a precise geometry — and a **scaffold** that does the holding. The scaffold contributes almost nothing chemically; its job is mechanical and electrostatic. It must (i) position the catalytic groups within tenths of an ångström of their ideal placement, (ii) hold that placement rigidly against thermal motion, (iii) build a pocket that excludes water where water would hurt and admits substrate where it helps, and (iv) fold reliably and stably enough to be expressed. Remarkably, evolution accomplishes this with a *small* repertoire of recurring architectures. The classification databases **CATH** and **SCOP** organize the ~200,000 experimental structures of the PDB into on the order of a thousand distinct folds, and a handful of those folds account for a wildly disproportionate share of enzymes.

Four are worth knowing by name because they recur both in nature and in design. The **(β/α)₈ TIM barrel** — eight parallel β-strands forming a central barrel ringed by eight α-helices — is the single most common enzyme fold, hosting an enormous range of chemistries with the active site invariably at the C-terminal mouth of the barrel. The **Rossmann fold** — alternating β-strands and helices forming a twisted parallel sheet — is the canonical dinucleotide-binding architecture, the host for most NAD(P)- and FAD-dependent oxidoreductases. The **α/β-hydrolase fold** is the durable home of the Ser–His–Asp catalytic triad, found in lipases, esterases, and proteases. And the **NTF2-like fold** — a cone-shaped β-sheet curled against helices, forming a deep hydrophobic cavity — is small, stable, and provides a ready-made pocket, which is exactly why it has become a darling of computational design.

The active site is marked on each; note how the TIM barrel and α/β-hydrolase present their chemistry at the C-terminal face of the central sheet.](assets/figures/fold_gallery.png)

The recurrence of these folds is not coincidence. They are *designable* — they tolerate sequence variation, fold robustly, and present geometrically convenient sites — which is also what makes them attractive starting points for engineering. A central theme of Part III is that modern generative models have, in effect, learned the statistics of designable folds and can now produce novel backbones with the same desirable properties, no longer limited to nature's catalogue.

## 3.2 Why Designers Love TIM Barrels and NTF2s

Two of the four folds dominate the de novo literature, for complementary reasons. The **NTF2-like fold** is small (~120 residues), expresses well, is thermostable, and — crucially — comes with a **pre-formed deep cavity** that needs only to be lined with the right residues. The designer is spared the hardest part of pocket-building; the cavity already exists, and the task reduces to decorating its walls. The classic Baker-lab Kemp eliminases and the retro-aldolases were built largely on TIM-barrel and jelly-roll scaffolds, but the NTF2 fold has become a preferred *de novo* host precisely because its hydrophobic interior is so amenable to placing a small catalytic constellation around a buried transition state.

The **TIM barrel** earns its place through versatility and modularity. Its eight βα units are quasi-independent, so the fold tolerates loop grafting and active-site remodeling; its funnel-shaped C-terminal mouth is an ideal substrate-binding geometry; and it has been the testbed for *de novo barrel design* (e.g., the four-fold-symmetric designed barrels of the 2010s), demonstrating that the fold can be built from scratch and then functionalized. The barrel's appeal to designers is the same as its appeal to evolution: a stable, modular chassis that decouples scaffold stability from active-site chemistry.

The strategic point for the modern designer is that the *fold-first* logic of these examples is being supplanted. Where the 2008–2018 generation grafted a theozyme onto a chosen natural or designed scaffold, the 2024–2026 generation can condition backbone *generation* on the catalytic geometry directly (Part III), producing a bespoke scaffold around the active site rather than searching a library of pre-made ones. But the criteria the scaffold must satisfy — rigidity, expressibility, a pocket of the right size and polarity, geometric precision — are unchanged. Knowing what makes a TIM barrel or an NTF2 a good host is knowing what to ask of a generated backbone.

::: {.bench data-title="From the Bench 3.1 · A fold that behaves, and one that fights you"}
On the bench, scaffold choice is not an abstraction — it is the difference between a clean SEC trace and a clogged column. NTF2-based and idealized small-β designs frequently express to tens of mg/L of soluble, monomeric protein in *E. coli* and survive heating to 70–95 °C; they are forgiving. By contrast, large all-β and complex multi-domain scaffolds, and any design with an exposed hydrophobic patch or an uncompensated buried charge, have a habit of expressing into inclusion bodies, eluting in the void volume as aggregates, or precipitating during concentration. A common, demoralizing pattern is a design that scores beautifully *in silico* and never reaches the assay because it will not stay in solution. The lesson the wet lab teaches early: weight expressibility and solubility heavily in your *in-silico* filters (Chapter 18), and prefer small, stable folds for first campaigns. A weakly active enzyme you can purify beats a brilliant one you cannot.
:::

## 3.3 Active-Site Anatomy

Zoom into the working part of any enzyme and you find not an undifferentiated cluster but a layered organization. It is essential — for both analysis and design — to classify each residue by its *role*, because the roles carry different geometric tolerances and different consequences when mutated.

**Catalytic residues** do the chemistry: a general base that abstracts a proton, a nucleophile that attacks a carbonyl, a metal-coordinating ligand. They are few — often two to four — and they are held to **sub-ångström tolerances**, because catalysis depends exponentially on geometry (Chapter 2). The paradigm is the **catalytic triad** of serine hydrolases: a serine nucleophile, a histidine general base that deprotonates it, and an aspartate (or glutamate) that orients and polarizes the histidine. The triad functions only when the three are positioned and hydrogen-bonded within a narrow geometric window.

**Binding (first-shell) residues** contact the substrate directly but do not perform chemistry. They confer specificity and orient the substrate so that the reacting atoms present correctly to the catalytic groups. Their geometric tolerance is looser than the catalytic residues' but still real; they define the *shape* of the pocket. The **oxyanion hole** — typically two backbone amide N–H groups poised to hydrogen-bond a developing negative charge on a carbonyl oxygen — straddles the boundary between binding and catalysis: it is structural, but its function is pure transition-state electrostatic stabilization.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 3.1 · Measuring catalytic geometry in a PDB entry"}
**Problem.** You open the structure of a serine protease (say, chymotrypsin, PDB 4CHA) to verify its catalytic triad. What geometric measurements establish that the Ser–His–Asp triad is intact and competent, and what values should you expect?

**Solution.** Identify the three residues (in chymotrypsin: Ser195, His57, Asp102). Then measure three things:

1. **Ser Oγ ··· His Nε2 distance** — the hydrogen bond by which His deprotonates Ser. Expect ≈ 2.6–3.2 Å (a good H-bond).
2. **His Nδ1 ··· Asp Oδ distance** — the H-bond orienting and polarizing the histidine. Expect ≈ 2.6–3.0 Å.
3. **Hydrogen-bond geometry (angles)** — the donor–H···acceptor angles should be reasonably linear (> ~120°, ideally near 180°); a short distance with a bent angle is a weak bond.

In PyMOL: `dist d1, /4CHA//A/SER`195`/OG, /4CHA//A/HIS`57`/NE2` and analogously for the His–Asp pair, then inspect angles with the wizard or `get_angle`. A competent triad shows two short, near-linear H-bonds linking Asp→His→Ser in series (the "charge-relay" arrangement). Crucially, also locate the **oxyanion hole** — the backbone amides of Gly193 and Ser195 — and confirm they point into the pocket where the tetrahedral-intermediate oxyanion will sit. Distances drifting beyond ~3.5 Å, or badly bent angles, indicate either a non-catalytic conformation, a mutant, or a poorly refined model — all of which a designer must catch before trusting the geometry. The same three-measurement protocol is exactly what you will apply, in reverse, to *check* a design's predicted active site.
:::

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 3.1 · Active-site analysis software (version-stamped)"}
**PyMOL** (Open-Source 3.1 / Incentive 3.x, 2024) — measure distances/angles (`distance`, `get_angle`, `get_dihedral`), the Wizard → Measurement tool, and `polar_contacts` for H-bond detection.  
**ChimeraX** (1.8, 2024) — `distance`, `angle`, `contacts`/`hbonds` commands; superb for clash and H-bond network analysis and publication figures.  
**PyMOL `find_pairs`** or ChimeraX `hbonds` — automated H-bond enumeration in a selection.  
**PDBe / RCSB** structure pages — pre-computed ligand contacts and validation reports; always check the validation report (resolution, Ramachandran, real-space R) before trusting a geometry.  
Report distances to 0.1 Å and state the PDB ID, chain, and resolution.
:::

## 3.4 Second-Shell Residues and Substrate Channels

Behind the residues that touch the substrate sits a layer that touches the *catalytic residues* — the **second shell**. These residues never contact the substrate, yet they are frequently decisive, because their job is to *fix the geometry and tune the electrostatics of the first shell*. A second-shell hydrogen-bond donor that anchors a catalytic histidine's orientation, or a buried residue that sets the pK_a of a catalytic aspartate, can be worth orders of magnitude in rate even though it does no chemistry itself. Directed-evolution campaigns on early de novo enzymes overwhelmingly improved activity through *second-shell* and remote mutations, not by altering the catalytic residues — a humbling demonstration that getting the catalytic constellation right is necessary but far from sufficient. Modern design pipelines therefore explicitly model the second shell rather than only the catalytic groups (Chapters 11–12).

Beyond the immediate environment, an enzyme must manage **transport**: substrate in, product out, and often solvent and protons routed precisely. Many enzymes have **substrate channels** or tunnels connecting a buried active site to the surface, sometimes gated by mobile loops. For a design, channel geometry governs whether substrate can even reach the site and whether product can leave before it inhibits; a beautifully constructed but *inaccessible* active site is a common and frustrating failure. Water management is the corollary: the design must exclude water from regions where it would quench a developing charge or compete with a catalytic H-bond, while admitting the specific ordered waters some mechanisms require. These transport and solvation considerations are why active-site design is never just the active site — it is the active site plus everything that controls access to it.

## 3.5 Conformational States of the Catalytic Cycle

The deepest correction this chapter makes to a naive view is temporal. A crystal structure is a single, time-averaged snapshot, usually of one state. But catalysis is a *cycle* — substrate binding, possibly an induced conformational change, the chemical step (through the transition state), product release — and the enzyme generally adopts **distinct conformational states** along the way. Many enzymes undergo **open↔closed transitions**, in which loops or whole domains clamp down on the bound substrate; the catalytically competent geometry exists only in the closed state, while binding and product release require the open state.

The design consequence is profound and easy to miss: **catalytic geometry must be satisfied in the relevant state, not in whatever state your single model happens to depict.** A backbone that places the catalytic triad perfectly in its ground (apo) conformation but cannot reach the closed, TS-competent geometry is not a catalyst. Conversely, a site that achieves perfect TS complementarity only by locking into a closed state from which product cannot escape will be a stoichiometric trap, not a turnover catalyst. This is why a static "does my theozyme superimpose?" check is insufficient, and why the field increasingly evaluates designs across an *ensemble* — through molecular dynamics, multi-state design, and (in the deep-learning era) by checking that predicted structures of the relevant complexes all support the geometry (Chapters 17–18). Form is not a single shape but a choreography, and function is defined across the whole dance.

## Chapter Summary

- **3.1** A small repertoire of folds (TIM barrel, Rossmann, α/β-hydrolase, NTF2) hosts a disproportionate share of enzymes; CATH/SCOP organize the structural universe and reveal these privileged, designable architectures.
- **3.2** Designers favor NTF2s (small, stable, pre-formed cavity) and TIM barrels (modular, versatile, de-novo-buildable); the scaffold criteria — rigidity, expressibility, right-sized pocket, geometric precision — persist even as backbone generation replaces scaffold libraries.
- **3.3** Active sites are layered: catalytic residues (sub-ångström tolerance) do chemistry, first-shell residues bind and orient, and the oxyanion hole stabilizes TS charge.
- **3.4** Second-shell residues fix first-shell geometry and electrostatics — often the locus of activity-improving mutations — and substrate channels plus water management determine accessibility.
- **3.5** Catalysis is a cycle of conformational states; the TS-competent geometry must be satisfied in the relevant (often closed) state and verified across an ensemble, not in one snapshot.
