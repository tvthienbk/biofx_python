## 27.1 Reporting Standards

A design campaign is a scientific claim, and a claim is only as good as what can be checked. The field has converged — through reviewer pressure more than formal standards — on a reporting checklist, and a paper that omits items from it should be read with suspicion.

The first requirement is an **honest funnel**. State how many designs were *generated*, how many passed in-silico filtering, how many were *experimentally tested*, and how many were *active*, with the **tested hit rate** computed on the number tested — not on the number generated (Chapter 26). A paper that reports "we designed an enzyme" without the denominator has reported an anecdote, not a rate.

The second requirement is the **control suite**. Every catalytic claim needs the controls appropriate to its chemistry: a **no-enzyme blank** and a **buffer/substrate-only** background, an **inactive-mutant** (catalytic residue knocked to alanine) that abolishes activity, and — for metalloenzymes — the metal-dependence, chelation, and stoichiometry controls of Chapter 24. Controls are not a formality; they are the difference between a designed catalyst and a contaminant.

The third requirement is **deposited artifacts**. Reproducibility means a reader can rebuild the work: **coordinates** deposited in the PDB (experimental structures) or as model files, **sequences** of every tested construct, the **code and model weights** (or exact versions and parameters) used to generate and filter designs, and the **raw kinetic data** behind every reported kcat, KM, and kcat/KM. "Available upon request" is not deposition. The discipline mirrors the DBTL notebook of Chapter 1: provenance recorded so the loop — and the reader — can close.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 27.1 · Auditing a paper against the checklist"}
**Problem.** A paper claims a de novo phosphoesterase at kcat/KM = 2 × 10⁴ M⁻¹s⁻¹. It reports: "We generated designs with our pipeline and identified an active enzyme. Kinetics were measured in triplicate. Structures and data are available from the authors." Audit it against the reporting checklist.

**Solution.** Walk the three requirements. **Funnel**: *fail* — no count of designs generated, filtered, tested, or active; no tested hit rate; "an active enzyme" hides the denominator. **Controls**: *fail* — no no-enzyme blank, no inactive-mutant knockout, and for a phosphoesterase (likely metal-dependent) no metal-dependence or chelation control; "triplicate" addresses measurement precision, not the catalytic-attribution controls that distinguish design from contamination. **Deposited artifacts**: *fail* — "available from the authors" is not deposition; no PDB code, no sequences, no code/weights or versions, no raw data. The audit verdict: the central number may be real, but *nothing in the paper lets a reader verify it*. The recommendation is to require the funnel with a tested hit rate, the full control set with the alanine-knockout and metal controls, and deposition of coordinates, sequences, code/weights, and raw kinetics before the claim can be assessed.
:::

## 27.2 Benchmarks and Their Limits

Benchmarks drive the field's sense of progress, and they deserve skeptical reading. A **benchmark** is a standardized task with a metric — sequence recovery for a design method, success rate on a held-out motif-scaffolding set, a self-consistency score between a designed sequence and its re-predicted structure. Benchmarks are indispensable for comparing methods on common ground, but they measure what is *easy to measure*, which is rarely what ultimately matters.

The first limit is **proxy validity**. Many benchmarks score *computational self-consistency* — does the designed sequence fold back to the intended backbone in a predictor — rather than experimental function. A method can top a self-consistency leaderboard and produce enzymes that do not catalyze anything, because folding to a backbone is necessary but nowhere near sufficient for catalysis. **Sequence recovery** (how often a method re-derives the native residue) is similarly seductive and misleading: recovering the native sequence rewards mimicry of evolution, which is the opposite of the de novo goal, and high recovery can coexist with poor function.

The second limit is **leakage and overlap**. If a benchmark's test set overlaps with a model's training data — a homolog in the training set, a near-duplicate structure — reported performance is inflated and does not generalize. A valid benchmark enforces a genuine train/test separation (by sequence identity, by structural similarity, by deposition date), and a paper that does not state how separation was enforced has not earned its numbers.

The third limit is **the metric is not the goal**. Optimizing a model to win a benchmark (Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure) can degrade the property the benchmark was meant to proxy. The only benchmark that cannot be gamed is **experimental function**, which is exactly the one benchmarks usually avoid because it is slow and expensive.

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 27.1"}
- **Benchmark-only claims.** "State-of-the-art sequence recovery / self-consistency" with *no experimental enzyme* — a computational leaderboard win is not a catalyst.
- **Missing or absent controls.** A catalytic claim with no no-enzyme blank and no inactive-mutant knockout; for metals, no chelation or stoichiometry control.
- **Undisclosed train/test separation.** Benchmark results with no statement of how leakage was prevented (sequence/structure identity cutoff or date cutoff).
- **Denominator hiding.** "We designed an enzyme" with no count of how many were tested; a hit rate computed on generated rather than tested designs.
- **Cherry-picked single success.** One spectacular result with the failed designs unreported.
- **"Available upon request."** Structures, sequences, code, weights, or raw data not deposited.
:::

## 27.3 The In-Silico-to-Experiment Gap

The deepest reason to read benchmarks skeptically is the **in-silico-to-experiment gap**: the persistent, large discrepancy between how well a method performs on computational metrics and how often its designs actually work at the bench. A pipeline can produce designs with superb predicted confidence, excellent self-consistency, and ideal modeled geometry, of which only a few percent fold, express, and catalyze (Chapter 1's 1–5% tested hit rate).

The gap exists because the computational metrics do not capture what the cell and the cuvette test. Predictors evaluate static, single-conformation, often apo structures; catalysis requires correct dynamics, the right protonation states, productive substrate binding, and a transition-state-stabilizing geometry maintained through the reaction coordinate — none of which a confidence score guarantees. Expression and solubility, which kill a large fraction of shortlists, are barely modeled at all. The honest consequence is that **a benchmark improvement is a hypothesis about experimental improvement, not a demonstration of it**. The field advances only when the gap is measured — when methods are evaluated by experimental hit rate, not merely by in-silico score — and a reader should treat any purely computational claim of progress as provisional until the wet-lab number arrives.

::: {.conceptcheck data-title="Concept Check 27.1"}
A new design method reports 15% higher self-consistency and 10% higher sequence recovery than the previous best, with no experimental enzymes. State the strongest *honest* claim the authors can make, and the claim they cannot. Which single experiment would convert the former into the latter?
:::

## 27.4 Biosecurity and Dual Use

Powerful design tools are **dual-use**: the same methods that build a benign biocatalyst could, in principle, be misused. A responsible practitioner reasons about this at the level of **governance and norms**, and this section deliberately stays at that level — it offers no operational detail that could provide uplift, and neither should any responsible publication.

Three governance mechanisms structure the field's response. The first is **DNA synthesis screening**: commercial gene-synthesis providers, coordinated through bodies such as the International Gene Synthesis Consortium, screen incoming orders against sequences of concern and screen customers, so that the physical step of turning a design into DNA is a checkpoint. Strengthening and broadening this screening — including against AI-designed sequences that may evade signature-based filters — is an active and important policy frontier. The second is **responsible disclosure**: when a method or result could materially lower the barrier to misuse, the norm (developed in computer security and increasingly adopted in biology) is to weigh what is published, to share sensitive operational detail only through controlled channels, and to coordinate with relevant authorities before release. The third is **institutional oversight**: biosafety committees, dual-use research-of-concern (DURC) review, and funder policies that require risk assessment before and during sensitive work.

The practitioner's obligations follow from these mechanisms rather than from any technical recipe: assess dual-use potential *before* doing the work, not after; structure publications so that legitimate science is communicated without providing a roadmap for harm; use providers that screen; and engage the oversight structures rather than route around them. The goal is not to halt the science — the benefits in medicine, sustainability, and basic understanding are real and large — but to make the misuse path narrower than the benefit path at every checkpoint the practitioner controls.

::: {.ethics data-title="Ethics & Responsibility 27.1 · Dual-use risk assessment"}
Before a sensitive project, a practitioner should answer governance questions, not technical ones. *Could this method or result lower the barrier to causing harm, and for whom?* *What is the marginal uplift over what is already public?* (Methods that add little to existing capability raise different questions than genuine new capability.) *Which checkpoints apply* — synthesis screening, institutional biosafety review, DURC oversight, funder policy — *and are they engaged?* *How should results be communicated* so that the science is reproducible to legitimate researchers without serving as an operational guide? These are decisions to make *with* biosafety committees and disclosure norms, in advance, and to document — exactly as one documents any other part of a responsible campaign. The framing is governance and stewardship; it is explicitly *not* the place for operational detail, and a responsible paper withholds such detail by design.
:::

## 27.5 Cutting Through Hype

De novo enzyme design is a genuinely transformative field, which is precisely why it attracts hype, and a graduate practitioner's job includes calibrated skepticism about both the field's claims and their own. The recurring distortions are predictable. **A binder is not an enzyme**: a designed protein that binds a transition-state analog tightly has demonstrated affinity, not turnover, and turnover requires multi-cycle kinetics (Chapter 22). **A benchmark win is not a catalyst** (Section 27.2). **One success is not a hit rate** (Chapter 26). **A cuvette result is not a product** (Chapter 26's industrial gap). **"AI designed an enzyme" usually means a pipeline generated thousands of candidates and a few worked after expression and screening** — an extraordinary achievement stated honestly, and a misleading one stated as if design alone produced the catalyst.

The antidote is the discipline of this whole book: predefine success, design in controls, report the honest funnel, deposit the artifacts, and distinguish what was *demonstrated* from what was *predicted*. Credibility, as the epigraph insists, is a method — a set of checkable practices — not a tone of confidence. The most valuable skill a reader of this field can carry is the reflex to ask, of any striking claim: *what is the denominator, what are the controls, and what was actually measured at the bench?*

## Chapter Summary

- **27.1** A credible campaign reports an honest funnel with a tested hit rate, the full control suite appropriate to its chemistry, and deposited coordinates, sequences, code/weights, and raw kinetic data — not "available upon request."
- **27.2** Benchmarks are indispensable but limited: self-consistency and sequence recovery are proxies that can be topped without producing a working enzyme; watch for train/test leakage and Goodhart effects, since only experimental function cannot be gamed.
- **27.3** The in-silico-to-experiment gap means a benchmark improvement is a hypothesis about experimental improvement, not a demonstration; progress is real only when measured by wet-lab hit rate.
- **27.4** Dual-use risk is managed by governance — DNA synthesis screening, responsible disclosure, and institutional/DURC oversight — engaged before the work and documented; the practitioner stays at the level of stewardship, not operational detail.
- **27.5** Cutting through hype means distinguishing binder from enzyme, benchmark from catalyst, one success from a hit rate, and cuvette from product — and asking always for the denominator, the controls, and the measured quantity.
