## 26.1 Green Chemistry and Industrial Biocatalysis

The largest near-term market for designed enzymes is **industrial biocatalysis** — replacing harsh chemical steps with enzymes that work in water, at moderate temperature, with exquisite selectivity and no heavy-metal catalysts. The pharmaceutical industry has been the early adopter because chiral selectivity is worth a great deal: an enzyme that makes one enantiomer cleanly removes a separation step and a waste stream. The landmark precedent predates de novo design — the engineered transaminase that manufactures the diabetes drug **sitagliptin** replaced a rhodium-catalyzed high-pressure hydrogenation with a single biocatalytic step, raising yield and eliminating heavy metal, and it stands as the proof that engineered enzymes can win on industrial terms.

The bottleneck here is rarely the initial activity; it is **process robustness**. An industrial enzyme must survive elevated temperature, organic cosolvents, substrate and product inhibition, and weeks of operation, and it must do so at a **total turnover number** high enough to justify its cost. De novo design contributes the *starting activity* on a non-natural substrate that no wild-type enzyme touches; the long tail of stability and turnover engineering (Chapter 25) is what makes it manufacturable. The field-shaping constraint is the gap between *demonstrating* activity and *operating* at ton scale.

## 26.2 Therapeutic Enzymes

Enzymes are drugs in their own right — enzyme-replacement therapies for metabolic disease, **asparaginase** for leukemia (which starves cancer cells of asparagine), and engineered enzymes that degrade circulating toxins. Designed enzymes promise therapeutics for targets evolution never addressed: degrading a disease metabolite, cleaving a specific pathogenic protein, or detoxifying a xenobiotic in vivo.

The bottleneck is **the body**, not the chemistry. A therapeutic enzyme must fold and function at 37 °C in serum, resist proteases, evade or tolerate an immune response (de novo sequences are by definition foreign and may be immunogenic), and clear on the right timescale. Catalytic efficiency that suffices in a cuvette may be irrelevant if the enzyme is cleared in minutes or triggers neutralizing antibodies. The design-specific concern — that a fully novel sequence has no evolutionary track record of immune tolerance — makes **immunogenicity** the defining risk, and it is one the in vitro assay cannot see.

## 26.3 Biosensors

A **biosensor** couples a molecular recognition or catalytic event to a measurable output — fluorescence, an electrochemical current, a color change. Designed binding proteins and enzymes can be built to recognize a specific analyte and report it, and the de novo route allows sensing of molecules for which no natural receptor exists. Catalytic biosensors amplify the signal: one enzyme turns over many substrate molecules per analyte-binding event, converting a weak recognition signal into a strong readout.

The bottleneck is **specificity in a complex matrix**. A sensor that responds cleanly to its analyte in buffer may be swamped by cross-reactivity in blood, soil, or food. Designed recognition can be made exquisitely selective in principle, but proving and maintaining that selectivity against the full chemical background of a real sample is the engineering challenge that determines whether a sensor leaves the bench.

## 26.4 Plastic and Pollutant Degradation

Environmental remediation is a domain where de novo and engineered enzymes have visible public stakes. The exemplar is **PET hydrolase** engineering: the natural enzyme **PETase** from *Ideonella sakaiensis* depolymerizes poly(ethylene terephthalate), and engineering campaigns have produced dramatically improved variants — most famously the **leaf-branch compost cutinase (LCC)** engineered for thermostability and activity, reported to depolymerize a large fraction of PET waste within hours under industrial conditions (Tournier et al., 2020). Related efforts target other recalcitrant polymers and persistent pollutants such as organophosphates and PFAS precursors.

The bottleneck is the **substrate itself**: crystalline, solid, insoluble plastic is a terrible substrate for an enzyme evolved to act on soluble molecules. Activity on amorphous regions does not translate to crystalline ones; the reaction is heterogeneous, surface-limited, and product-inhibited. The field-shaping problem is less "can we cleave the bond" — we can — than "can we cleave it fast enough, on real-world waste, at a cost that competes with making new plastic." De novo design here is most promising for *new* hydrolytic chemistries against polymers (like PFAS) that no natural enzyme attacks at all.

## 26.5 Genuinely Non-Natural Chemistries

The most scientifically ambitious application is catalysis of reactions **biology never invented**. Artificial metalloenzymes and engineered hemeproteins now perform carbene and nitrene transfers — cyclopropanations, C–H aminations, carbon–silicon and carbon–boron bond formations — chemistry with no natural enzymatic precedent, much of it pioneered in the Arnold laboratory by repurposing the iron of heme to carry abiological intermediates. De novo design extends this logic: build a pocket and a cofactor environment around a transition state that evolution had no reason to stabilize.

The bottleneck here is **the transition state itself**. For a reaction with a natural analog, mechanistic intuition guides the theozyme; for a genuinely abiological reaction, the designer must reason from quantum chemistry about a transition state no enzyme has ever seen, with correspondingly higher uncertainty. This is where de novo design has its sharpest comparative advantage — there is no natural enzyme to start from, so directed evolution has no foothold — and its hardest problem, because the target is least understood.

## 26.6 Three Full Case Studies

Reading a campaign in the literature is a skill: behind a clean abstract sit thousands of designs, a steep funnel, and a control suite that either does or does not carry the claim. Here are three traced end to end, with honest accounting.

**Case 1 — The Kemp eliminase lineage (the field's teaching case).** The Kemp elimination — a proton abstraction from a benzisoxazole, with no natural enzyme — became the canonical de novo target. Early Rosetta-designed Kemp eliminases (Röthlisberger et al., 2008) screened a large panel of computational designs to find a handful of active hits with very modest efficiency (kcat/KM on the order of 10²–10³ M⁻¹s⁻¹). The campaigns' lasting lesson came afterward: **directed evolution** (and crowdsourced optimization through the Foldit game) improved the best designs by orders of magnitude, reaching kcat/KM near 10⁵–10⁶ M⁻¹s⁻¹. The honest reading is that the *initial design* hit rate was low and activities weak, and that optimization, not design alone, produced the strong catalysts — the lineage is as much a lesson about the design-plus-evolution pairing as about design.

**Case 2 — A de novo serine hydrolase (2025).** A 2025 campaign designed **serine hydrolases** from scratch around the classic **Ser–His–Asp catalytic triad** with an oxyanion hole, using atom-level generative tools. The instructive features are the funnel and the controls: designs were generated in large numbers, filtered in silico to a tested shortlist, and the active hits were validated with the triad-knockout controls (mutate the catalytic serine; activity must vanish) and covalent-inhibitor labeling that confirmed the serine was the nucleophile. The reported efficiencies were genuine multi-turnover catalysis with a designed triad — a milestone because the serine-hydrolase mechanism is multi-step and demanding, not a single-step proton transfer.

**Case 3 — A de novo Zn metallohydrolase (2025).** The metallohydrolase of Chapter 24 (*Nature*, 2025) is the third case, and the one with the most economical funnel. The campaign tested **fewer than 96 designs per round** over a few rounds and reached **kcat/KM ≈ 5 × 10⁴ M⁻¹s⁻¹**, carrying the claim with the full metalloenzyme control suite — metal dependence, chelation quench, alanine-scrambled site, and metal stoichiometry. The funnel here is the signature of the atom-level era: small test sets, real activity, rigorous controls.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 26.1 · Reconstructing a campaign funnel"}
**Problem.** A paper reports: 12,000 backbones generated, 3,000 passing in-silico filters, 90 genes synthesized and expressed, 40 soluble, 6 with activity above background, and 1 advanced through three rounds of optimization to kcat/KM = 4 × 10⁴ M⁻¹s⁻¹. Build the funnel and compute the meaningful rates.

**Solution.** The funnel tiers are 12,000 → 3,000 → 90 → 40 → 6 → 1. The **in-silico filter rate** is 3,000/12,000 = 25%. The **tested hit rate** — the number a reviewer cares about — is 6 active / 90 tested = **6.7%**, comfortably in the Chapter 1 1–5% band (slightly above, consistent with strong filtering). The **soluble-expression rate** is 40/90 = 44%, a reminder that expression, not design, kills nearly half the shortlist. The optimization carried the single best hit from its initial (weak) activity to 4 × 10⁴ M⁻¹s⁻¹ over three rounds — within the 10–1000× band of Chapter 25. The number *not* to quote is "1 success out of 12,000," which conflates generation with testing; the honest headline rates are the **6.7% tested hit rate** and the **per-round optimization fold-gain**.
:::

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 26.1 · Lab success versus industrial viability"}
A characterized kcat/KM in a paper is the *start* of the road to use, not the end. Industrial viability adds requirements the academic assay never tests: stability over weeks at process temperature, tolerance of organic cosolvents and high substrate loading, resistance to product inhibition, a total turnover number high enough to amortize enzyme cost, and a host that expresses the protein at gram-per-liter titers. Most published de novo enzymes are years and many engineering rounds away from any of these. The gap between "we report a designed catalyst" and "this enzyme runs a process" is the single most overclaimed step in the field — and it is almost never the chemistry that fails, but the operating envelope.
:::

::: {.bench data-title="From the Bench 26.1 · Scale-up realities"}
A process chemist's checklist looks nothing like a designer's. *Titer*: a beautiful catalyst that expresses at 5 mg/L is a non-starter; manufacturing wants grams per liter, which usually means re-engineering the construct for the production host (often a *Pichia* or *Bacillus*, not the *E. coli* of the screen). *Cost*: enzyme cost per kilogram of product must be a small fraction of product value, set by titer and total turnover number. *Stability*: an enzyme that loses half its activity in a day forces continuous re-dosing; process wants a half-life in weeks at operating temperature. *Downstream*: the enzyme must not contaminate the product or complicate purification. None of these appear in a kcat/KM measurement, and all of them decide whether a design becomes a product. The lesson for designers: if industrial use is the goal, design for the operating envelope from round one, not as an afterthought.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **26.1** Industrial biocatalysis (e.g., the sitagliptin transaminase) is the largest near-term market; the bottleneck is process robustness — stability, turnover, and operation — not initial activity.
- **26.2** Therapeutic enzymes face the body as the bottleneck: 37 °C serum stability, protease resistance, clearance, and especially immunogenicity, which the in vitro assay cannot see.
- **26.3** Biosensors amplify recognition into signal; the bottleneck is specificity in a complex matrix, not selectivity in buffer.
- **26.4** PET/LCC engineering shows degradation works, but the bottleneck is the solid crystalline substrate and the cost of competing with virgin plastic; de novo design is most promising for polymers with no natural enzyme (e.g., PFAS).
- **26.5** Genuinely non-natural chemistries (carbene/nitrene transfer, C–Si bonds) are de novo design's sharpest advantage and hardest problem; the bottleneck is reasoning about an abiological transition state.
- **26.6** Three traced campaigns — Kemp eliminase (design + evolution), de novo serine hydrolase (2025), Zn metallohydrolase (2025) — illustrate honest funnels, with the tested hit rate and the control suite carrying the claim, not the generation count.

**Quantitative & Computational.**

1. A paper reports 8,000 backbones → 1,600 filtered → 80 tested → 5 active → 1 optimized to 3 × 10⁴ M⁻¹s⁻¹. Compute the filter rate, the tested hit rate, and place the hit rate in the Chapter 1 band.
2. An enzyme costs $2,000/kg to produce and achieves a total turnover number of 10⁵ for a product worth $50/kg. Estimate whether the enzyme cost is plausibly a small fraction of product value, and state the assumption you must make.
3. A therapeutic enzyme has kcat/KM = 10⁶ M⁻¹s⁻¹ but a serum half-life of 8 minutes. Qualitatively, why might its in vivo efficacy still be poor, and what engineering target would you prioritize?

**Challenge.**

1. Choose one of the five application domains and write the success criteria a campaign in that domain must meet *beyond* a reported kcat/KM. Justify each criterion against the domain's bottleneck.
