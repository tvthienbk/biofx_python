## 4.1 Acid–Base Catalysis

The most universal catalytic strategy is the transfer of a proton at the right moment to the right place. In **general acid–base catalysis**, a side chain donates a proton to (general acid) or abstracts a proton from (general base) the substrate during the rate-limiting step, stabilizing developing charge in the transition state. This is distinct from *specific* acid–base catalysis, in which the catalyst is bulk H⁺ or OH⁻; enzymes overwhelmingly use the *general* form because it lets them deliver the proton from a precisely positioned group rather than relying on solvent.

The chemistry the designer must respect is the **pK_a**. A group can act as a general base only if it is at least partly deprotonated at the operating pH, and as a general acid only if at least partly protonated; maximal buffering capacity occurs when pK_a ≈ pH. **Histidine** is the workhorse of acid–base catalysis precisely because its imidazole pK_a (≈ 6.0 free in solution, tunable by environment) sits near physiological pH, so it can act as *either* acid or base within one cycle. **Aspartate and glutamate** (carboxylate pK_a ≈ 3.5–4.5, raisable to 7+ when buried) serve as bases or, when protonated, as acids. **Lysine** (ε-amino pK_a ≈ 10.5, lowerable in a hydrophobic site) and **tyrosine** (phenol pK_a ≈ 10) and **cysteine** (thiol pK_a ≈ 8.3) round out the menu. The single most important design insight is that the active-site environment *shifts* these pK_a values — sometimes by several units — through desolvation and electrostatic interactions, so the *effective* pK_a in the design, not the textbook solution value, is what governs function. Getting a buried catalytic carboxylate's pK_a right is a recurring make-or-break detail.

## 4.2 Covalent (Nucleophilic) Catalysis

In **covalent catalysis**, the enzyme forms a transient covalent bond to the substrate, routing the reaction through a covalent intermediate that splits the high single barrier into two lower ones. The classic case is the **serine hydrolases** (Chapter 3): the Ser–His–Asp triad activates the serine Oγ into a potent nucleophile (His deprotonates it; Asp orients and polarizes His), which attacks the substrate carbonyl to form a covalent **acyl-enzyme intermediate** through a tetrahedral transition state whose oxyanion is stabilized by the oxyanion hole. A water molecule, activated by the same histidine, then hydrolyzes the acyl-enzyme to release product and regenerate the enzyme.

The recurring nucleophiles are **serine** (as the activated alkoxide), **cysteine** (the thiolate is an even better nucleophile and needs less activation — the basis of cysteine proteases), and **lysine** (whose ε-amine attacks carbonyls to form **Schiff bases / imines**, the mechanism of class I aldolases and the retro-aldolases discussed in Chapter 5). Covalent catalysis is attractive for design because its requirements are well-defined and largely electrostatic: a positioned nucleophile, a base to activate it, and stabilization of the developing oxyanion or iminium charge. It is, alongside acid–base catalysis, the most tractable mechanism for de novo work.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 4.1 · Deriving a catalytic set for ester hydrolysis"}
**Problem.** You are asked to design an esterase that hydrolyzes a simple carboxylic ester R–CO–OR′. Working only from the mechanism, derive the minimal catalytic set.

**Solution.** Write the mechanism and read off the requirement at each step.

1. *Activate a nucleophile.* The reaction needs an attacking nucleophile on the carbonyl carbon. Choose **Ser Oγ**. A free serine is a poor nucleophile, so it must be activated by deprotonation → we need a **general base**.
2. *Provide the base.* A **His** imidazole (pK_a near neutral) abstracts the serine proton. To make the histidine a strong, correctly oriented base, polarize it with **Asp/Glu** → the **catalytic triad** (Ser–His–Asp).
3. *Stabilize the tetrahedral oxyanion.* Nucleophilic attack creates a tetrahedral intermediate with a developing negative charge on the former carbonyl oxygen. Stabilize it with an **oxyanion hole** — two H-bond donors (typically backbone amide N–H groups) poised at that oxygen.
4. *Collapse and acylate.* The tetrahedral intermediate collapses, expelling the alcohol R′OH (His now acts as a general *acid*, protonating the leaving group), forming the acyl-enzyme.
5. *Deacylate.* A water molecule, deprotonated by the same His, attacks the acyl-enzyme through a second tetrahedral TS (again oxyanion-hole-stabilized), releasing the acid and regenerating free enzyme.

**Minimal catalytic set:** Ser (nucleophile) + His (general base/acid) + Asp or Glu (orient/polarize His) + an oxyanion hole (two H-bond donors). Required geometry: Ser Oγ–His Nε2 ≈ 2.6–3.2 Å H-bond; His Nδ1–Asp Oδ ≈ 2.6–3.0 Å H-bond; Ser Oγ positioned ~3 Å from the substrate carbonyl carbon along the Bürgi–Dunitz attack trajectory (~105°); two oxyanion-hole donors ≤ ~3.2 Å from the carbonyl oxygen. This four-element set, plus its geometry, *is* the theozyme you would build in Chapter 11.
:::

## 4.3 Metal-Ion Catalysis

Roughly a third of enzymes use a **metal ion**, because metals do things amino-acid side chains cannot. Their catalytic roles fall into a few categories. As **Lewis acids**, metals (Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺, Mn²⁺) polarize a bound substrate or lower the pK_a of a coordinated water, generating a hydroxide nucleophile at neutral pH — the strategy of carbonic anhydrase, whose active-site Zn²⁺ (coordinated by three histidines) produces a zinc-bound hydroxide that attacks CO₂. Metals also provide **electrostatic stabilization** of developing negative charge in the TS, **bridge** two substrates or a substrate and a nucleophile, and — for the redox-active metals (Fe, Cu, Mn, Mo, Ni)— mediate **electron transfer** and **oxygen activation** that no side chain can perform.

The design currency of metalloenzymes is the **coordination sphere**: which residues ligate the metal, the coordination number and geometry (tetrahedral, square-planar, octahedral), and the identity of the metal. The common protein ligands are **His** (Nδ1 or Nε2), **Asp/Glu** (carboxylate), and **Cys** (thiolate), with the geometry dictated by the metal's preferences (e.g., Zn²⁺ favors tetrahedral coordination; an octahedral metal leaves coordination positions for substrate and water). Designing a metal site means placing the correct number of ligands at the correct distances (typically ~2.0–2.3 Å metal–ligand) and angles to satisfy the metal's preferred geometry while leaving an open coordination position for catalysis. Metal sites are moderately tractable for design — the geometry is well-characterized — but metal *selectivity* (ensuring the design binds the intended metal, not a tighter-binding adventitious one) and avoiding mismetalation are real, often underappreciated, difficulties.

## 4.4 Cofactor-Dependent Chemistry

Some chemistry is simply beyond the reach of the twenty amino acids, and for it nature recruits **cofactors** — small organic molecules (coenzymes) that act as the enzyme's chemical reagents while the protein supplies positioning, specificity, and rate. Four families dominate.

**Pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP)**, the vitamin-B6 cofactor, is the master of amino-acid chemistry — transaminations, decarboxylations, racemizations, β/γ-eliminations. It works by forming a Schiff base with the substrate amino group and acting as an **electron sink**, stabilizing the carbanionic intermediate that forms when a bond to the α-carbon breaks. **Flavins (FMN/FAD)** are the redox Swiss-army knives: they can accept or donate one or two electrons, enabling hydride transfers, oxygen activation, and one-electron radical chemistry — versatility that comes from the flavin's accessible two-electron *and* one-electron states. **Heme** (iron protoporphyrin IX) carries oxygen, transfers electrons, and — in the cytochrome P450s — performs the spectacular insertion of oxygen into unactivated C–H bonds via a high-valent iron-oxo (Compound I) species. **NAD(P)⁺/NAD(P)H** are the diffusible hydride carriers of central metabolism, shuttling two electrons as a hydride between substrates and the cell's redox pools, hosted overwhelmingly on Rossmann folds.

For the designer, cofactors are a double-edged gift. They *extend* the accessible chemistry enormously — install a flavin and oxidative chemistry becomes thinkable — but they *raise* the design burden: the protein must now bind the cofactor in the correct orientation, position both cofactor and substrate, tune the cofactor's reactivity (e.g., the flavin redox potential) through the surrounding electrostatics, and often manage a second substrate and the timing of two half-reactions. Cofactor-dependent de novo design is an active frontier; binding and orienting a cofactor de novo has been demonstrated, but achieving efficient, well-tuned cofactor *catalysis* from scratch remains hard.

## 4.5 Pericyclic and Radical Reactions

Two categories sit at the difficult edge of the mechanistic map. **Pericyclic reactions** — Diels–Alder cycloadditions, Claisen rearrangements, and the like — proceed through a single concerted, cyclic transition state with no charged intermediate. Because there is little charge to stabilize electrostatically, the enzyme's leverage comes mainly from *proximity, orientation, and conformational restriction* (clamping the reactants into the reactive geometry) plus modest TS electronic stabilization. Diels–Alderases and chorismate mutase (a natural Claisen catalyst) are the canonical examples, and a de novo Diels–Alderase was one of the Rosetta era's signal achievements (Chapter 5) — though its rate enhancement was modest, precisely because there is little electrostatic handle to pull on.

**Radical reactions** are harder still. Enzymes such as the radical SAM superfamily and ribonucleotide reductase generate and control highly reactive carbon- or sulfur-centered radicals, performing chemistry (C–H abstraction from unactivated positions, complex rearrangements) that nothing else can do. But radicals are promiscuous and destructive; the enzyme must generate the radical at exactly the right moment, channel it along a precise trajectory, and quench it before it damages the protein. This demands exquisite control of geometry and electron flow that we do not yet know how to specify de novo. Radical chemistry, multi-state photochemistry, and the more elaborate cofactor cycles are the genuine frontier — instructive to attempt, but not where a first campaign should aim.

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 4.1 · Tractability by mechanism"}
Not all chemistries are equally designable, and pretending otherwise wastes campaigns. A rough, honest ranking as of 2026:

- **Tractable now:** acid–base and covalent catalysis (hydrolases, eliminases, aldol/retro-aldol, isomerases). Well-defined, largely electrostatic requirements; the great majority of de novo successes live here.
- **Moderately tractable:** simple metal-ion catalysis (Lewis-acid activation, e.g., a Zn-hydroxide), and cofactor *binding* and *orientation*. Geometry is known; selectivity and tuning are the obstacles.
- **Frontier:** efficient cofactor *catalysis* from scratch (well-tuned flavin/heme/PLP turnover), redox chemistry, oxygen activation.
- **Hard frontier:** controlled radical chemistry, multi-state and photochemical reactions, anything requiring exquisite electron-flow choreography.

The practical guidance: classify your target's mechanism first, and if it falls below "moderately tractable," expect to lean heavily on directed evolution after design — or choose a different first target.
:::

## 4.6 Mapping Mechanism → Minimal Catalytic Set

Everything in this chapter exists to support one procedure, which recurs throughout Part IV. Given a target reaction, the designer derives a **minimal catalytic set** — the smallest constellation of functional groups, in defined geometry, that the mechanism requires — by reasoning step-by-step through the mechanism exactly as in Worked Example 4.1. The recipe:

1. **Write the mechanism**, identifying every elementary step and, critically, the rate-limiting transition state(s) and the charge/geometry developing there.
2. **For each developing charge, assign a stabilizer** — a general acid/base for a proton in flight, an oxyanion hole for a developing anion, a metal or positive residue for a developing negative charge, a cofactor for chemistry side chains cannot reach.
3. **For each bond made or broken, assign the agent** — the nucleophile, the base that activates it, the electrophile or electron sink.
4. **Add the supporting cast** — the residues that orient and polarize the catalytic groups (the Asp behind the His), and the binding residues that present the substrate correctly.
5. **Specify the geometry** — distances, angles, and trajectories for every interaction, to the tolerances Chapter 3 demands.

The output — a labeled set of functional groups plus their target geometry — *is* the specification a theozyme captures and a generative model is conditioned on. The discipline of this section is to add nothing the mechanism does not require (Occam's razor for active sites) and to omit nothing it does. A catalytic set that is too sparse will not work; one cluttered with groups that address no real charge wastes design freedom and invites the failure modes below.

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 4.1 · Assuming a residue is catalytic"}
- Labeling a conserved residue "catalytic" from sequence conservation alone. Conservation can reflect folding, binding, or structural roles. Demand mechanistic evidence: a mutation that kills *k_cat* (not just K_M), pH–rate dependence, or direct structural placement at the developing charge.
- Counting a residue that contacts the substrate as catalytic. Contact ≠ chemistry; first-shell binding residues orient but do not transform.
- Including a metal or cofactor a reaction's mechanism does not call for, then attributing activity to it.
- Asserting an oxyanion hole is "present" without checking that its donors point at the *developing* charge in the TS geometry, not at the ground-state position.
- Proposing a catalytic set whose required pK_a values are incompatible with the operating pH or with the residues' buried environment.
:::

::: {.studio data-title="Studio Exercise 4 · Mechanism to catalytic set"}
For an assigned reaction (e.g., a Kemp elimination, a retro-aldol cleavage, a phosphate-ester hydrolysis, or a simple transamination), produce (1) a complete arrow-pushing mechanism diagram marking every transition state and its developing charges, and (2) a minimal-catalytic-set table listing each functional group, its mechanistic role (nucleophile / general base / general acid / oxyanion stabilizer / metal ligand / cofactor), the partial charge or bond it addresses, the proposed residue or cofactor, and the target geometry (distance/angle). Conclude with one sentence placing the reaction on the Reality Check 4.1 tractability ladder.

## Chapter Summary

- **4.1** General acid–base catalysis transfers protons from positioned side chains (His, Asp/Glu, Lys, Tyr, Cys); the *effective*, environment-shifted pK_a — not the solution value — governs function.
- **4.2** Covalent catalysis routes the reaction through a covalent intermediate via a positioned nucleophile (Ser, Cys, Lys), an activating base, and oxyanion/iminium stabilization; alongside acid–base, the most tractable mechanism.
- **4.3** Metals act as Lewis acids, electrostatic stabilizers, bridges, and redox/oxygen-activation centers; the design currency is the coordination sphere (ligand identity, number, geometry), with metal selectivity the key difficulty.
- **4.4** Cofactors (PLP, flavin, heme, NAD(P)) extend chemistry beyond the side chains but raise the design burden of binding, orienting, and tuning the cofactor; efficient de novo cofactor catalysis is a frontier.
- **4.5** Pericyclic reactions rely on proximity/orientation with little electrostatic handle (hence modest rate enhancements); radical and multi-state chemistry demand electron-flow control we cannot yet specify de novo.
- **4.6** The core procedure: write the mechanism, stabilize every developing charge, assign every bond-making agent, add the supporting cast, and specify geometry — yielding the minimal catalytic set that becomes the theozyme.

## Problem Set

**Review.**

1. Distinguish general from specific acid–base catalysis, and explain why enzymes prefer the general form.
2. Why is histidine the most versatile acid–base catalyst? Give its approximate imidazole pK_a.
3. Explain how the Ser–His–Asp triad activates serine, naming the role of each residue.
4. What is an acyl-enzyme intermediate, and why does forming it accelerate hydrolysis?
5. List four catalytic roles a metal ion can play, with an example of each.
6. Name the four major cofactor families and one reaction class each enables.
7. Why are pericyclic reactions hard to accelerate strongly with an enzyme?
8. State two pieces of evidence that establish a residue is genuinely catalytic.

**Quantitative & Computational.**

1. A catalytic carboxylate must act as a general base at pH 7.0. If its effective pK_a in the site is 6.0, what fraction is deprotonated and thus competent? What if burial raises its pK_a to 8.0?
2. For the ester-hydrolysis catalytic set of Worked Example 4.1, tabulate every catalytic group, its role, and its target geometry (distance/angle); identify which interaction has the tightest geometric tolerance and why.
3. A designed Zn²⁺ site uses three His ligands and leaves one tetrahedral position for a hydroxide nucleophile. Sketch the coordination geometry, give the expected Zn–N distances, and state the bond angles a tetrahedral site requires.
4. Using the Reality Check 4.1 ladder, classify five reactions of your choosing by tractability and justify each placement in one sentence.

**Challenge.**

1. Propose a minimal catalytic set for a reaction *not* worked in this chapter (e.g., a phosphotriesterase, an epoxide hydrolase, or a decarboxylase), deriving it from the mechanism. State every developing charge and its stabilizer, the required pK_a values, and the full target geometry, then assess its tractability.
2. Cofactor catalysis is rated a frontier. Choose one cofactor (PLP, flavin, or heme) and argue concretely what *additional* design requirements it imposes beyond a cofactor-free active site, and propose how a 2026-era pipeline might address each (binding, orientation, electrostatic tuning, two-substrate timing).
