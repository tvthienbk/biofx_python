Most of the chemistry biology cannot do with twenty amino acids alone, it does with a metal or a cofactor. This chapter shows how to specify a metal site — geometry, ligand set, the open coordination position where chemistry happens — and how atom-level generative tools place those atoms. It closes with the validation traps unique to cofactor enzymes and a 2025 designed Zn metallohydrolase that reached useful catalytic efficiency in a handful of rounds.

## 24.1 Designing Around Metals

The twenty canonical amino acids are a chemically narrow palette. Their side chains offer acids, bases, nucleophiles, and hydrogen-bond donors, but they cannot easily perform one-electron chemistry, bind and activate O₂, or polarize a water molecule strongly enough to attack an unactivated carbonyl. Roughly a third of all natural enzymes solve this by recruiting a **metal ion** or an organic **cofactor**. For the de novo designer, this is both an opportunity and a discipline: a metal vastly expands the reachable chemistry, but it also adds a hard geometric constraint that the protein must satisfy to within tenths of an ångström.

Consider the **zinc metallohydrolases** — carbonic anhydrase, carboxypeptidase, the metallo-β-lactamases. Their shared logic is simple and worth memorizing because it recurs in design. A single Zn²⁺ ion is held by three protein side chains in an approximately **tetrahedral** arrangement, leaving a fourth coordination position open. That open site binds a **water molecule** and lowers its pKₐ dramatically — bulk water has a pKₐ of 15.7, but Zn-bound water can have a pKₐ near 7, so at physiological pH a substantial fraction exists as a **zinc-bound hydroxide**. That hydroxide is a potent, pre-positioned nucleophile. The metal does three jobs at once: it generates the nucleophile, it orients it, and (often) it polarizes the substrate's electrophilic center through a second coordination interaction.

The canonical zinc ligand set in carbonic anhydrase is **three histidines**; in carboxypeptidase it is **two histidines and a glutamate**; many designed sites use **His/His/Asp**, trading one neutral imidazole for a carboxylate to tune charge and Lewis acidity. The geometry is the load-bearing specification. Zn²⁺ has no ligand-field stabilization preference (d¹⁰), so it tolerates coordination numbers of four, five, and six, but a *catalytic* zinc almost always wants a four-coordinate, open tetrahedral site with metal–ligand distances near **2.0–2.1 Å** (Zn–N to histidine Nδ1/Nε2 ≈ 2.0 Å; Zn–O to a carboxylate ≈ 2.0–2.2 Å). Get the distances and angles right and the metal binds with the geometry that does chemistry; get them wrong and you build a structural metal site that holds the fold together but turns over nothing.

![**Figure 24.1** Tetrahedral Zn²⁺ coordination in a designed metallohydrolase: three protein ligands (His/His/Asp) at ≈ 2.1 Å fix the metal, leaving the fourth position open for a catalytic water that the metal deprotonates to a nucleophilic hydroxide.](assets/figures/metal_coordination.png)

The distinction between a **catalytic metal** and a **structural metal** is the first thing to fix in any design. Structural zinc sites — the classic zinc finger, with four cysteines or two cysteines and two histidines, fully saturating the coordination sphere — exist to fold the protein, not to react. They are *closed*: no open position, no bound water, no chemistry. A designer who wants catalysis must deliberately leave one coordination position vacant and aimed at where the substrate will sit. This "open site facing the pocket" requirement is the single most common thing first-time metal designs get wrong.

A student designs a Zn site with four histidine ligands and reports that the metal binds tightly by ITC but the enzyme shows no hydrolytic activity. Give the most likely structural explanation, and name the one change to the ligand set you would try first.
:::

## 24.2 Heme, PLP, Flavin, and NAD(P)

Organic cofactors extend the palette further, and each imposes its own design grammar. Four families dominate practice.

**Heme** — an iron-protoporphyrin IX — is the workhorse of oxidative chemistry: peroxidases, cytochromes P450, globins. The iron sits in the porphyrin plane with four equatorial nitrogen ligands fixed by the cofactor itself, so the protein need only supply the **axial ligands**. A single **proximal ligand** (a histidine in peroxidases and globins, a deprotonated **cysteine thiolate** in P450s) tunes the iron's redox potential and reactivity; the **distal pocket** is left open for O₂ or peroxide and is shaped to position the substrate and stabilize high-valent intermediates such as Compound I. Designing a heme protein therefore reduces largely to building a hydrophobic cavity of the right size with one well-placed proximal ligand — which is why heme proteins were among the earliest cofactor enzymes successfully redesigned and why artificial heme enzymes now catalyze abiological carbene and nitrene transfers.

**Pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP)** is the universal cofactor of amine chemistry — transaminations, decarboxylations, racemizations, β-eliminations. Its mechanistic engine is the **electron sink**: PLP forms a Schiff base (an **internal aldimine**) with an active-site lysine, the substrate amine then transaldiminates to form an **external aldimine**, and the protonated pyridinium ring stabilizes the carbanionic intermediate that forms when a bond to the α-carbon breaks. The design grammar here is about controlling *which* bond breaks (Dunathan's hypothesis: the bond aligned perpendicular to the ring plane is the one that is labilized), so the protein must orient the external aldimine precisely. A reactive lysine and a phosphate-binding cup are the anchoring features.

**Flavins (FMN, FAD)** are the redox Swiss-army knives, capable of both one- and two-electron transfers, which makes them ideal for coupling the obligate two-electron chemistry of organic substrates to the one-electron world of metals and O₂. The isoalloxazine ring is the business end; the protein modulates its redox potential over a remarkable ~0.5 V range simply by hydrogen bonding to N5 and N1/O2 and by the polarity of the surrounding pocket. **NAD(P)⁺/NAD(P)H** are diffusible hydride carriers for dehydrogenases; here the design challenge is a Rossmann-like binding cleft that presents the nicotinamide C4 face to the substrate with the correct *pro-R*/*pro-S* stereochemistry of hydride transfer.

The common thread is that each cofactor brings its own reactive geometry, and the protein's job is **orientation and electrostatic tuning**, not the invention of reactivity from scratch. This is good news for design: the hard chemistry is delegated to a well-understood prosthetic group, and the model need only build a pocket that holds it correctly and presents the substrate to the right face.

## 24.3 LigandMPNN and RFD3 for Coordination Geometry

The generative pipeline of Part III assumed a protein-only world. Metal and cofactor design demand tools that reason about **non-protein atoms** explicitly — the metal ion, the porphyrin, the phosphate of PLP — because those atoms participate directly in the geometry being designed.

The key sequence-design advance is **LigandMPNN** (Dauparas et al., 2023), a successor to ProteinMPNN that conditions the designed sequence on the atomic context of bound small molecules, metals, and nucleotides. Where ProteinMPNN sees only the protein backbone, LigandMPNN sees the ligand's atoms and their chemistry, so when a Zn²⁺ sits 2.1 Å from a backbone position it preferentially assigns a coordinating residue (His, Asp, Glu, Cys) with the side-chain rotamer pointed at the metal. In benchmarks it recovers native metal-coordinating residues far better than ligand-blind methods and produces sequences whose predicted side chains actually point at the cofactor.

On the backbone side, **RFdiffusion** and its all-atom successor **RFdiffusion3 (RFD3)** can generate or scaffold backbones around a fixed heteroatom motif. The practical pattern is **motif scaffolding**: fix the metal ion and its intended ligand atoms (or the entire cofactor) in space as the catalytic motif, then diffuse a backbone that places residues to satisfy that geometry. The atom-level successor architectures handle the ligand natively rather than as an afterthought, which is what makes coordination-geometry design tractable at the few-tens-of-designs scale rather than the thousands-of-designs scale of the Rosetta era.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 24.1 · Atom-level tools for metals and cofactors"}
**LigandMPNN** (2023; `ligandmpnn` repo) — sequence design conditioned on ligands, metals, and nucleotides; the default choice for any active site containing a heteroatom. Use the metal-aware checkpoint and pass the ion in the input PDB.
**RFdiffusion** (2023) — backbone generation and motif scaffolding; supports fixed external atoms via the `ppi`/`scaffoldguided` and contig syntax.
**RFdiffusionAllAtom / RFD3** (2024–2025) — all-atom diffusion that models the ligand and metal explicitly during backbone generation; preferred when the cofactor geometry must be honored throughout, not just at the sequence step.
**Chai-1 / AlphaFold3** (2024) — folding-with-ligand prediction for the *holo* validation step; can place metals and cofactors and report whether the predicted complex retains the designed coordination.
Always re-predict the **holo** complex (protein + metal/cofactor), never the apo protein alone.
:::

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 24.1 · Specifying a catalytic Zn site"}
**Problem.** You want a tetrahedral catalytic Zn²⁺ site for ester hydrolysis. Write the geometric specification you would hand to a motif-scaffolding run, and state where the substrate goes.

**Solution.** Place a single Zn²⁺ ion at the origin of the motif. Define **three protein ligands**: two histidines coordinating through ring nitrogens (Zn–Nε2 ≈ 2.0 Å) and one aspartate coordinating monodentate through a carboxylate oxygen (Zn–Oδ ≈ 2.0 Å). Arrange the three Zn–ligand vectors at tetrahedral angles (≈ 109.5°) so that the **fourth tetrahedral vertex is empty**. That open position is reserved for the catalytic water; specify it explicitly as a coordinated water at Zn–O ≈ 2.0 Å so the metal can deprotonate it to hydroxide near physiological pH. Orient the open vertex so that the substrate's scissile carbonyl carbon will lie ≈ 3 Å beyond the hydroxide oxygen, in line for nucleophilic attack, and add an oxyanion-stabilizing hydrogen-bond donor (a backbone amide or an Asn/Gln) positioned to contact the developing tetrahedral oxyanion. The substrate occupies the pocket built around that fourth coordination position — it is the metal's open face that defines the reaction trajectory.
:::

::: {.method data-title="Method 24.1 · A cofactor-aware design run"}
1. **Fix the catalytic motif.** Assemble the metal (or cofactor) and its intended ligand atoms in a single PDB file with correct distances and angles (Worked Example 24.1). This is the theozyme.
2. **Scaffold the backbone.** Run RFD3 / RFdiffusionAllAtom with the motif held fixed, generating several hundred backbones that place residues to satisfy the coordination geometry. Use contigs to pin the ligand residues.
3. **Design sequence with LigandMPNN.** For each backbone, run the metal/ligand-aware checkpoint so coordinating positions are assigned chemically appropriate residues pointed at the heteroatom. Generate multiple sequences per backbone.
4. **Filter on holo prediction.** Re-fold each design *with the metal/cofactor present* (AlphaFold3 / Chai-1). Keep designs where the predicted complex retains the coordination geometry (ligand atoms within ~0.5 Å of target, open site preserved) and pLDDT/PAE are confident.
5. **Score the active site.** Check metal–ligand distances, the open coordination position, the substrate trajectory, and the oxyanion contact in the predicted holo model. Reject closed or distorted sites.
6. **Shortlist and order.** Select a manageable test set (often < 96 sequences), maximizing geometric quality and sequence diversity.
:::

## 24.4 Special Validation Concerns

Metallo- and cofactor enzymes fail validation in ways protein-only designs do not, and the controls must be designed in from the start. The dominant hazard is **getting fooled by the metal rather than the design**.

First, **metal occupancy is not guaranteed**. A design that binds metal by ITC or by a colorimetric indicator (PAR, mag-fura-2) has cleared only the first gate; the metal may bind in a non-catalytic geometry or at an adventitious surface site. The decisive evidence is a **holo structure** (X-ray or cryo-EM) showing the metal in the designed coordination sphere, supported by **anomalous scattering** to confirm the metal's identity and position, or by EXAFS to measure ligand distances and coordination number directly.

Second, **metal mis-incorporation** is endemic. Zn²⁺, Co²⁺, Ni²⁺, Cu²⁺, and Fe²⁺ share similar ionic radii and coordination preferences, and an *E. coli* expression host offers all of them. A site designed for Zn²⁺ may load the wrong metal, and the wrong metal may be more or less active. Always **strip the metal** (with a chelator such as EDTA), confirm the **apo** form is inactive, and **reconstitute** with a defined metal at defined stoichiometry — then measure activity as a function of which metal is added. Report metal content by ICP-MS, not by inference.

Third — and most insidious — **adventitious metal can produce apparent activity with no designed site at all**. Trace transition metals in buffers, in glassware, or free in solution hydrolyze activated esters and phosphoesters at measurable rates. A "designed metallohydrolase" can be nothing but contaminating Zn²⁺ catalyzing the background reaction. The mandatory controls are a **no-protein metal-only blank**, a **chelator-quench** (activity must drop when excess EDTA is added), and a **scrambled-active-site mutant** (mutate the coordinating residues to alanine; a real designed site loses activity, an adventitious one does not).

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 24.1 · When the metal fools you"}
The most common false positive in metalloenzyme design is **adventitious metal catalysis**. Free Zn²⁺ at micromolar concentrations hydrolyzes p-nitrophenyl esters fast enough to mimic a weak designed hydrolase; trace Cu²⁺ does oxidative chemistry uninvited. The second most common is **mis-metalation**: a site built for one metal loads another from the host and either dies or, worse, shows activity attributed to the wrong ion. The discipline is brutal but simple — no metalloenzyme claim survives without (1) a metal-only blank, (2) loss of activity on chelation, (3) loss of activity in the alanine-scrambled site, and (4) ICP-MS metal stoichiometry. Reviewers who have been burned will ask for all four.
:::

## 24.5 Case Study: A Designed Metallohydrolase

In 2025, a de novo design campaign reported **zinc metallohydrolases** built from scratch — not grafted onto a natural scaffold — that catalyzed ester hydrolysis with catalytic efficiencies reaching **kcat/KM ≈ 5 × 10⁴ M⁻¹s⁻¹** (*Nature*, 2025). What makes the result instructive is not only the efficiency but the **economy**: the active catalysts emerged from only a few rounds of design, each testing **fewer than 96 sequences**, a scale that would have been unthinkable in the Rosetta era when comparable campaigns screened thousands of designs for a handful of weak hits.

The logic followed Section 24.1 exactly. The theozyme fixed a tetrahedral Zn²⁺ with a His/His/Asp-type ligand set and an open fourth position aimed at a catalytic water, with an oxyanion-stabilizing contact positioned for the developing tetrahedral intermediate. Atom-level tools (RFdiffusion-family backbone generation with ligand-aware sequence design) built de novo backbones around that motif, holo prediction filtered for designs that retained the coordination geometry, and the shortlist went to the bench. Crucially, the campaign performed the full control suite — metal-dependence, chelation sensitivity, and active-site knockouts — so the reported activity is attributable to the designed site rather than to adventitious metal. A few rounds of redesign and optimization (Chapter 25) then pushed efficiency into the useful 10⁴–10⁵ M⁻¹s⁻¹ range.

Put in context: a kcat/KM of 5 × 10⁴ M⁻¹s⁻¹ is still several orders of magnitude below a diffusion-limited natural hydrolase (≈ 10⁸ M⁻¹s⁻¹), but it is a genuine, metal-dependent, designed catalyst with no natural template — and it was reached at a design scale small enough for an academic lab to run in a single thesis chapter. That combination of real catalysis and small test sets is the signature of the atom-level era, and the metal is the lever that made the chemistry reachable at all.

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 24 · Design and validate a metal-coordinating site"}
Design a simple metal-coordinating active site and validate it computationally. (1) Build a tetrahedral Zn²⁺ theozyme with a His/His/Asp ligand set and an open fourth coordination position (Worked Example 24.1). (2) Scaffold ~200 backbones around the motif with RFD3 / RFdiffusionAllAtom, holding the metal and ligand atoms fixed. (3) Design sequences with the metal-aware LigandMPNN checkpoint, several per backbone. (4) Re-fold each design as a **holo** complex (protein + Zn²⁺) with AlphaFold3 or Chai-1; measure how far the predicted metal site drifts from the target geometry. (5) Rank by retained coordination (ligand atoms within 0.5 Å, open site preserved) and confidence, and select a top design. Produce a **holo-refold report**: predicted coordination distances and angles, open-site check, oxyanion contact, and a side-by-side of designed vs. predicted active site.

**Platform:** HPC / cloud GPU · **Deliverable:** one designed metal site plus a holo-refold validation report.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **24.1** A catalytic metal expands reachable chemistry but adds a hard geometric constraint; zinc metallohydrolases use a tetrahedral His/His/Asp-type site with an open position that deprotonates water to a nucleophilic hydroxide. Catalytic sites are *open*; structural sites are *closed*.
- **24.2** Heme, PLP, flavin, and NAD(P) each bring their own reactive geometry; the protein's job is orientation and electrostatic tuning, not inventing reactivity.
- **24.3** LigandMPNN designs sequence conditioned on heteroatoms, and RFD3 / RFdiffusionAllAtom scaffold backbones around a fixed metal/cofactor motif.
- **24.4** Metalloenzyme validation demands metal-only blanks, chelation quench, alanine-scrambled controls, ICP-MS stoichiometry, and ideally a holo structure — because adventitious and mis-incorporated metal cause false positives.
- **24.5** A 2025 *Nature* campaign reached de novo Zn metallohydrolases at kcat/KM ≈ 5 × 10⁴ M⁻¹s⁻¹ from a few rounds of < 96 designs, with the full control suite.

6. Write the contig/motif specification (atoms, distances, angles) you would pass to a motif-scaffolding run for a His/His/Glu tetrahedral site with a coordinated water.

**Challenge.**
