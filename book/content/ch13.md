## 13.1 Reaction Selection

A design campaign begins not with a model but with a sentence. The most expensive mistake in de novo enzyme design is to start generating backbones before the target reaction is written down as a balanced chemical equation, because every downstream choice — the transition state you compute in Chapter 14, the motif you diffuse around in Chapter 15, the residues you fix in Chapter 16 — is an answer to a question that the reaction poses. If the reaction is fuzzy, the answers are fuzzy, and the campaign produces a protein that binds something but catalyzes nothing.

Recall the **four questions** that closed Part I and structure all of Part IV: *What reaction? What transition state? What catalytic set? What pocket?* This chapter is dedicated almost entirely to answering the first question precisely enough that the other three become tractable. "Precisely enough" has a concrete meaning: you should be able to write the reactant(s), product(s), and stoichiometry; name the bond being made or broken; and state the physiological or assay conditions (pH, temperature, solvent, cofactor or metal requirement). A reaction that cannot be written this way is not yet a design target.

Three properties make a reaction a *good* candidate for a de novo campaign, especially a first one. First, it should be **single-step or rate-limited by a single step**, so that one transition state dominates and one theozyme can capture the catalytic chemistry. Multi-step reactions with covalent intermediates (think serine-protease acylation/deacylation) require modeling several transition states and are far harder. Second, it should have a **measurable, low-background readout** — ideally a spectrophotometric or fluorogenic substrate so that turnover can be quantified in a plate reader against a clean uncatalyzed baseline. Third, the **uncatalyzed reaction should be slow** but not impossibly so: a reaction with a measurable background rate gives you both a control and a denominator for rate enhancement, while a reaction that proceeds instantly in water leaves nothing to catalyze.

It also matters *why* you are designing the enzyme, because the motivation sets the bar. A **new-chemistry** target (an abiological bond formation with no natural precedent) is the most scientifically ambitious and the least forgiving. A **designer-biocatalyst** target asks for robustness under industrial conditions and may tolerate modest activity. A **mechanistic** target — designing an enzyme as a falsifiable hypothesis about catalysis — can succeed even if the rate enhancement is small, provided the catalytically-dead control confirms the mechanism. Name the motivation explicitly; it is the difference between "this design failed" and "this design taught us that the proton abstraction, not the binding, was limiting."

## 13.2 Mechanism Classification

Once the reaction is fixed, the next task is to classify its **mechanism**, because the mechanism determines what chemical machinery the protein must supply. The most useful first cut is by the kind of chemistry the transition state demands. **Acid–base catalysis** needs a residue to donate or abstract a proton at the right pK_a and geometry — a glutamate, aspartate, histidine, lysine, or tyrosine positioned to act as a general base or general acid. **Covalent (nucleophilic) catalysis** needs a nucleophile (serine, cysteine, lysine) to form a transient bond with the substrate, which implies at least two transition states and usually an oxyanion hole. **Metal-ion catalysis** needs a coordinated metal (Zn²⁺, Fe, Mn, Cu) to polarize a bond, stabilize charge, or activate water; this couples your design to the metal-coordination geometry of Chapter 12. **Electrostatic and proximity catalysis** — preorganized charge complementary to the transition state, and the entropic effect of holding two substrates together — underlies essentially every enzyme and is the one form of catalysis that even a "minimal" designed pocket can deliver.

Most reactions combine these. A useful exercise is to draw the **reaction-coordinate diagram** and annotate, at the transition state, exactly what is happening to the charge distribution: where negative charge is developing (it needs an H-bond donor or a cation to stabilize it), where a proton is in flight (it needs a base or acid at the corresponding position), where a bond is being polarized (it may need a metal). This annotation is the bridge between the abstract mechanism and the concrete catalytic set of the next section. The mechanism is not a label you attach for tidiness; it is the specification of the forces your protein must exert on the substrate at the moment of reaction.

A classification that frequently rescues a campaign is recognizing when **transition-state stabilization alone** suffices. Linus Pauling's insight — that an enzyme is a structure complementary to the transition state rather than to the substrate — means that a pocket which simply binds the TS more tightly than the ground state will accelerate the reaction even with no proton-shuttling residue at all. Many of the field's earliest successes (and the Kemp eliminase we will adopt) lean heavily on this principle: a single well-placed base plus an electrostatically complementary pocket.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 13.1 · Writing a design specification"}
**Problem.** Turn the goal "design an enzyme that performs a retro-aldol reaction on a fluorogenic substrate" into a specification that answers the four questions well enough to begin theozyme construction.

**Solution.**
*Reaction.* Retro-aldol cleavage of 4-hydroxy-4-(6-methoxy-2-naphthyl)-2-butanone → 6-methoxy-2-naphthaldehyde + acetone. Readout: the naphthaldehyde product is fluorescent (excitation ~330 nm, emission ~452 nm), giving a continuous, low-background assay in aqueous buffer at pH 7.5, 25 °C, no metal or cofactor required.

*Transition state.* C–C bond cleavage proceeds through a C–C-breaking transition state with developing enolate character; the carbonyl oxygen accumulates negative charge.

*Catalytic set.* A lysine ε-amine to form a Schiff-base (enamine) intermediate with the substrate ketone — this is covalent catalysis, so note that there will be more than one TS — assisted by a residue to tune the lysine pK_a and a water/ordering network. (For a teaching campaign one might instead pick a simpler single-TS reaction; see §13.5.)

*Pocket.* Hydrophobic cavity sized to the naphthyl group for binding and desolvation of the reactive lysine, with the lysine pointed at the substrate carbonyl carbon at ~3 Å.

The specification now names the reaction, the chemistry of the TS, the catalytic residues, and the pocket — exactly the inputs Chapter 14 needs. It also flags a complication (multiple transition states) honestly, which is itself a planning decision.
:::

## 13.3 Minimal Catalytic Set and Pocket Requirements

With mechanism in hand, reduce it to the **minimal catalytic set**: the smallest collection of functional groups whose precise geometry, if reproduced, would deliver the rate enhancement. This is the seed of the theozyme. The discipline here is *minimalism*. Each additional required group multiplies the difficulty of finding a backbone that can present all of them simultaneously with sub-ångström accuracy, and each one is another residue that sequence design must hold fixed while still folding a soluble protein. A theozyme with two functional groups is dramatically easier to realize than one with five. Start with the fewest groups that the mechanism truly requires, and add second-shell helpers only when a clear catalytic deficit demands them.

For each group, the specification must state three things: its **chemical identity** (a carboxylate base, an H-bond donor, an aromatic ring for π-stacking), its **role** at the transition state (abstracts the proton, stabilizes the developing oxyanion, sandwiches the substrate), and its **geometry relative to the substrate atoms** — the distances and angles that will become the constraints exported in Chapter 14. A carboxylate acting as a general base, for instance, is specified not just as "Asp/Glu" but as an oxygen 2.6–3.0 Å from the proton it abstracts, with the O···H–C angle near linear so that the proton transfers along the lone-pair direction.

The **pocket** is the container that holds the catalytic set against the substrate and excludes bulk water from the reactive center. Its requirements follow from the substrate and the mechanism: a shape complementary to the substrate (sized for binding, not so tight as to forbid the geometric change at the TS), a polarity matched to the chemistry (hydrophobic to raise the pK_a and reactivity of a buried base; capable of desolvating a charge that the TS will then stabilize), and an accessible channel for substrate in and product out. The pocket is also where **electrostatic preorganization** lives — the field that a folded protein imposes on its active site, complementary to the TS charge distribution, is one of the largest contributors to catalysis and one of the hardest to design deliberately. We will return to it as a filter (Chapter 18) and a frequent cause of failure (Part VI).

::: {.method data-title="Method 13.1 · The go/no-go gate template"}
A go/no-go gate is a checkpoint, defined *before* the campaign starts, at which results are compared to a pre-committed threshold and the campaign either proceeds, iterates, or stops. Build one for each DBTL stage.

1. **Name the stage and its single deliverable** (e.g., "Theozyme: a DFT-validated TS with attached catalytic groups").
2. **State the quantitative pass criterion** for that deliverable (e.g., "exactly one imaginary frequency; constraint geometries within tolerance of the QM optimum").
3. **State the minimum quantity** that must pass to proceed (e.g., "≥ 200 backbones with motif RMSD < 1.0 Å").
4. **Name the control** that must also pass or fail as expected at this stage.
5. **Write the action for each outcome**: pass → proceed; partial → defined iteration (looser/tighter parameters); fail → stop and diagnose.
6. **Record the decision and its date** in the design notebook, with the numbers, before looking at the next stage's data.
:::

## 13.4 Success Criteria and Go/No-Go Gates

This is the section that justifies the chapter's epigraph. The single most common methodological failure in the literature — and the easiest for a reviewer to catch — is declaring success against a threshold chosen *after* seeing the data. The remedy is to commit, in writing and before any computation, to what "success" means for the campaign as a whole and at each gate.

At the campaign level, success is a statement about **catalysis**, not binding. The minimal honest claim is a measurable **rate enhancement** over the uncatalyzed reaction, k_cat/k_uncat, demonstrated with the full control suite. Set a numeric threshold in advance: for a teaching or pilot campaign, a rate enhancement of roughly 10²–10³ (k_cat/k_uncat) or a catalytic proficiency that clears background by a comfortable margin is a defensible bar; record the actual number you will accept. State it in the units you will measure — k_cat (s⁻¹), K_M (M), and the second-order k_cat/K_M (M⁻¹s⁻¹) — and state the background rate k_uncat you will compare against. A design that merely binds the substrate with high affinity but turns over no faster than buffer is *not* a hit, however confident its structure prediction (recall Red Flags 1.1).

The criteria are meaningless without **controls**, and the controls must be designed in from the start. Three are non-negotiable. The **uncatalyzed (buffer-only) control** establishes k_uncat and the assay background. The **scaffold-only or non-catalytic protein control** rules out adventitious catalysis by any protein surface and by the buffer additives. And — the control that uniquely validates a *designed* mechanism — the **catalytically-dead mutant**: the same protein with the single proposed catalytic residue mutated to something inert (the general base Glu/Asp → Gln/Asn or → Ala). If activity tracks the catalytic residue — present in the wild-type design, abolished in the dead mutant — you have positive evidence that your designed mechanism, not a contaminant or an artifact, is responsible. Plan this mutant now, in the specification, so that the gene is ordered alongside the design and the controls are never an afterthought. (We treat the full control logic experimentally in Chapter 19; the point here is that it begins at specification time.)

The **go/no-go gates** (Method 13.1) distribute these criteria across the pipeline so that the campaign fails *early and cheaply* when it is going to fail. A theozyme that does not pass the frequency check (Chapter 14) should never consume scaffold-generation compute. A backbone library with too few high-fidelity motifs (Chapter 15) should not advance to sequence design. A sequence set in which the catalytic residues drift (Chapter 16) should be re-designed, not synthesized. Each gate has a number attached *before* the data arrive. This is what converts a hope into an experiment.

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 13.1 · No predefined success criteria"}
- Choosing the activity threshold after seeing which designs were active ("they cleared 50× background, so 50× is our cutoff").
- Reporting tight binding (low K_d) and implying catalysis without a turnover number.
- Omitting the catalytically-dead mutant, so a positive result cannot be attributed to the designed mechanism.
- Defining "success" only at the end of the pipeline, with no intermediate gates — guaranteeing maximum compute spent on a doomed campaign.
- Stating a rate enhancement without stating k_uncat or the buffer-only background it was measured against.
:::

## 13.5 Choosing a Teaching-Friendly Model Reaction

For the running campaign of Part IV we need a reaction that is real, historically important, single-transition-state, cleanly assayable, and forgiving enough that a student can carry it end to end. The **Kemp elimination** satisfies every criterion, which is why it has become the field's standard benchmark.

The Kemp elimination is the base-catalyzed ring-opening of 5-nitrobenzisoxazole: a base abstracts the proton at C3, the isoxazole ring opens by breaking the weak N–O bond, and the product is 2-cyano-4-nitrophenol (with the conjugate acid of the base). It is a concerted, single-step E2-like process — *one* transition state — in which negative charge develops on the phenolate oxygen as the ring opens. The product is brightly yellow (the nitrophenolate absorbs strongly near 380 nm), giving a continuous, low-background spectrophotometric assay in a plate reader. The uncatalyzed reaction in water is slow and well characterized, providing a clean k_uncat. And the catalytic logic is minimal and legible: a general **base** (a carboxylate or, classically, a buried glutamate/aspartate) to abstract the proton, an **H-bond donor** to stabilize the developing phenolate charge, and an aromatic residue for **π-stacking** to bind and orient the planar substrate. That three-group theozyme is exactly the worked example of Chapter 14.

The Kemp elimination is also pedagogically honest about *limits*. The first designed Kemp eliminases (Röthlisberger et al., 2008) achieved real but modest rate enhancements; subsequent directed evolution (the **HG3.17** lineage, Blomberg et al., 2013) improved one design by orders of magnitude to k_cat/K_M near 2 × 10⁵ M⁻¹s⁻¹, demonstrating both that de novo design can produce a genuine catalyst and that it often produces a *starting point* that optimization then finishes. It therefore teaches the whole DBTL loop, not just the design half. We adopt it as the capstone's model reaction with eyes open: it is not the hardest target, and that is the point — a first campaign should be winnable.

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 13 · Write the design specification for your capstone enzyme"}
Write the complete specification for the capstone enzyme you will carry through Part IV (the target is detailed in Appendix E). On paper or in your design notebook, produce a one-page **design brief** that answers the four questions and commits to success criteria *before* any computation.

1. **Reaction.** Write the balanced equation, name the bond made/broken, and state the assay readout and conditions (pH, temperature, solvent, any metal/cofactor). Identify your design motivation (new chemistry / biocatalyst / mechanism).
2. **Mechanism.** Classify it (acid–base / covalent / metal / electrostatic–proximity), draw the reaction-coordinate diagram, and annotate where charge develops and protons move at the TS.
3. **Minimal catalytic set and pocket.** List the smallest set of functional groups with each one's identity, role, and target geometry; specify the pocket's shape, polarity, and substrate channel.
4. **Success criteria.** Commit to a numeric rate-enhancement threshold in real kinetic units (k_cat, K_M, k_cat/K_M; k_uncat baseline), and name the full control suite — buffer-only, scaffold-only, and the specific catalytically-dead mutant (which residue → what).
5. **Go/no-go gates.** Using Method 13.1, write a one-line gate with a numeric pass criterion for each downstream stage (theozyme, scaffolds, sequences).

**Platform:** paper / design notebook · **Deliverable:** a one-page design brief with predefined quantitative success criteria, controls (including the catalytically-dead mutant), and stage gates.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **13.1** A campaign begins with a single named reaction written as a balanced equation; good first targets are single-step, cleanly assayable, and have a measurable uncatalyzed background.
- **13.2** Mechanism (acid–base, covalent, metal, electrostatic/proximity) dictates the catalytic apparatus; annotate the reaction-coordinate diagram to see where charge develops and protons fly.
- **13.3** Reduce the mechanism to a minimal catalytic set — each group specified by identity, role, and geometry — plus a pocket that holds the set against the substrate and excludes water.
- **13.4** Commit to quantitative success criteria (a rate enhancement threshold in real kinetic units) and a full control suite — including a planned catalytically-dead mutant — *before* any computation, distributed across go/no-go gates.
- **13.5** The Kemp elimination is the book's model reaction: single-TS, spectrophotometric, minimal three-group catalytic logic, and a documented design-then-evolve history.
