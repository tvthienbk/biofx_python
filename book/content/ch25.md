A first design is a starting point, not a product. This chapter is about turning weak hits into useful catalysts. It covers the triage decision (which hits deserve investment), the classic engine of error-prone PCR plus screening, structure-guided redesign of the active site, and the modern coupling of directed evolution with machine-learning models that predict variant activity. ## 25.1 When (and When Not) to Optimize

The epigraph states the central fact of the post-design phase. A de novo campaign that returns a weak hit — a designed esterase at kcat/KM ≈ 10² M⁻¹s⁻¹, say — has produced something genuinely valuable: a folded protein with the intended chemistry, however feeble. Optimization exists to amplify that signal. But not every hit is worth optimizing, and the first skill of this chapter is **triage**.

A hit is **worth optimizing** when three conditions hold. First, the activity is **real** — metal-dependent and control-clean (Chapter 24), above background, and attributable to the designed site, not to contamination or a promiscuous trace activity. Second, the mechanism is **on-pathway**: the enzyme appears to be doing the intended chemistry by the intended route, so that improving binding or positioning will improve the target reaction rather than some side reaction. Third, there is **headroom**: the current efficiency sits far below the diffusion limit (≈ 10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹s⁻¹), which it almost always does, so order-of-magnitude gains are physically available.

A hit is **not worth optimizing** when it is **dead** in a way no amount of tinkering fixes: misfolded or aggregation-prone in a manner that mutation cannot rescue, catalytically reliant on an artifact, or so far from the intended geometry that the "activity" is a different reaction entirely. The hardest triage calls involve expression: a design that does not express in *E. coli* may be perfectly catalytic if it can be made soluble, and a switch of host, tag, or fusion partner is often a better first move than mutagenesis. Triage is an evaluation, not a reflex — and it is cheaper to kill a bad lead on paper than after three rounds of screening.

::: {.conceptcheck data-title="Concept Check 25.1"}
You have two hits. Hit A: kcat/KM = 5 × 10² M⁻¹s⁻¹, control-clean, well-expressed, correct stereochemistry. Hit B: kcat/KM = 5 × 10³ M⁻¹s⁻¹ but activity drops only 20% when you mutate the designed catalytic residue to alanine. Which do you advance, and why is the higher number the worse lead?
:::

## 25.2 Error-Prone PCR and Screening

The classic engine of optimization is **directed evolution**: generate a diverse library of variants, screen or select for improvement, keep the winners, and repeat. Frances Arnold's foundational insight — that iterated rounds of mutation and selection can climb a fitness landscape toward almost any property — earned a share of the 2018 Nobel Prize and remains the field's reliable workhorse.

The standard diversity generator is **error-prone PCR (epPCR)**, which uses a low-fidelity polymerase, manganese, or biased nucleotide pools to introduce random point mutations during amplification. The single most important knob is the **mutation rate**, tuned to land **one to three amino-acid substitutions per gene** on average. The reason is a fitness-landscape argument: most mutations are neutral or deleterious, so a library with five or more mutations per gene is dominated by dead variants, while a library with much less than one per gene is mostly wild-type and wastes screening capacity. One-to-three is the sweet spot where a useful fraction of variants are both viable and meaningfully different.

![**Figure 25.1** The directed-evolution cycle: a parent sequence is diversified (epPCR or recombination), the library is screened or selected, improved variants become the parents of the next round, and the cycle repeats. ML-guided variant selection (Section 25.4) replaces random diversification with model-proposed variants.](assets/figures/directed_evolution.png)

**Screening** measures each variant individually (microplate assays, colorimetric or fluorogenic substrates) and is limited to ~10³–10⁴ variants per round but tolerates almost any readout. **Selection** couples survival or growth to the desired activity and can interrogate 10⁶–10⁹ variants, but requires a clever genetic linkage and is prone to "cheating" — variants that satisfy the selection without doing the intended chemistry. The practical constraint is almost always **screening throughput**, which is why library *quality* matters more than library *size*: a small, well-designed library beats a huge random one when each measurement is expensive.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 25.1 · Sizing a screening campaign"}
**Problem.** You will screen variants in 96-well plates and can run 10 plates per round (≈ 960 wells, ≈ 880 variants after controls). Your epPCR delivers an average of 2 amino-acid substitutions per gene over a 300-residue protein. Roughly what fraction of single-mutant sequence space can one round cover, and what does that tell you about strategy?

**Solution.** A 300-residue protein has 300 × 19 = 5,700 possible single amino-acid substitutions. With an average of 2 substitutions per gene, most variants carry 1–3 mutations, so a single 880-variant round samples on the order of ~10²–10³ distinct mutations — roughly **15–20% of single-mutant space at best**, and far less of the combinatorial multi-mutant space (5,700² ≈ 3 × 10⁷ double mutants). The lesson: random screening cannot remotely cover the landscape. Coverage statistics argue for either (a) **focusing** mutagenesis on active-site positions where beneficial mutations concentrate (site-saturation of ~10 residues = 200 variants, easily covered), or (b) using a **model** to propose the few hundred variants most likely to help (Section 25.4). Brute-force random screening is the least efficient option and should be the fallback, not the plan.
:::

## 25.3 Structure-Guided Active-Site Redesign

When a structure or a confident predicted model is in hand, optimization need not be blind. **Structure-guided redesign** asks a sharper question than random mutagenesis: given where the design fell short, which specific residues, if changed, would fix it?

The diagnostic comes first. A molecular-dynamics trajectory or a careful look at the predicted holo complex (Chapter 24) often reveals the defect: a catalytic residue that drifts out of position, a substrate that does not seat in the productive pose, an oxyanion hole that is a hair too wide, a water network that is missing or misrouted. Each defect implies a targeted intervention — tighten a loop, add a hydrogen-bond donor, fill a packing void, reposition a base. The redesign is then a **focused library**: site-saturation mutagenesis (NNK codons) at the handful of diagnosed positions, or computational re-prediction of a small panel of point mutants, rather than genome-wide randomization.

The payoff of focusing is statistical. Beneficial mutations are not uniformly distributed across a protein; they concentrate at and near the active site and at positions that gate substrate access or conformational dynamics. Saturating ten well-chosen positions (200 variants, trivially screenable) interrogates a far richer slice of useful space than 200 random epPCR variants spread over the whole gene. This is why the most efficient modern campaigns front-load structural reasoning and reserve random mutagenesis for the diversity it uniquely provides — surprising, non-obvious epistatic mutations that no model predicts.

## 25.4 ML-Guided Directed Evolution

The decisive recent change is the marriage of directed evolution to **machine learning**. The idea, developed extensively by Yang, Wu, and Arnold and others, is to treat optimization as a **learning loop**: each round of measured variants is training data for a **sequence–activity model**, and the model proposes the next round's variants instead of random mutagenesis. The model does not need to be accurate everywhere — only accurate enough to rank candidates and steer screening toward the productive region of the landscape.

Two ingredients make this work. The first is a **sequence representation** the model can learn from. Simple **one-hot encodings** of the mutated positions work surprisingly well for focused libraries; richer **protein language model embeddings** — ESM-2 or ESM-1v feature vectors — capture evolutionary and biophysical context and reduce the data needed to learn. The second is a **regression model** mapping representation to measured activity: ridge regression, random forests, or Gaussian processes are standard, the last especially valued because it returns a **calibrated uncertainty** alongside each prediction.

That uncertainty enables the loop's strategic heart: the **acquisition function**. Pure **exploitation** (pick the top predicted variants) is greedy and gets stuck; pure **exploration** (pick the most uncertain) wastes measurements. Bayesian optimization balances them — upper-confidence-bound or expected-improvement acquisition picks variants that are *either* predicted-good *or* informative, so the model improves where it matters. Even a handful of training rounds with a modest model routinely outperforms random screening of the same total number of variants, because the screening budget is spent where it teaches the most.

::: {.method data-title="Method 25.1 · ML-guided variant selection"}
1. **Assemble training data.** Pool all measured variants to date — sequence and quantitative activity (kcat/KM, or a screen proxy). Even a few dozen labeled points can seed the loop.
2. **Encode sequences.** Represent each variant by its mutated positions (one-hot) for focused libraries, or by mean-pooled ESM-2 embeddings for broader sequence variation.
3. **Train a regression model.** Fit ridge / random-forest / Gaussian-process regression with cross-validation; report held-out R² or Spearman ρ so you know how much to trust the ranking.
4. **Score candidates.** Enumerate a candidate set (e.g., all single and double mutants over diagnosed positions) and predict activity with uncertainty.
5. **Acquire the next batch.** Apply an acquisition function (UCB / expected improvement) to select a screenable batch (often 50–200 variants) balancing predicted gain against information.
6. **Measure, then iterate.** Screen the batch, add results to training data, retrain, and repeat. Stop when gains plateau (Section 25.6).
:::

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 25.1 · Sequence–activity modeling"}
**scikit-learn** (1.x) — `Ridge`, `RandomForestRegressor`, `GaussianProcessRegressor`; the standard toolkit for small-data regression with cross-validation built in.
**ESM-2 / ESM-1v** (`fair-esm`, `esm`) — protein language-model embeddings; mean-pool per-residue features or use per-mutation logits as zero-shot priors.
**GPy / GPyTorch / BoTorch** — Gaussian-process regression and Bayesian-optimization acquisition functions when calibrated uncertainty is needed.
**numpy / pandas** — data wrangling and one-hot encoding of mutated positions.
For focused libraries, start with one-hot + ridge; add ESM embeddings only when sequence variation exceeds a few positions.
:::

## 25.5 The Iterative Redesign Loop

These methods are not alternatives but stages of one loop, which is the **Learn** quadrant of the book's DBTL cycle made concrete. A mature optimization campaign cycles: **diagnose** the current best variant (structure, MD, kinetics) → **propose** changes (focused saturation, computational mutants, or model-acquired batch) → **build and screen** the batch → **learn** by folding the new data back into the model and the structural hypothesis → repeat.

The loop blends the methods deliberately. Early rounds, when data are scarce and the landscape is unknown, lean on **structure-guided focusing** to seed the model with informative variants near the active site. Middle rounds hand the wheel to the **ML loop**, which exploits the accumulating data to climb efficiently. Random epPCR is kept in reserve for the diversity it uniquely supplies — the unexpected, epistatic, far-from-active-site mutations that structural reasoning misses and small models cannot extrapolate to. The art is knowing which engine to run when, and the discipline is recording every variant and measurement so the loop can actually close.

## 25.6 Realistic Gains

Honest accounting is the point of this section. A well-run optimization campaign on a real de novo hit typically achieves **10× to 1000× improvement in kcat/KM over two to four rounds**. A 10²-M⁻¹s⁻¹ designed esterase becoming a 10⁴–10⁵-M⁻¹s⁻¹ catalyst is a representative and respectable trajectory — and matches the 2025 metallohydrolase result of Chapter 24, where a few rounds carried a weak design into the useful 10⁴–10⁵ range.

But the gains are not linear, and **plateaus are the rule, not the exception**. The first round often yields the largest jump because the lowest-hanging defects (a mispositioned residue, a too-loose pocket) are the easiest to fix. Subsequent rounds yield **diminishing returns** as the campaign climbs into a local optimum where every nearby mutation is neutral or deleterious. Escaping a plateau usually requires a qualitatively different move — recombination of beneficial mutations from independent lineages, a jump to a new region of sequence space, or a **return to design** (a fresh backbone or a re-engineered active site) rather than further local optimization. Recognizing a plateau early — and not burning three more rounds against it — is as valuable a skill as recognizing a good lead.

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 25.1 · Diminishing returns and plateaus"}
Optimization curves are concave. Expect the first one or two rounds to deliver most of the total improvement and later rounds to flatten. A campaign that has gained 100× and then stalls for two rounds is almost certainly at a local optimum, and the right response is rarely "screen harder." It is to recombine winning mutations, switch the acquisition strategy toward exploration, or redesign. The deepest plateaus are biophysical, not algorithmic: a backbone that cannot position the transition state well will not be coaxed into doing so by point mutations, and the only fix is a better scaffold. Knowing when to stop optimizing and start redesigning is what separates a productive campaign from a stalled one.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **25.1** Triage before optimizing: advance hits that are real, on-pathway, and have headroom; kill leads that are dead, artifactual, or off-mechanism — even when their raw number is higher.
- **25.2** Error-prone PCR plus screening is the classic engine; tune the mutation rate to 1–3 substitutions per gene, and prefer library quality over size because screening is the bottleneck.
- **25.3** Structure-guided redesign focuses mutagenesis on diagnosed active-site positions, where beneficial mutations concentrate, far outperforming whole-gene randomization per measurement.
- **25.4** ML-guided evolution treats each round as training data; a sequence–activity model (one-hot/ESM embeddings + regression) with an acquisition function steers screening toward the productive landscape.
- **25.5** The methods combine into one iterative Learn loop: structure-focus early, ML-exploit mid-campaign, random epPCR in reserve for epistatic diversity.
- **25.6** Typical gains are 10–1000× over 2–4 rounds with diminishing returns; plateaus are biophysical, and escaping them means recombination, exploration, or redesign — not screening harder.

## Problem Set

**Review.**

1. State the three conditions under which a hit is worth optimizing and give a one-line failure mode for each.
2. Why is the epPCR mutation rate tuned to 1–3 amino-acid substitutions per gene? Argue from the fitness landscape.
3. Contrast screening and selection in throughput and in failure modes.
4. Why do beneficial mutations concentrate near the active site, and how does that justify focused libraries?
5. What does a Gaussian process give an optimization loop that a random forest does not?
6. Why are optimization gains concave, and what move escapes a plateau?

**Quantitative & Computational.**

1. A 250-residue protein: how many single amino-acid substitutions exist? If you can screen 950 variants per round, what fraction of single-mutant space does one round cover?
2. Over three rounds you observe kcat/KM of 1.0 × 10², 2.0 × 10³, and 8.0 × 10³ M⁻¹s⁻¹. Compute the per-round fold-improvement and the cumulative gain. Is the campaign plateauing? Justify.
3. You train a ridge model on 40 labeled variants and get cross-validated Spearman ρ = 0.55. A colleague says "that's too low to be useful." Argue, in the context of ranking for acquisition, whether they are right.
4. A UCB acquisition function scores candidate i as μᵢ + κσᵢ. Two candidates: A (μ = 3.0, σ = 0.2), B (μ = 2.4, σ = 1.5). For κ = 0, 1, and 2, which is acquired? Interpret each regime.
5. A focused library saturates 8 positions. How many single mutants? How many double mutants? Which is screenable in one 960-well round, and what does that imply for sampling strategy?
6. You have a screen proxy (fluorescence) that correlates with kcat/KM at Pearson r = 0.7. What are the risks of training a model on the proxy instead of the true kinetic value, and how would you mitigate them?

**Challenge.**

1. Design a two-round ML-guided campaign for a designed esterase given a budget of 200 screened variants total. Specify encoding, model, acquisition strategy, and the round-1/round-2 split, and justify each choice against a random-screening baseline.
2. Your campaign has plateaued at 100× after three rounds. Lay out a decision procedure — with the specific evidence you would gather — for choosing among recombination, exploration, and full redesign.
