## 6.1 AlphaFold2 and ColabFold

The 2020 CASP14 result, in which **AlphaFold2** (AF2) predicted protein structures at near-experimental accuracy, did more than solve a fifty-year problem. For the designer it created a *substrate*: a fast, free oracle that takes a sequence and returns a three-dimensional model with a per-residue estimate of its own reliability. Every generative pipeline in Parts III–IV leans on this oracle to ask the single most important *in silico* question — *does my designed sequence fold to the backbone I asked for?* — before any gene is ordered.

AF2 works by combining evolutionary information with a learned geometric reasoning engine. Given a query sequence, it first builds a **multiple sequence alignment (MSA)** of evolutionary relatives and, where available, structural templates. The MSA is the heart of the method: covarying columns betray residues that contact each other in three dimensions, because a destabilizing mutation on one side of a contact tends to be compensated by a mutation on the other. The **Evoformer** trunk passes information back and forth between a representation of the MSA and a representation of residue–residue pairs, and the **structure module** then folds an explicit set of backbone frames and side-chain torsions into coordinates. The model is run *recycled* — its output fed back as input several times — which sharpens the prediction.

For practical work, almost nobody runs the original AF2 code path directly. **ColabFold** (Mirdita et al., 2022) re-engineered the front end: it replaces the slow genetics-database MSA step with the **MMseqs2** server, which returns an alignment in seconds rather than hours, and wraps the whole pipeline in a Google Colab notebook that needs no local GPU. This is the workhorse the rest of this book assumes. A monomer of a few hundred residues predicts in one to a few minutes on a free Colab T4.

A subtlety that matters enormously for design: AF2 is, in effect, a function of its **MSA depth**. For a natural protein with thousands of homologs, the alignment is rich and the prediction is strong. A *de novo* designed sequence has no natural homologs — its MSA is essentially a single sequence. Remarkably, AF2 still folds many designs correctly in **single-sequence mode**, and this is precisely the regime that makes it useful as a design filter: a confident single-sequence prediction that matches the intended backbone is meaningful evidence that the sequence encodes the fold, not merely that evolution already solved it.

::: {.conceptcheck data-title="Concept Check 6.1"}
You predict a *de novo* designed sequence two ways: once with a deep MSA assembled by accident from a few weak hits, and once in single-sequence mode. The single-sequence model matches your target backbone with high confidence; the MSA model does not. Which result better supports the claim that your *sequence* encodes the fold, and why?
:::

## 6.2 AlphaFold3 and Boltz-2: Ligand-, Cofactor-, and Metal-Aware Prediction

AF2 predicts a protein chain in isolation — its **apo** form. For enzyme design this is a crippling limitation, because an enzyme is defined by what it does to a bound molecule. The active site only makes sense in the presence of the substrate, the catalytic metal, or the cofactor. The 2024 generation of models closed this gap by becoming **all-atom**: they represent and predict protein, nucleic acid, ligand, ion, and modified residue in one unified token stream.

**AlphaFold3** (Abramson et al., 2024) replaced the AF2 structure module with a **diffusion**-based head that generates atomic coordinates directly, allowing arbitrary chemical components specified by SMILES or CCD code. It predicts protein–ligand, protein–ion, protein–nucleic-acid, and covalently modified complexes in a single model. AF3 is available through the **AlphaFold Server** for non-commercial use (with throughput limits and a restricted ligand set), and the weights were later released for academic use. Its reported accuracy on protein–ligand complexes in the PoseBusters benchmark substantially exceeded classical docking, though — see 6.6 — "exceeded docking" is not "got it right."

**Boltz-2** (2025) is the open-source counterpart that the practicing designer can run locally without a server queue. Built on the AF3 architecture, Boltz-2 predicts all-atom complexes from sequence plus ligand specification and, distinctively, also predicts **binding affinity**, trained to approximate FEP-quality estimates at a fraction of the cost. For enzyme work its value is concrete: you can hand it a designed pocket plus the substrate and ask whether the model places the ligand where your theozyme intended, with metals coordinated by the residues you chose. **Chai-1** (2024) is a third open model in the same family. None of these requires you to pre-dock the ligand; they co-fold protein and ligand together.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 6.1 · Structure prediction (version-stamped)"}
**ColabFold** (v1.5.x, 2024) — AF2 with MMseqs2 MSAs in a Colab notebook; supports `num_recycles`, single-sequence mode, and AF2-Multimer for complexes.  
**AlphaFold3** (Abramson et al., 2024) — all-atom diffusion model; via the AlphaFold Server (non-commercial, limited ligand set) or released academic weights for local runs.  
**Boltz-2** (2025) — open-source all-atom co-folding *and* binding-affinity prediction; runs locally on a single modern GPU.  
**Chai-1** (2024) — open all-atom complex prediction, AF3-class architecture.  
**ESMFold** (v1, 2022) — single-sequence, MSA-free, seconds-fast; orthogonal check (§6.3).  
Always record the exact model, weights date, recycle count, and random seed in the design notebook.
:::

## 6.3 ESMFold for Fast Orthogonal Checks

**ESMFold** (Lin et al., 2023) folds a protein from a single sequence with no MSA at all. In place of an alignment it uses the internal representations of the **ESM-2** protein language model, which learned the statistics of natural sequences during masked-token pre-training. The result is a model that is roughly an order of magnitude faster than AF2 and somewhat less accurate on hard targets — but for the designer its speed and its *methodological independence* are exactly the point.

ESMFold is the ideal **orthogonal filter**. Because it reaches its prediction by a different route than AF2 (a language model rather than an explicit MSA + Evoformer), agreement between the two is stronger evidence than either alone. A common triage workflow: screen thousands of designs through ESMFold in minutes, keep those that fold confidently to the target, then re-predict the survivors with ColabFold or Boltz-2 at higher cost. Where the two methods *disagree* — ESMFold confident, AF2 not, or vice versa — treat the design as suspect rather than trusting the more favorable number. Cherry-picking the kinder predictor is one of the most common self-deceptions in computational design.

## 6.4 Confidence Metrics Decoded

A prediction without a confidence estimate is nearly useless for filtering. The 2020s models all emit calibrated self-assessments; you must read them correctly.

**pLDDT** (predicted Local Distance Difference Test) is a *per-residue* score from 0 to 100 estimating how well the local atomic environment of that residue matches what an experimental structure would show. It is local: a residue with pLDDT 90 sits in a confidently predicted neighborhood. By convention, pLDDT > 90 is very high, 70–90 confident, 50–70 low, and < 50 is best read as a *disorder predictor* — the model is telling you it sees no single well-defined conformation. Crucially, pLDDT says nothing about whether two confident regions are correctly *positioned relative to each other*.

That relative question is answered by the **PAE** (Predicted Aligned Error) matrix. PAE(i, j) is the model's expected positional error, in Å, of residue j when the predicted and true structures are aligned on residue i. It is a full N×N matrix and is the single most informative confidence output for enzyme design, because catalysis depends on the *mutual* arrangement of residues that may sit far apart in sequence. Low PAE between two regions means the model is confident about their relative geometry; a high-PAE block between two domains means they are confidently folded individually but their relative orientation is uncertain.

Two global scores summarize PAE. **pTM** (predicted TM-score) estimates the TM-score of the whole prediction to the true structure — a single number for overall topological correctness. **ipTM** (interface pTM) does the same but restricted to inter-chain contacts, and is the primary confidence read-out for complexes and binders: an ipTM above ~0.8 indicates a confidently predicted interface, below ~0.6 a dubious one. AF3 and Boltz-2 extend these to **ranking scores** that fold in ligand and metal placement.

![**Figure 6.1** Reading the confidence stack. pLDDT (left) is a per-residue color over the backbone — local geometric accuracy. The PAE matrix (right) is the expected relative error between every pair of residues; off-diagonal blue blocks mean two regions are confidently positioned with respect to each other, red blocks mean their relative orientation is uncertain. pTM and ipTM collapse PAE into single global scores.](assets/figures/confidence_metrics.png)

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 6.1 · Reading a PAE matrix at an active site"}
**Problem.** You designed a two-domain enzyme whose catalytic triad places residues 45 (domain A), 188 (domain B), and 210 (domain B) around a substrate. The prediction shows pLDDT > 90 for all three residues. But the PAE matrix has a deep-blue (low-error) block within domain B and a red (high-error) block between domains A and B. The PAE between residue 45 and residues 188/210 is ~18 Å. Is the active-site geometry trustworthy?

**Solution.** No. High pLDDT confirms each residue is locally well-modeled, and the low intra-B PAE confirms 188 and 210 are confidently positioned *relative to each other*. But the catalytic set spans the domain boundary, and the A–B PAE of ~18 Å says the model has no confident opinion about where domain A sits relative to domain B. The triad's *mutual* geometry — the only thing that matters for catalysis — is therefore unresolved. The correct reading is "two confident sub-structures in an uncertain relative pose," not "confident active site." Flag for redesign with a rigidifying inter-domain interface, or treat the inter-domain PAE as a hard filter.
:::

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 6.1 · pLDDT is not a stability score"}
- **Reading pLDDT as thermostability or activity.** pLDDT predicts *local geometric agreement with an experimental structure*. It is not ΔG of folding, not a melting temperature, not k_cat. A design can have uniform pLDDT 95 and be catalytically dead or thermally fragile.
- **Averaging away the PAE.** A single mean PAE hides exactly the inter-domain or protein–ligand block you most need to see. Always inspect the matrix, not just its mean.
- **Trusting high pLDDT in a region the model thinks is rigid but the real protein is dynamic.** High confidence in a single conformation says nothing about whether the protein samples others.
- **Comparing pLDDT across models** (AF2 vs. ESMFold vs. AF3) as if the scales were identical. They are separately calibrated.
:::

## 6.5 Predicting Holo States

The reason all-atom models matter for this book is that an enzyme validated only in its apo form has been validated for the wrong thing. The design question is always *holo*: with the substrate, metal, or cofactor present, does the active site assemble correctly?

A practical holo-validation workflow with AF3 or Boltz-2: specify the protein sequence together with the ligand (SMILES or CCD), any catalytic metal ion (e.g., Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe), and any cofactor (heme, PLP, NAD⁺, flavin) as additional tokens, then co-fold the complex. Read three things in order. First, the protein **pLDDT and intra-chain PAE** — is the scaffold itself confident? Second, the **ligand placement**: does the model seat the substrate in the designed pocket, with the reactive atom near the catalytic residues? Third, **metal coordination geometry** — are the intended coordinating residues actually within bonding distance (typically ~2.0–2.3 Å for Zn–N/O) and in a sensible coordination number? For metalloenzymes especially, a model that places the metal but coordinates it with the wrong residues, or leaves a coordination site open, is diagnosing a flawed design.

Compare every holo prediction to reality where you can. If you are validating a *known* enzyme as a positive control (the recommended first step, and the subject of this chapter's lab), align the predicted holo complex to the deposited PDB structure and report the all-atom and active-site **RMSD**, plus the ligand pose RMSD. For *designed* enzymes there is no ground-truth structure yet, so the holo prediction becomes a *self-consistency* test: does the sequence designed for a given backbone, when co-folded with the substrate, reproduce that backbone *and* the intended ligand pose? This self-consistency loop — generate backbone → design sequence → re-predict holo → compare — is the backbone of the *in silico* filter in Part IV.

::: {.bench data-title="From the Bench 6.1"}
A crystallographer's caution: a co-folded model is a hypothesis about the *ground state* of the complex, and crystals can trap states that solution prediction will not show — alternate conformers, crystallographic waters bridging the metal, ligand poses biased by lattice contacts. When your predicted holo pose disagrees with a PDB structure, the model is not automatically wrong, but the burden of proof is on the prediction. Check the deposited electron density (the EDS server) before concluding the model "beat" the crystal structure.
:::

## 6.6 What Prediction Cannot Tell You

The single most expensive mistake in computational enzyme design is mistaking a confident static structure for a working catalyst. Prediction is the cheapest experiment you will run — and like any cheap experiment, it measures less than you wish.

Prediction returns **one dominant conformation**, typically the ground state. Catalysis is motion: induced fit, loop closure over a bound substrate, conformational selection, the trajectory toward and through the transition state. A model that nails the ground state tells you little about the conformational ensemble that does the chemistry, and nothing about the **transition state** itself — there is no learned representation of a bond mid-cleavage. Rate, ΔG‡, and k_cat live in physics the model never saw.

These tools are also **interpolators over their training distribution**, which is overwhelmingly the natural PDB. They are strongest where evolution has been busy and weakest exactly where de novo design wants to go: genuinely novel folds, non-natural substrates, abiological chemistries, and ligands or metal geometries absent from the training data. AF3 and Boltz-2 can place a drug-like ligand they have seen analogs of; hand them a bespoke transition-state analog or an unusual organometallic cofactor and confidence becomes unreliable. Reported benchmark numbers (e.g., PoseBusters pose accuracy) describe *natural-like* complexes and overstate performance on the long tail that matters here.

Finally, prediction is silent on everything off the structure axis: expression, solubility, aggregation, pH and temperature stability, and — the headline — **activity**. A pipeline that filters only on pLDDT, PAE, and ligand pose will happily advance a beautifully folded, perfectly inactive protein. The structure is necessary, not sufficient. That gap is why this book devotes all of Part V to building, expressing, and *assaying* the molecule — the half of the field a purely computational treatment ignores.

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 6.1 · Where prediction quietly fails"}
- **Dynamics:** one ground-state snapshot, no ensemble, no transition state. Allosteric and induced-fit behavior are invisible.
- **Novel chemistry:** accuracy degrades on substrates, cofactors, and metal geometries outside the training distribution — precisely the de novo regime.
- **Unseen ligands:** SMILES the model has no analog for produce confident-looking but unreliable poses; the confidence score does not always flag the extrapolation.
- **Function:** no metric here predicts k_cat, K_M, expression, or solubility. Confident fold ≠ active enzyme.

Honest practice: use prediction to *reject* implausible designs cheaply and to *rank* survivors, never to *certify* a winner. The assay certifies.
:::

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 6 · Apo, holo, and the PDB"}
Choose a well-characterized metalloenzyme or cofactor-dependent enzyme with a deposited holo structure (e.g., carbonic anhydrase II, PDB 1CA2, Zn²⁺; or a PLP-dependent transaminase). Work three targets in parallel — your enzyme, a close homolog, and a deliberately mutated active-site variant.

1. **Apo prediction.** Run ColabFold on each sequence (5 recycles). Record per-residue pLDDT, the PAE matrix, and pTM.
2. **Holo prediction.** Run AF3 (AlphaFold Server) or Boltz-2 on each sequence *with* the metal/cofactor and substrate specified. Record protein pLDDT, ligand/metal placement, coordination distances, and the ranking/affinity score.
3. **Ground truth.** Align both predictions to the deposited PDB structure. Report all-atom RMSD, active-site RMSD (catalytic residues + metal), and ligand-pose RMSD.
4. **Interpret.** For the mutant, does the predicted holo state correctly lose the coordinating residue? Does any confidence metric *flag* the broken site, or does pLDDT stay high (the Red Flags 6.1 lesson)?

**Platform:** Google Colab (ColabFold) + AlphaFold Server or local Boltz-2 · **Deliverable:** a confidence report across the three targets — a table of pLDDT/PAE-block/pTM/ipTM, RMSD-to-PDB, and metal-coordination distances, with a one-paragraph verdict on what each metric did and did not catch.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **6.1** AlphaFold2, run through ColabFold with MMseqs2 MSAs, is the workhorse fold-check; single-sequence mode is the regime that makes it meaningful for *de novo* sequences with no homologs.
- **6.2** AlphaFold3 and the open Boltz-2/Chai-1 family are all-atom diffusion models that co-fold protein with ligands, metals, and cofactors — essential for enzymes, which are defined by what they bind.
- **6.3** ESMFold is MSA-free and seconds-fast; its methodological independence makes it the ideal orthogonal filter, and disagreement with AF2 should be treated as a warning, not an excuse to cherry-pick.
- **6.4** pLDDT is per-residue *local* accuracy; PAE is the *relative*-geometry matrix that catalysis depends on; pTM and ipTM are global topology and interface summaries.
- **6.5** Holo prediction validates the active site with substrate, metal, and cofactor present; compare to the PDB for known enzymes and use a self-consistency loop for designs.
- **6.6** Prediction returns one ground-state conformation, extrapolates poorly to novel chemistry, and predicts no kinetics or expression — it rejects and ranks, it does not certify.
