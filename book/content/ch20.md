## 20.1 Codon Optimization and Gene Synthesis

Your design is a string of amino acids. A cell reads codons, so the first build step is reverse-translation, and the choices made here quietly determine whether you ever see protein. The genetic code is degenerate — most amino acids have two to six synonymous codons — and the synonymous choice is not neutral to expression. Each host species has a characteristic **codon usage** that mirrors the abundance of its charged tRNA pool; using rare codons starves the ribosome and stalls translation, particularly when rare codons cluster. **Codon optimization** is the process of choosing synonymous codons to match the expression host.

Naïve optimization simply replaces every codon with the host's single most frequent synonym. This is a mistake. A monotonous, maximally "optimal" sequence can fold mRNA into stable secondary structure at the ribosome-binding site, create internal Shine–Dalgarno-like sequences that pause ribosomes, or introduce long homopolymer and repeat tracts that are hard to synthesize and recombination-prone. Modern practice instead targets a **codon adaptation index (CAI)** in a sensible window (roughly 0.7–0.9 for *E. coli*), flattens GC content into a 40–60% band, smooths local GC over a sliding window, and *removes* a defined blacklist: restriction sites used in your cloning, the ribosome-binding-site consensus, cryptic splice sites (for eukaryotic hosts), and runs of identical bases longer than about 8–9 nt. The 5′ region of the coding sequence deserves special attention: weak mRNA secondary structure for the first ~15 codons correlates strongly with high expression, so good tools deliberately reduce structure there even at the cost of CAI.

Once a sequence is designed, you order it as a **synthetic gene**. Commercial gene synthesis assembles oligonucleotides into double-stranded DNA, sequence-verifies it, and delivers it in a cloning vector or as a linear fragment for around US$0.07–0.15 per base pair with turnaround of a few business days to two weeks. A typical 250–400-residue enzyme gene (750–1,200 bp) costs well under US$200 — cheap relative to the weeks of bench time it commits you to. Order codon-optimized, sequence-perfect, and flanked by whatever overhangs your cloning method needs.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 20.1 · Codon-optimization tools"}
**IDT Codon Optimization Tool** (web, 2024) — free; optimizes for a chosen host, screens for IDT synthesis complexity (repeats, GC extremes, hairpins). Pairs naturally with ordering gBlocks or clonal genes.
**Thermo Fisher GeneArt GeneOptimizer** (web/desktop) — multi-parameter optimization (CAI, GC, motif removal, mRNA structure) with a documented track record across hosts.
**DNAchisel** (Python, v3.2) — open-source, scriptable, *constraint-based*: you declare hard constraints (avoid `EcoRI`, GC in [0.4, 0.6], no 9-mer repeats) and soft objectives (match *E. coli* codon usage), and it optimizes. The right choice when optimization must be reproducible and version-controlled in a pipeline.
**Benchling / GenSmart (GenScript)** — vendor tools tied to ordering workflows.
Always re-screen the final sequence for *your* cloning enzymes and verify the 5′ region is structurally relaxed.
:::

## 20.2 Cloning Strategy and Tags

The synthetic gene must land in an **expression vector** that supplies a promoter, ribosome-binding site, start and stop codons, an origin of replication, and a selectable marker. For *E. coli*, the **pET** family (Novagen) is the default: a strong T7 promoter that is silent unless T7 RNA polymerase is present, which is the basis of the BL21(DE3) system in 20.3. pET-28a (kanamycin resistance, N- or C-terminal His₆) and pET-22b (ampicillin, C-terminal His₆, optional *pelB* periplasmic signal) cover most cases.

Getting the gene into the vector is a solved problem; pick the method that gives you scarless, sequence-defined junctions. **Gibson assembly** joins fragments with ~20–40 bp homology overlaps in a single isothermal reaction (50 °C, 15–60 min) and is ideal when you order the gene with overhangs matching a linearized vector. **Golden Gate** uses Type IIS enzymes (BsaI, BsmBI) for scarless, ordered, one-pot assembly — excellent for libraries and modular tag swapping. Classic **restriction–ligation** still works when convenient sites flank the insert. Whatever the method, confirm the construct by full-insert **Sanger sequencing** (or whole-plasmid nanopore sequencing) before you trust it; a single frameshift or point mutation invalidates everything downstream.

**Tags** are short appended sequences that aid purification, detection, or solubility. The near-universal purification tag is the **hexahistidine (His₆) tag**, which binds immobilized Ni²⁺ or Co²⁺ for affinity capture (20.5). Place it N- or C-terminally; the choice matters when a terminus sits near the designed active site or a structural element — inspect your model and avoid burying the tag or blocking the pocket. Interpose a **cleavable linker** with a protease site (TEV, `ENLYFQ↓G`, or PreScission/3C, `LEVLFQ↓GP`) so you can remove the tag after purification; TEV protease is cheap, specific, and active at 4 °C overnight, leaving a single non-native Gly. For detection, a short **FLAG** or **Strep-tag II** can be added. Solubility-enhancing fusions — **MBP** (maltose-binding protein), **SUMO**, **Trx** (thioredoxin), **GST** — are larger partners that can rescue insoluble designs (see 20.6); SUMO is attractive because its cognate protease (Ulp1/SENP) leaves a *native* N-terminus.

::: {.conceptcheck data-title="Concept Check 20.1"}
Your design has its N-terminus packed against the catalytic pocket in the model, and the C-terminus solvent-exposed on the opposite face. Where do you put the His₆ tag, and why? What single experiment would you do before trusting a tagged construct's activity?
:::

## 20.3 Expression in *E. coli* BL21(DE3)

*E. coli* is the first host you should try: it grows fast, is cheap, and is the most thoroughly characterized expression chassis on Earth. The standard strain is **BL21(DE3)**, which is protease-deficient (lacking *lon* and *ompT*) and carries a chromosomal copy of **T7 RNA polymerase** under the *lacUV5* promoter — the "(DE3)" lysogen. When you add the lactose mimic **IPTG** (isopropyl-β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside), *lacUV5* fires, T7 polymerase is made, and it transcribes your gene from the T7 promoter at ferocious speed. That speed is a double-edged sword: rapid, high-level translation often outruns the cell's folding capacity and dumps your protein into **inclusion bodies** (insoluble aggregates). The single most effective lever against this is *slowing everything down* by expressing at low temperature.

For a first attempt with a de novo design, **express at 16–18 °C** after induction. Low temperature slows transcription, translation, and aggregation while leaving folding mostly intact, and it is the highest-yield single change you can make for a marginal design. Common companion strains help with specific problems: **Rosetta** strains supply tRNAs for rare codons (a backup if optimization was imperfect); **SHuffle** and **Origami** strains have an oxidizing cytoplasm that permits disulfide-bond formation — relevant if your design contains structural disulfides, which most cytoplasmic *E. coli* cannot form.

::: {.method data-title="Method 20.1 · Expression of a designed protein in BL21(DE3) at 16–18 °C"}
1. **Transform.** Add 1–2 µL miniprepped plasmid to 50 µL chemically competent BL21(DE3) on ice (30 min); heat-shock 42 °C, 30–45 s; recover in 450 µL SOC at 37 °C, 200 rpm, 60 min; plate on LB-agar with the vector's antibiotic (kanamycin 50 µg/mL or carbenicillin 100 µg/mL). Grow overnight at 37 °C.
2. **Starter culture.** Inoculate 10 mL LB + antibiotic from a single colony; grow overnight at 37 °C, 220 rpm.
3. **Day culture.** Dilute starter 1:100 into 0.5–1 L LB (or auto-induction TB) + antibiotic in a baffled flask. Grow at 37 °C, 220 rpm.
4. **Monitor growth.** Track OD₆₀₀. When it reaches **0.6–0.8** (mid-log), the culture is induction-ready.
5. **Pre-cool and induce.** Move flasks to a **16–18 °C** shaker for 20–30 min to equilibrate, then add **IPTG to 0.1–0.5 mM** (0.2 mM is a good default). Take a pre-induction "0 h" 1 mL sample for SDS-PAGE.
6. **Express.** Shake at 16–18 °C, 200 rpm, for **16–20 h** (overnight).
7. **Harvest.** Centrifuge 4,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C. Decant, weigh the pellet, and freeze at −80 °C or proceed to lysis.
8. **Check the small scale first.** Run pre- and post-induction whole-cell samples, plus soluble vs. insoluble fractions of a small test lysis, on SDS-PAGE before committing to a full purification. *This 3 h check saves days.*
:::

## 20.4 Alternative Hosts

When *E. coli* fails — no expression, intractable inclusion bodies, or a requirement for eukaryotic features — change hosts before you change designs. The yeasts ***Pichia pastoris*** (*Komagataella phaffii*) and ***Saccharomyces cerevisiae*** are eukaryotes with secretory pathways, native disulfide-bond machinery, and chaperone repertoires that handle many proteins *E. coli* cannot; *Pichia* in particular secretes correctly folded protein to high density, simplifying purification. ***Cell-free protein synthesis*** (CFPS) — *E. coli* lysate or PURE reconstituted systems plus your DNA template and an energy mix — skips cell viability entirely, expresses in hours, tolerates toxic products, and is superb for rapid screening of many designs in parallel; yields are lower per reaction but the loop is fast. For designs requiring mammalian post-translational modifications or where folding genuinely needs a metazoan environment, transient transfection of **HEK293** (e.g., Expi293) or insect-cell **baculovirus** systems are the standard, at higher cost and longer timelines. The decision rule: try *E. coli* at low temperature first, escalate to a solubility fusion or chaperone strain, and only then move to yeast or cell-free. Reach for mammalian/insect systems when the chemistry demands it, not by default.

## 20.5 Purification by IMAC and SEC

A His₆-tagged protein is purified in two orthogonal steps: an affinity capture that exploits the tag, followed by a sizing step that reports on quality.

**Immobilized-metal affinity chromatography (IMAC)** uses a resin charged with Ni²⁺ (Ni-NTA, Ni Sepharose) or Co²⁺ (TALON). The His₆ tag coordinates the immobilized metal; everything else mostly washes through. Lyse cells (sonication or a homogenizer) in a binding buffer at pH ~8 with **physiological-to-high salt and a low imidazole concentration** (typically 20–50 mM NaH₂PO₄/Tris, 300–500 mM NaCl, 10–20 mM imidazole, plus a protease-inhibitor cocktail and, optionally, lysozyme and DNase). Imidazole mimics the histidine side chain; the low concentration in binding/wash buffers competes off weakly bound host proteins, and a high-imidazole **elution buffer (250–500 mM imidazole)** then releases your protein. Clarify the lysate (≥20,000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C) and *only load the soluble supernatant* — the pellet holds the inclusion bodies. After elution, if you used a cleavable tag, add TEV/SUMO protease and dialyze out the imidazole overnight at 4 °C, then run the digest back over Ni resin so the cleaved tag and His-tagged protease stick and your untagged protein flows through (a "reverse IMAC" or subtractive step).

**Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC)**, run on an ÄKTA-style FPLC with a column such as a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL, separates by hydrodynamic size: large species elute first (smallest elution volume), small ones last. SEC is both a polishing step and a quality assay. A well-behaved monodisperse protein gives a single symmetric peak at the volume expected for its molecular weight; soluble aggregates and misfolded oligomers appear at or near the **void volume**, and a "shoulder" or multiple peaks signal heterogeneity. For a de novo design, the SEC trace is your first honest readout of whether the protein is folded and monodisperse — *before* you spend weeks on assays.

An early void/aggregate peak resolves from a later, symmetric monomer peak; integration and a calibration curve convert peak volume to apparent molecular weight.](assets/figures/sec_trace.png)

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 20.1 · Reading an SEC trace"}
**Problem.** You purify a 28 kDa designed enzyme (His-tagged, expected to be monomeric) on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL (void volume V₀ ≈ 8.0 mL, total volume Vₜ ≈ 24 mL). The A₂₈₀ trace shows two peaks: a sharp one at **8.2 mL** and a broad symmetric one at **15.6 mL**. The column was calibrated with standards giving log(MW) = 6.7 − 0.19·Vₑ (Vₑ in mL). Interpret the trace and decide which fractions to keep.

**Solution.** The 8.2 mL peak is essentially at V₀ (8.0 mL): species too large to enter the resin pores — **soluble aggregate / void material**. Discard it. For the 15.6 mL peak, apparent MW = 10^(6.7 − 0.19·15.6) = 10^(6.7 − 2.964) = 10^(3.736) ≈ **5.4 × 10³**… in the calibration's MW units; with a calibration returning daltons this corresponds to roughly **30 kDa**, consistent with a 28 kDa monomer (calibration is approximate; SEC reports hydrodynamic size, not exact mass). The symmetric monomer peak is your protein. **Pool the 15.6 mL peak fractions**, confirm purity by SDS-PAGE, and note the void peak in your records — a large void fraction is a yellow flag about folding even when a clean monomer peak exists. To assign true molecular weight and oligomeric state rigorously, run SEC-MALS (Chapter 21), which does not rely on a shape-dependent calibration.
:::

## 20.6 Troubleshooting Insolubility and No Expression

Two failures dominate the build stage, and both are diagnosable from the SDS-PAGE gel you ran in Method 20.1, step 8.

**No expression** — no new band in the post-induction whole-cell lane. Rank causes: (1) construct error (frameshift, premature stop, wrong reading frame) — re-check the sequencing; (2) toxic product killing or stalling cells — look for poor post-induction growth, and try tighter repression (pLysS strain, glucose in the medium) or lower IPTG; (3) rare-codon stalling — switch to a Rosetta strain or re-optimize; (4) mRNA instability or strong 5′ structure — re-optimize the 5′ region; (5) genuinely unstable protein degraded as fast as it is made. Confirm the construct *first*; most "no expression" is a cloning or sequence problem, not biology.

**Insolubility** — a strong band that is present in the post-induction whole-cell lane and the insoluble pellet but absent from the soluble supernatant. The protein is made but folds into inclusion bodies. The escalation ladder, cheapest first: lower the expression temperature further (try 12–16 °C); reduce IPTG to slow translation; co-express **molecular chaperones** (the GroEL/ES and DnaK/DnaJ/GrpE systems, available on plasmids such as the Takara pG-Tf2/pGro7 set); add an N-terminal **solubility fusion** (MBP or SUMO are the strongest); for disulfide-containing designs, move to a **SHuffle** or **Origami** strain; and only as a last resort, purify from inclusion bodies under denaturant and **refold** *in vitro* (dilution or on-column), accepting low yield and uncertain native state. Each rung is a single cloning or condition change; work down the ladder before abandoning a design.

::: {.bench data-title="From the Bench 20.1 · Rescuing insoluble designs"}
The instinct on seeing inclusion bodies is to declare the design wrong and move on. Resist it for at least one round of rescue, because the *single most informative* and cheapest intervention — dropping to 16 °C with 0.1 mM IPTG overnight — rescues a meaningful fraction of marginal designs at zero extra reagent cost. After that, an MBP or SUMO fusion is the workhorse: SUMO is preferred when the native N-terminus matters because Ulp1 cleavage leaves no scar. A practical trick is the *split-test*: express the design as both a His-only and a His-SUMO fusion side by side at 16 °C; if SUMO is soluble and His-only is not, you have learned that the fold is achievable and only needs kinetic help, which is itself a useful design signal. Keep a small inclusion-body pellet from every prep — if everything else fails, refolding is still a path to *some* material for a first assay. And remember the reality gate: a protein that never leaves the pellet has, for now, no measurable function, however high its pLDDT.
:::

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 20.1"}
- Claiming function for a design that was never purified to a defined, monodisperse species — a band on a whole-cell gel is not a characterized protein.
- Reporting activity from crude lysate without ruling out a host enzyme (see the control suite in Chapter 22).
- Pooling SEC fractions that include the void/aggregate peak and calling the mixture "the protein."
- Skipping the post-induction soluble-vs-insoluble check and scaling straight to a 1 L prep, then discovering everything was in inclusion bodies.
- Trusting a tagged construct's activity without ever testing whether tag removal changes it.
:::

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 20 · Planning an expression and purification campaign"}
You have three shortlisted designs from a Part IV campaign: **D1** (210 aa, no Cys, predicted monomer), **D2** (340 aa, two predicted structural disulfides), **D3** (180 aa, very hydrophobic surface in the model, flagged as aggregation-prone). For each design, write an annotated SOP that takes it from sequence to purified protein, with explicit **decision points** and the *next action* at each branch:

- Reverse-translate and optimize each gene; state the tool, target CAI/GC window, and the motifs you blacklist.
- Choose a vector, tag placement (N vs. C), cleavable linker, and whether to start with a solubility fusion — justify per design (e.g., D2 needs disulfide-competent expression; D3 likely needs SUMO/MBP).

## Chapter Summary

- **20.1** Codon optimization is reverse-translation tuned to the host: target a sensible CAI/GC window, relax 5′ mRNA structure, and remove cloning sites and repeats — then order a sequence-verified synthetic gene.
- **20.2** Choose an expression vector (pET for *E. coli*) and a cloning method that gives scarless, sequenced junctions; add a His₆ tag with a cleavable linker, and consider a solubility fusion up front.
- **20.3** BL21(DE3) with T7/IPTG induction is the default; expressing at 16–18 °C is the single highest-yield change for a marginal design, and a small-scale soluble/insoluble check precedes any scale-up.
- **20.4** Escalate hosts deliberately — yeast for secretion and disulfides, cell-free for fast parallel screening, mammalian/insect only when the chemistry demands it.
- **20.5** IMAC captures the His-tagged protein; SEC polishes it and gives the first honest readout of folding and monodispersity (single symmetric monomer peak vs. void aggregate).
- **20.6** No-expression is usually a construct/sequence problem; insolubility is climbed with a cheapest-first ladder (temperature, IPTG, chaperones, fusions, disulfide strains, refolding).
