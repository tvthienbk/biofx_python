## 19.1 Composite Scoring

By the end of Chapter 18 each surviving design carries a handful of numbers — scRMSD, catalytic-residue pLDDT, catalytic-geometry RMSD, a ligand-pocket RMSD, an MD competent-frame fraction or NAC fraction, perhaps a docking score and a physics energy. None of these alone orders the designs well, so the task is to combine them into a single **composite score** that can rank the shortlist. Doing this defensibly is harder than it looks, because the metrics live on different scales, point in different directions, and are not equally trustworthy.

The first step is **normalization**. The metrics have incompatible units and ranges (an RMSD in ångströms, a pLDDT on 0–100, a fraction on 0–1) and opposite polarities (lower scRMSD is better; higher pLDDT is better). You must put them on a common, comparable scale before combining. Two standard choices are **min–max normalization** (rescale each metric to [0, 1] across the shortlist) and **z-scoring** (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation). For "lower-is-better" metrics, flip the sign or use (1 − normalized value) so that every normalized term points the same way: larger means better. Z-scoring is generally preferable when a metric has outliers, because min–max lets a single extreme design compress everyone else into a narrow band.

The second step is **weighting**, and this is where judgment enters. The weights should reflect how predictive each metric is (Chapter 17.5): the active-site metrics — catalytic-geometry RMSD and local catalytic pLDDT — and the MD stability/NAC terms deserve the most weight, because they probe catalysis most directly; scRMSD deserves modest weight as a gate that has already mostly done its job; global pLDDT deserves little or none. A defensible composite states its weights explicitly and justifies them, rather than burying equal weights in code and pretending the choice was neutral. Weighting is a scientific claim about what matters, and it should be written down as one.

A crucial discipline is to keep **hard filters separate from the soft composite**. Thresholds like scRMSD < 2 Å or geometry RMSD < 0.5 Å are pass/fail gates applied *before* scoring; the composite then ranks only the designs that already cleared every gate. Folding a hard requirement into a weighted sum lets a design compensate for a catastrophic geometry failure with a great pLDDT — exactly the wrong behavior. Gate first on the non-negotiables, then rank the survivors with the soft composite. Finally, report the composite alongside its components, never as a bare number: a reviewer (and your future self) must be able to see *why* a design ranked where it did.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 19.1 · Building a weighted composite score"}
**Problem.** Three designs survive all hard gates. Combine four metrics into a composite using z-scores and the weights w = {geometry RMSD 0.35, catalytic pLDDT 0.25, MD competent-frac 0.30, scRMSD 0.10}. Raw values:

| Design | geom RMSD (Å) | cat pLDDT | competent-frac | scRMSD (Å) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | 0.30 | 96 | 0.92 | 0.8 |
| D2 | 0.45 | 93 | 0.55 | 1.1 |
| D3 | 0.38 | 94 | 0.81 | 1.5 |

**Solution.** Z-score each metric across the three designs (mean, then (x − mean)/SD), flipping sign for lower-is-better metrics (geom RMSD, scRMSD) so larger = better.

*geom RMSD:* mean 0.377, SD 0.075 → raw z = (−0.30, +0.97, +0.04) after sign flip become (+0.97, −0.97, +0.04) for (D1, D2, D3) — D1 best (lowest RMSD).
*cat pLDDT:* mean 94.33, SD 1.53 → z = (+1.09, −0.87, −0.22).
*competent-frac:* mean 0.760, SD 0.193 → z = (+0.83, −1.09, +0.26).
*scRMSD:* mean 1.13, SD 0.35 → after sign flip z = (+0.94, +0.09, −1.06).

Weighted composite = 0.35·z_geom + 0.25·z_pLDDT + 0.30·z_frac + 0.10·z_scRMSD:

- **D1** = 0.35(0.97) + 0.25(1.09) + 0.30(0.83) + 0.10(0.94) = 0.340 + 0.273 + 0.249 + 0.094 = **+0.956**
- **D2** = 0.35(−0.97) + 0.25(−0.87) + 0.30(−1.09) + 0.10(0.09) = −0.340 − 0.218 − 0.327 + 0.009 = **−0.876**
- **D3** = 0.35(0.04) + 0.25(−0.22) + 0.30(0.26) + 0.10(−1.06) = 0.014 − 0.055 + 0.078 − 0.106 = **−0.069**

Ranking: **D1 ≫ D3 > D2.** D2 is dragged down chiefly by its poor MD competent-fraction (0.55) — exactly the catalysis-relevant term we weighted heavily — even though its geometry RMSD still passed the hard gate. This is the composite working as intended: it demotes a design that passed every threshold but is dynamically weak. Report the table, the weights, and the per-metric z-scores, not just the final scalar.
:::

## 19.2 Balancing Diversity and Confidence

If you simply sort by the composite and take the top 96, you will almost certainly fill the plate with near-clones of the one or two backbones the models happen to like best. That is a trap. The composite score reflects the *models' confidence*, and the models share systematic biases — they were trained on similar data and make correlated errors. A plate that is 96 variants of one backbone is a single bet: if that backbone has a flaw the models cannot see (a subtle expression problem, a docking artifact, a force-field idiosyncrasy), the entire experiment fails together. **Diversity** is the hedge against correlated model error.

The selection must therefore balance two objectives in tension. **Confidence** pulls toward the top-ranked designs — you want your best shots on the plate. **Diversity** pulls toward spreading across distinct backbones, sequence clusters, and active-site solutions — you want independent bets so that the campaign is robust to any single mode of failure. The right posture is not to maximize one at the expense of the other but to allocate the budget across both: a portion of the plate to your highest-confidence designs, and a portion deliberately spread across clusters even at some cost in rank.

The practical mechanism is **cluster-then-select**. Cluster the shortlist by sequence identity (MMseqs2 at, say, 70%) and by backbone similarity (TM-score), so that designs sharing a fold or sequence motif fall into the same group. Then select representatives across clusters rather than skimming the global top: take the best one or two designs from each of the largest clusters, then continue filling from the global ranking. A useful rule of thumb is a cap on per-backbone representation (e.g., no more than ~6–8 sequences from any single backbone), so no one backbone monopolizes the plate. The exact split is a judgment call, but the principle is firm: a diverse plate at slightly lower mean confidence beats a high-confidence monoculture, because it samples more independent hypotheses about how to catalyze the reaction.

::: {.conceptcheck data-title="Concept Check 19.1"}
Your composite ranking's top 96 designs come from only three backbones. A colleague says "great, the models are confident — ship it." Give the two-sentence argument for why this is risky and what you would do instead.
:::

## 19.3 Selecting the Experimental Set

Selection is where in-silico work meets a physical, finite resource: the plate. The **96-well mindset** is the discipline of treating that plate as a precious, fixed budget and asking what allocation of its wells maximizes what the campaign will *learn*, not just the chance of one hit. A 96-well plate (or a 384-well plate at higher throughput) is not 96 slots for your 96 favorite designs; it is an experiment whose composition you design.

![**Figure 19.1** The campaign funnel: from thousands of raw designs through the four-layer filter and MD to a ranked shortlist, then to a 96-well plate whose composition is deliberately allocated across designs, replicates, and controls.](assets/figures/campaign_funnel.png)

A well-designed plate allocates its wells across four categories. **Designs** — the diverse, high-composite candidates from Section 19.2 — are the bulk, but not all, of the plate. **Replicates** of a subset of designs let you estimate assay noise and distinguish a real weak signal from a measurement artifact; even a handful of replicated designs transforms your ability to interpret marginal results. **Positive controls** — a known active enzyme for the assay, or a previously validated design if you have one — confirm that the assay is working at all; a plate with no signal anywhere is uninterpretable without a positive control to tell you whether the assay or the designs failed. **Negative controls** — the catalytically dead mutants of Section 19.4 and empty-vector or buffer-only wells — establish the background against which "activity" is defined. A common, defensible layout reserves on the order of 70–80 wells for designs (with some replicated), and the remainder for the control suite.

The output of this section is concrete: a **plate map** — a table assigning every well a content (design ID, control type, or replicate), arranged so that controls are distributed across the plate rather than clustered in one corner (edge effects and pipetting gradients are real). The plate map is an experimental design document, and it should be built with the same care as the designs it carries. A brilliant set of designs in a plate with no controls and no replicates can produce a result that cannot be defended; a more modest set with a proper control suite produces a result you can stand behind.

## 19.4 Designing In-Silico Controls

A positive result is only as interpretable as its controls. If a designed enzyme shows activity, the immediate question a skeptic — and a reviewer — will ask is: *is the activity coming from the catalytic site you designed, or from something else* — a contaminating enzyme, the bulk protein surface, a metal in the buffer, nonspecific chemistry? The answer is a **catalytically dead mutant**: the same protein with the catalytic machinery deliberately broken, so that any residual signal reflects everything *except* the designed chemistry.

The canonical dead-mutant strategy is to **mutate the catalytic base (or nucleophile) to a chemically inert residue** — typically alanine (removing the side chain entirely) or, for a residue whose side-chain shape matters for folding, a near-isosteric but catalytically inert substitution (e.g., Asn for Asp, which preserves size and a polar character but cannot act as a general base). A His general base goes to Ala (or Asn/Gln); a Ser/Cys nucleophile goes to Ala; a catalytic Asp/Glu goes to Asn/Gln. The design intent is a **matched pair**: the active design and its dead mutant differ at one or two catalytic positions and are otherwise identical, so they fold the same, express the same, and bind the substrate the same — the *only* thing that changed is the catalysis. If the active design turns over substrate and the dead mutant does not, the activity is attributable to the designed site. If both are active, the "activity" was never your design.

The discipline is to design these controls *in silico, at the same time as the designs themselves*, not as an afterthought when a hit appears. Each dead mutant should itself pass the relevant Chapter 17 folding checks (you want it to fold like its parent, so that loss of activity reflects loss of chemistry, not loss of structure), and it occupies a well on the plate. Building dead-mutant pairs in from the start is what separates a campaign that can claim a designed catalyst from one that found a protein that does something for reasons unknown.

::: {.method data-title="Method 19.1 · Designing catalytically dead-mutant controls"}
1. **Identify the catalytic set** for each design to be controlled (the residues from the theozyme that perform the chemistry).
2. **Choose the inactivating mutation.** Mutate the key catalytic residue: general base His → Ala; nucleophile Ser/Cys → Ala; catalytic acid/base Asp/Glu → Asn/Gln (near-isosteric) or → Ala (clean knockout). Prefer the isosteric option when side-chain packing is structurally load-bearing.
3. **Keep the pair otherwise identical.** Change only the catalytic position(s); leave all other residues unchanged so the pair is matched.
4. **Refold the dead mutant** (Chapter 17): confirm scRMSD < 2 Å and that the *non-catalytic* structure is unchanged, so loss of activity will reflect lost chemistry, not lost fold.
5. **Pair on the plate.** Place each dead mutant in a defined well, ideally near (but not edge-clustered with) its active partner, and record the pairing in the plate map and notebook.
6. **Add complementary negatives:** empty-vector/no-protein wells and a substrate-only well to establish chemical background.
:::

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 19.1"}
- **No negative controls** — reporting "activity" with no dead mutant, so the signal cannot be attributed to the designed site.
- **An unmatched dead mutant** — a control that also disrupts folding, conflating loss of structure with loss of chemistry.
- **No positive control** — a flat plate that cannot distinguish "designs failed" from "assay failed."
- **No replicates** — a single-well measurement reported as a result, with no estimate of assay noise.
- **Unrecorded tool versions or seeds** — a ranking that cannot be reproduced, so the campaign cannot be defended or repeated.
- **Controls clustered in one plate corner** — edge effects masquerading as a control signal.
:::

## 19.5 Reproducibility and the Design Notebook

A computational campaign that cannot be reproduced is a campaign that cannot be defended, extended, or learned from. The randomness is real: diffusion backbone generation, ProteinMPNN sampling, and structure prediction all involve stochastic steps, so the *same* nominal protocol with a *different* seed yields different designs and different rankings. Reproducibility is therefore not a virtue you add at the end; it is **provenance** captured continuously as the campaign runs.

The provenance standard has three pillars. **Seeds** — record the random seed for every stochastic stage (backbone generation, sequence design, structure prediction, docking), so any design can be regenerated bit-for-bit. **Versions** — record the exact version of every tool and model weights (RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN/LigandMPNN, AlphaFold3/Boltz-2, OpenMM, the force fields), because a model update silently changes results; pin them in an environment file. **Lineage** — record, for each final design, its full ancestry: which backbone it came from, which theozyme, which sequence-design run, which refold, what scores it earned at each filter, and which threshold config and weight set produced its rank. Lineage is what lets you answer, six months later, "why is *this* sequence on *this* plate?" — and it is exactly what a reviewer or a "Learn"-phase post-mortem will demand.

The instrument that holds all of this is the **design notebook** — the computational analog of the wet-lab notebook, and a first-class deliverable of the campaign, not an afterthought. Concretely it combines: a **version-controlled repository** (git) holding the pipeline code and the threshold/weight **config files**, so every parameter choice is in the history; a pinned **environment specification** (conda/`environment.yml` or a container) so the software stack is reconstructable; **structured logs** that record seeds, versions, inputs, and outputs for every run; and a **per-design provenance record** carried in the data table from generation through to the plate. The notebook entry for the final plate should let a competent stranger regenerate the entire shortlist from the recorded seeds, versions, configs, and code. That is the operational meaning of "record everything," and it is what closes the Design–Build–Test–Learn loop: when the assay results come back, a campaign with complete provenance can ask *why* the hits won and the failures lost; a campaign without it can only shrug.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 19.1 · Provenance and seed tracking (version-stamped)"}
**git** — version-control the pipeline code *and* the config files (thresholds, weights); tag the commit used for the final plate.  
**conda / mamba** with a pinned `environment.yml`, or a **container** (Docker/Apptainer) image digest — reconstructable software stack.  
**Hydra** or **OmegaConf** (config-as-code) — every threshold and weight is a logged parameter, not a magic number.  
**Structured logging / experiment tracking** (e.g., MLflow or Weights & Biases, or plain JSON logs) — seeds, versions, inputs, outputs per run.  
**A per-design provenance table** (parquet/CSV) — backbone ID, theozyme, seed, tool versions, all filter scores, composite, rank, plate well.  
**A data-availability checklist** — designs, seeds, configs, and notebook archived with a DOI on release.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **19.1** A defensible composite score normalizes metrics (z-score or min–max, polarity-aligned), weights them by predictiveness, keeps hard gates separate from the soft rank, and is reported with its components.
- **19.2** Diversity hedges against correlated model error; balance confidence against diversity by clustering and selecting representatives rather than skimming the global top.
- **19.3** The 96-well mindset allocates a fixed plate across designs, replicates, positive controls, and negative controls, captured in a deliberate plate map.
- **19.4** Catalytically dead mutants — the catalytic base/nucleophile mutated to Ala or a near-isosteric inert residue, in a matched pair — make a positive result attributable to the designed site; design them in from the start.
- **19.5** Reproducibility is provenance captured continuously — seeds, versions, lineage — held in a design notebook (git, pinned environment, structured logs, per-design records) that lets a stranger regenerate the plate.
