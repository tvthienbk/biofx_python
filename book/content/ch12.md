## 12.1 Inputs to the Decision

Before choosing a model, write down four properties of the problem. They, not fashion, determine the stack.

**The reaction.** What bond is made or broken, and what is the transition state? A reaction with a clear, small catalytic set (a general-acid/base hydrolysis) is a different problem from a redox reaction needing a cofactor or a multi-step mechanism. The reaction also sets your *success metric* — turnover, rate enhancement, selectivity — which propagates back into what you must control during design (Chapter 13).

**Active-site complexity.** Count the mutually constrained catalytic residues and the interactions among them. Two or three groups whose *positions* define the chemistry point toward functional-group conditioning (RFD2). A microenvironment that hinges on typed hydrogen bonds, an oxyanion hole, or required burial points toward all-atom control (RFD3). A large, tightly coupled array points toward array scaffolding (Riff-Diff). This count, from Chapter 10, is the single most informative input.

**Cofactor or metal.** Does catalysis require a metal ion, a heme, a flavin, PLP, or a metal–organic cluster? Cofactors impose coordination geometry that the generator and the inverse-folding step must respect, and they demand a *ligand-aware* sequence designer (LigandMPNN) and a *ligand-aware* predictor (AF3, Chai-1, Boltz) for validation. A metal site also raises the catalytic-residue count and usually pushes toward RFD3 or Riff-Diff.

**Budget.** Compute, money, and wet-lab capacity are constraints, not afterthoughts. A campaign that can test 96 designs but only generate on free Colab GPUs is a different plan from one with an HPC allocation and a screening robot. Budget determines how wide you can generate and how deeply you can validate — and validation, as Section 12.4 shows, usually dominates the bill.

## 12.2 The Master Decision Flowchart

The flowchart in Figure 12.1 is the chapter's centerpiece. It takes the four inputs and routes them to a recommended generator and the supporting stack. Read it as a sequence of gates.

![**Figure 12.1** Master decision flowchart. Starting from the reaction and active-site specification, gates on active-site complexity, microenvironment requirements, cofactor/metal presence, and budget route the designer to a recommended generator (RFD2 / RFD3 / Riff-Diff / classic RFdiffusion) and the matching sequence-design and validation stack.](assets/figures/decision_flowchart.png)

The gates, in order:

1. **Is the active site essentially a small set of functional-group positions, with no critical typed-H-bond or burial requirement?** If yes, route to **RFdiffusion2** with LigandMPNN and a ligand-aware refold. This is the default for clean two- to four-group sites.
2. **Does the chemistry hinge on a microenvironment — a specific H-bond donor/acceptor pattern, an oxyanion hole, required burial, or explicit substrate placement?** If yes, route to **RFdiffusion3**, whose all-atom conditioning is the only one that can specify these, and exploit its speed to generate a larger pool.
3. **Is the defining difficulty a large, tightly coupled catalytic array (many interdependent residues, e.g., a metal site plus base plus oxyanion hole plus relay)?** If yes, route to **Riff-Diff** for array scaffolding.
4. **Is the target a structural motif, binder, symmetric assembly, or a case with no atom-level chemical specification?** Then classic **RFdiffusion** (Chapter 9) is still the right, lighter tool.
5. **Cofactor/metal present?** Regardless of generator, switch the sequence stage to **LigandMPNN** and the validation stage to a **ligand-aware predictor** (AF3 / Chai-1 / Boltz), and verify coordination geometry explicitly.
6. **Budget-constrained?** Take the closest route in Section 12.5 — the same logic on free/low-cost platforms with a smaller generated pool.

The flowchart is a starting recommendation, not a straitjacket. When the active-site complexity is borderline, the mature move is to run two routes in parallel (e.g., RFD2 and RFD3 as in Lab 10) and let the geometry metrics decide.

## 12.3 Composing Pipelines

A design campaign is a *pipeline*, and most failures are hand-off failures — a stage receiving input it cannot use, or losing information the next stage needs. The canonical enzyme pipeline is:

**theozyme → generator → sequence design → structure prediction (refold) → geometry/quality filter → shortlist → (synthesis).**

Each arrow is a hand-off with a contract:

- **theozyme → generator.** The generator needs the catalytic geometry in its expected format (functional groups for RFD2; typed H-bonds and burial flags for RFD3; the full array for Riff-Diff). Garbage geometry in, garbage backbones out — the theozyme is the single highest-leverage artifact in the campaign.
- **generator → sequence design.** Backbones (and, for RFD3, side-chain atoms and the placed ligand) pass to **ProteinMPNN** (apo) or **LigandMPNN** (with cofactor/substrate). Fix catalytic identities; sample several sequences per backbone (e.g., 8) at low temperature.
- **sequence design → prediction.** Each sequence is refolded by a predictor. For ligand/metal sites this *must* be ligand-aware (AF3, Chai-1, Boltz); for apo sites ESMFold or AF2 (initial-guess) suffice and are cheaper.
- **prediction → filter.** Compute global confidence (pLDDT/PAE), global Cα-RMSD to the design, and — decisively — **catalytic-atom RMSD**, H-bond geometry recovery, and burial. Filter on the *active-site* metrics, not just the global ones.
- **filter → shortlist.** Rank survivors by active-site fidelity and assemble a test set heading toward ~96 (Reality Check 12.1).

::: {.method data-title="Method 12.1 · Assembling an enzyme-design pipeline"}
1. **Fix the spec.** Write the four inputs (Section 12.1) and the success metric. Build and sanity-check the theozyme.
2. **Pick the generator** via the flowchart. Pin its version and set a seed.
3. **Size the generation batch** from the budget (Section 12.4): start with a pilot (e.g., 200) to confirm outputs look sane before scaling to the full pool (e.g., 1,000–5,000).
4. **Wire sequence design.** ProteinMPNN (apo) or LigandMPNN (cofactor/metal); fix catalytic residues; ~8 sequences/backbone at T ≈ 0.1.
5. **Wire prediction.** Ligand-aware (AF3/Chai-1/Boltz) if a cofactor/substrate matters, else ESMFold/AF2. This is usually the compute bottleneck — plan it explicitly.
6. **Define filters before running.** Set thresholds for global confidence, global RMSD, catalytic-atom RMSD, H-bond geometry, burial, and clashes. Record them.
7. **Run the pilot, inspect, then scale.** Verify each hand-off on the pilot; only then commit the full pool.
8. **Shortlist and record provenance.** Rank by active-site fidelity; save seeds, versions, theozyme, constraint files, and every threshold for the design notebook.
:::

## 12.4 Compute and Cost Planning

Turning a plan into GPU-hours and dollars is a skill reviewers and PIs both expect. The arithmetic is straightforward once you separate the three cost centers — **generation**, **sequence design**, and **validation refold** — and recognize that the last usually dominates.

Order-of-magnitude per-design costs on a modern data-center GPU (e.g., an A100/H100-class card), useful for planning rather than precise billing:

- **Generation.** Classic RFdiffusion / RFD2: roughly seconds to a couple of minutes per backbone depending on length and step count; RFD3 is ≈10× faster than RFD2. Call it ~0.5–2 GPU-minutes per design as a planning figure.
- **Sequence design.** ProteinMPNN/LigandMPNN is cheap — many sequences per second to a few seconds per backbone; effectively negligible against the other two.
- **Validation refold.** This is the expensive stage. A ligand-aware refold (AlphaFold3, Chai-1, Boltz) for a single sequence can take from under a minute to several minutes per prediction depending on size and MSA settings, and you refold *several sequences per backbone*. Multiplied across the pool, refolding routinely consumes the majority of the campaign's compute.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 12.1 · Costing a 1,000-design campaign"}
**Problem.** Plan a campaign that generates 1,000 backbones, designs 8 sequences each (8,000 sequences), and refolds every sequence with a ligand-aware predictor for validation. Estimate GPU-hours per stage and a dollar figure, assuming an A100 at ≈$2/GPU-hour (typical cloud spot/committed rate).

**Solution.** Take planning rates: generation ≈ 1 GPU-minute/backbone; sequence design ≈ negligible (say 0.05 GPU-minute/backbone); refold ≈ 1.5 GPU-minutes/sequence.

- *Generation:* 1,000 × 1 min = 1,000 GPU-min ≈ **16.7 GPU-h**.
- *Sequence design:* 1,000 × 0.05 min = 50 GPU-min ≈ **0.8 GPU-h**.
- *Validation refold:* 8,000 × 1.5 min = 12,000 GPU-min ≈ **200 GPU-h**.

Total ≈ **217.5 GPU-h**. At $2/GPU-h that is ≈ **$435**. The dominant term is refolding at ~92% of the compute — generation is under 8% and sequence design is a rounding error. Two leverage points follow: (1) *filter before you refold* — discard low-quality backbones with cheap checks (clash, radius of gyration, a fast apo predictor) so you refold fewer than 8,000 sequences; (2) refold fewer sequences per backbone (e.g., the top 2–3) and only escalate to the expensive ligand-aware predictor for backbones that pass a cheaper screen. Either change cuts the bill far more than swapping generators. Note also that using RFD3 (≈10× faster generation) saves only ~15 GPU-h here — real, but small against the refold term.
:::

The planning discipline is therefore: estimate each stage separately, identify the dominant term (almost always validation), and spend your optimization effort there. A faster generator is nice; a *smaller, smarter refold set* is decisive.

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 12.1 · The validation bottleneck is the real cost"}
The seductive part of a campaign is generation — it is fast, cheap, and produces pretty backbones. The expensive, rate-limiting part is **validation**: ligand-aware refolding (AlphaFold3 / Boltz / Chai-1) of every candidate sequence, which routinely consumes the large majority of GPU-hours and wall-clock time, as Worked Example 12.1 shows. Two consequences. First, *budget around the predictor, not the generator* — that is where the money goes. Second, *the cheapest GPU-hour is the refold you do not run*: every cheap upstream filter that removes a hopeless backbone before refolding saves real money. And none of this in-silico spend substitutes for the wet lab — you still synthesize and assay on the order of ~96 designs to find robust activity (Chapters 1 and 10).
:::

## 12.5 Resource-Limited Paths

A reader with no GPU cluster can still run the full framework; the routes are just narrower. Three platform tiers make this practical.

**Free (Colab and notebooks).** Google Colab provides free GPU sessions adequate for piloting: a small RFdiffusion or RFD2 batch, ProteinMPNN/LigandMPNN sequence design, and ESMFold or ColabFold (AF2) refolds. Community notebooks for each of these tools exist and are the standard on-ramp. The limits are session timeouts, modest VRAM, and queueing — fine for tens of designs and learning the pipeline, not for thousands.

**Low-cost managed platforms.** Web platforms such as **Tamarind** and **Neurosnap** expose protein-design and structure-prediction tools (RFdiffusion family, ProteinMPNN/LigandMPNN, AlphaFold, Boltz, and others) through a browser with pay-as-you-go or subscription pricing, removing the need to install or maintain a GPU environment. They are ideal for groups without infrastructure and for occasional campaigns; the trade-offs are per-run cost, version control that you do not fully own, and data residing on a third-party service — so record exactly which version a run used and prefer them for convenience rather than as the irreproducible core of a published result (Chapter 11).

**Spot/committed cloud GPUs.** For a real campaign without an HPC allocation, on-demand or spot A100/H100 instances from a cloud provider give full control at the per-hour rates used in Worked Example 12.1. Use spot pricing for fault-tolerant batch generation and reserved/committed instances for the refold stage you cannot afford to lose to a preemption.

The resource-limited recipe is the same logic at smaller scale: pilot free on Colab, validate the pipeline end-to-end on tens of designs, then either scale on a managed platform or rent cloud GPUs for the (refold-dominated) full run — always filtering hard before the expensive validation step.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 12.1 · Cloud and low-resource platforms"}
**Google Colab** — free/low-cost notebook GPUs; community notebooks for RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN/LigandMPNN, ColabFold/ESMFold. Best for piloting tens of designs.  
**Tamarind Bio** — browser-based access to the RFdiffusion family, MPNN variants, AlphaFold, Boltz, and more; pay-as-you-go; no local install.  
**Neurosnap** — managed web platform exposing protein-design and structure-prediction tools through a browser with subscription/credit pricing.  
**Cloud GPUs (A100/H100)** — on-demand or spot instances (~$1–3/GPU-h typical); full control for refold-heavy full runs; use spot for generation, committed for validation.  
For any platform, record the tool version/commit and the run parameters; reproducibility is the user's responsibility, not the platform's.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **12.1** Four inputs determine the stack: the reaction, active-site complexity, cofactor/metal presence, and budget; complexity is the most informative.
- **12.2** The master flowchart routes those inputs through gates (functional-group placement → RFD2; microenvironment → RFD3; coupled array → Riff-Diff; structural/binder → classic RFdiffusion; cofactor → ligand-aware MPNN and predictor) to a recommended stack.
- **12.3** A campaign is a pipeline of contracted hand-offs (theozyme → generator → MPNN → predictor → filter → shortlist); filter on active-site metrics, not just global ones.
- **12.4** Cost each stage separately; validation refolding almost always dominates (≈90%+), so optimize the refold set, not the generator.
- **12.5** Free (Colab) and managed (Tamarind, Neurosnap) platforms plus spot/committed cloud GPUs make the framework runnable at any budget; pilot free, filter hard, then scale.
