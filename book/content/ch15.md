## 15.1 Running RFD2/RFD3/Riff-Diff at Scale

The theozyme of Chapter 14 is a geometry; a scaffold generator turns it into protein backbones. The defining shift of the modern era is that we no longer *search* a database of natural folds for one that happens to accommodate the motif (the RosettaMatch era) — we *generate* backbones built around the motif from noise. The three tools this book uses are **RFdiffusion2 (RFD2)**, **RFdiffusion3 (RFD3)**, and **Riff-Diff**, and all three solve the same problem: given the ligand/transition-state model and the atomized catalytic motif, produce backbones that present those catalytic atoms to the ligand in the theozyme geometry.

The mechanics are the diffusion process of Part III. Each tool starts from a cloud of random residue frames and iteratively **denoises** toward a plausible backbone, conditioned at every step on the fixed motif (the catalytic residues and the ligand). RFD2 and RFD3 do this with an **atomized-motif** representation — the catalytic side chains are represented at the atomic level and held fixed while the surrounding backbone is generated around them, which is exactly what catalysis needs because it is *side-chain functional groups*, not whole residues, whose geometry must be preserved. Riff-Diff takes the complementary path of assembling a consistent rotamer ensemble (the "riff") and diffusing scaffolds to support it. RFD3 generalizes and unifies the conditioning of its predecessor with broader ligand and small-molecule support. In practice you will run at least two generators on the same theozyme, because their inductive biases differ and their unions are more diverse than either alone.

"At scale" is not rhetorical. A backbone generator run produces, per the parameters you set, a backbone every few seconds to a minute or two on a single modern GPU (an A100 or H100 generates a ~150-residue backbone in seconds to tens of seconds at typical step counts). A campaign generates **hundreds to thousands** of backbones, so a run is a batch job: a loop over output indices, each writing a backbone PDB, distributed across GPUs by a scheduler. The unit of work is the *library*, not the design. You will set a target library size (say 1,000), a per-GPU throughput, and a wall-clock budget, then launch the batch and let it run — orchestration, not babysitting, is the skill (Tool Box 15.1).

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 15.1 · Batch orchestration (version-stamped)"}
**RFdiffusion2 / RFdiffusion3** (RosettaCommons / Institute for Protein Design, 2024–2025) — atomized-motif backbone generation around ligands; PyTorch + GPU. Driven by a config (Hydra/YAML) plus an output prefix and a count.
**Riff-Diff** (Höcker lab, 2024) — theozyme/rotamer-ensemble-driven scaffold assembly.
**SLURM** (23.x) — HPC scheduler; use a **job array** (`#SBATCH --array=0-999%50`) so each task generates one backbone (or a small block) and the cluster runs 50 concurrently.
**Snakemake** (v8) or **Nextflow** (24.x) — workflow managers that express the whole funnel (generate → motif-check → predict → filter) as a DAG with checkpointing and re-entrancy, so a crashed node resumes without regenerating everything.
**GNU parallel** / a simple `for` loop — adequate for a single multi-GPU node.
Always log the seed, config hash, and tool version per output so a backbone is reproducible.
:::

## 15.2 Key Parameters and Failure Signatures

A handful of parameters dominate the outcome, and each has a recognizable **failure signature** — a way the output looks wrong that points back at the setting that caused it. Learning to read these signatures is the difference between a productive batch and a thousand useless PDBs.

The **number of denoising steps** trades quality for speed. Too few steps (aggressively reduced timestep schedules) produces backbones with broken geometry, unclosed loops, and clashes; too many wastes compute for marginal gains. The **length / length range** sets the residue count of the generated chain; a length too short to wrap a pocket around the ligand yields backbones where the motif sits on the surface rather than in a cavity, and a length too long yields sprawling, poorly packed folds with the active site exposed. The **motif / contig specification** — how you declare which residues are the fixed catalytic motif and how much flexible backbone surrounds them — is the most consequential input: errors here produce backbones that ignore or distort the motif. The **noise scale** (and any sampling-temperature analog) controls diversity versus regularity: low noise gives clean, idealized, but repetitive backbones; high noise gives diverse but increasingly unphysical ones. The **guidance / potentials** (where the tool supports them) bias generation toward desired properties — burial of the ligand, a closed pocket, secondary-structure content — at the risk of over-constraining.

The most important failure signatures to recognize: **the exposed motif** (the catalytic residues end up solvent-exposed rather than buried in a pocket — usually too-short length, weak burial guidance, or a motif spec that did not request contacts); **the clashing or broken backbone** (chain breaks, non-physical bond lengths, steric clashes — too few steps or too-high noise); **mode collapse** (hundreds of near-identical backbones — too-low noise/temperature, over-tight guidance); and **the satisfied-but-unfoldable backbone** (the geometry is perfect but the topology is one no sequence will fold — caught downstream by structure prediction in Chapter 16, but often foreshadowed by unusual secondary-structure patterns or implausibly long unsupported loops). None of these is a reason to despair; each is a reason to adjust one parameter and relaunch.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 15.1 · Interpreting a failed-scaffold output"}
**Problem.** A 1,000-backbone RFD2 run completes. Inspection shows: (a) ~30% have the catalytic glutamate `OE2` more than 6 Å from any backbone burial and pointing into solvent; (b) ~10% have a chain break between residues 60 and 70; (c) the remaining backbones cluster into only three near-identical topologies. Diagnose each and prescribe one parameter change apiece.

**Solution.**
*(a) Exposed motif.* The base is on the surface, so the pocket never closed around the ligand. Likely causes: the chain length is too short to wrap the substrate, or the motif/contig spec did not request ligand burial. Fix: increase the length (and length range), and add/strengthen a burial guidance potential or specify additional motif–ligand contacts. Re-run a small pilot (50 backbones) before the full batch.
*(b) Broken backbone.* Chain breaks signal under-converged denoising. Fix: increase the number of denoising steps; if it persists, lower the noise scale slightly.
*(c) Mode collapse.* Three topologies from 1,000 attempts means the sampler is not exploring. Fix: raise the noise scale / sampling temperature, vary the length across the batch, and seed each output independently. Diversity is the product (§15.3), so this is the most damaging of the three even though it produced "clean" structures.

The discipline: diagnose from the signature, change *one* parameter, pilot before committing the full GPU budget.
:::

## 15.3 Generating Diversity (Hundreds to Thousands)

The campaign's entire statistical logic rests on **diversity**. Recall the hit-rate landscape of Chapter 1: experimental success rates of 1–5% are normal, and the *in silico* raw success rate before filtering is far lower. You cannot improve a single design into activity by staring at it; you improve your *odds* by generating a large, varied library and letting the downstream filters enrich it. This is why the chapter's epigraph is "generate widely; judge ruthlessly," and why optimizing one beautiful backbone is a Red Flag rather than a virtue.

Diversity has two axes, and a good library spans both. **Geometric diversity** is variation in how the backbone presents the motif — different pocket shapes, different burial depths, different approaches of secondary structure to the ligand — so that the eventual sequence-design and folding filters have many independent chances to find a combination that works. **Topological diversity** is variation in the fold itself — α-helical bundles, αβ folds with a central sheet, TIM-barrel-like (β/α)₈ arrangements — because some folds present a catalytic pocket far more naturally than others, and you rarely know in advance which one your theozyme prefers. A library of 1,000 backbones that collapses into three topologies (Worked Example 15.1c) is, for the purposes of the campaign, a library of three.

The practical lever for diversity is varying the generation conditions across the batch: sweep the length over a range, sweep the noise scale across a few values, use independent random seeds per output, and run more than one generator. The payoff is a funnel that survives attrition: if 1,000 diverse backbones become 200 after motif triage, 60 after structure-prediction agreement, and a handful after the full filter stack, you still have candidates to synthesize — whereas a library of 30 over-optimized backbones can be emptied by a single filter. Generate widely now so that you can afford to judge ruthlessly later.

::: {.method data-title="Method 15.1 · A 1,000-backbone generation run"}
1. **Freeze the theozyme inputs**: the ligand/TS model and the atomized motif config exported in §14.5. Verify the round-trip distances one last time.
2. **Pilot small**: generate 25–50 backbones at your default parameters. Inspect for the four failure signatures (§15.2). Adjust *one* parameter at a time until the pilot is clean and the motif is buried.
3. **Plan the sweep for diversity**: choose 3–4 length values, 2–3 noise scales, and run ≥ 2 generators (e.g., RFD2 and RFD3). Allocate output counts so the total reaches ~1,000.
4. **Submit as a job array**: one SLURM array task per output (or per small block), `--array=0-999%50` to cap concurrency; log seed, config hash, tool, and version per backbone.
5. **Monitor throughput, not individual jobs**: track completion count and GPU utilization; let failed tasks auto-resume (Snakemake/Nextflow re-entrancy) rather than restarting the batch.
6. **Collect and index**: gather all backbone PDBs into a library directory with a manifest (index → parameters/seed). The library, not any single backbone, is the deliverable that enters §15.5 triage.
:::

## 15.4 Topology Conditioning for Catalysis-Friendly Folds

Not all folds are equally hospitable to catalysis. Some topologies present a deep, enclosed, preorganizable pocket almost for free; others bury their cores so completely that no cavity remains, or expose every loop to solvent. **Topology conditioning** is the practice of biasing generation toward folds that make a good active site likely, rather than accepting whatever the unconditioned model produces.

The empirical lesson from natural enzymes is instructive. The **(β/α)₈ TIM barrel** is nature's most common enzyme fold precisely because its eight parallel β-strands form a closed central stave whose C-terminal ends converge on a funnel-shaped pocket — a ready-made, electrostatically tunable active site. **Rossmann-like αβ folds** present cofactor- and substrate-binding clefts at the C-terminal edge of a central sheet. **α-helical bundles** can host pockets between helices and are easy to generate and fold, though their pockets are often shallower. A catalysis-friendly fold is one with (i) a concave, enclosable pocket of the right size, (ii) enough secondary structure converging on that pocket to position multiple catalytic residues from different elements (which preorganizes geometry), and (iii) a buried-but-accessible active site with a substrate channel.

You condition toward these in two ways. The direct route is **secondary-structure or topology specification** where the generator supports it: declaring the desired fraction and arrangement of helices and strands, or providing a coarse topological scaffold (a "fold class") as a conditioning input, so the diffusion process targets that family. The indirect route is **selection after generation**: generate broadly, then assign secondary structure (DSSP) and a fold classification to each backbone, and keep those whose topology and pocket geometry match catalysis-friendly criteria. In a real campaign you do both — gently bias generation toward enclosable αβ or barrel-like folds while still sampling broadly, then enrich for catalysis-friendly topologies in triage. The caution mirrors §15.2: over-tight topology conditioning collapses diversity, so condition with a light hand.

::: {.conceptcheck data-title="Concept Check 15.1"}
Your theozyme requires three catalytic groups contributed from three different secondary-structure elements converging on one pocket. Explain why a TIM-barrel-like topology might satisfy this more reliably than a single long α-helical hairpin, and why over-specifying the fold could still hurt your campaign.
:::

## 15.5 Triage by Motif Fidelity

A 1,000-backbone library is raw material; triage turns it into a *ranked* shortlist before the more expensive filters of Chapters 16–18. The first and most important triage criterion is **motif fidelity**: how faithfully does the generated backbone reproduce the theozyme geometry it was supposed to host? A backbone that drifted from the motif during denoising has, by definition, lost the catalytic geometry, and no downstream step will restore it.

The core metric is the **motif RMSD** — the root-mean-square deviation of the catalytic motif atoms (and, where relevant, the ligand contact atoms) in the generated backbone against the theozyme reference, computed after superposition on the motif. A defensible triage threshold for a first pass is **motif RMSD < 1.0 Å** (tighten to < 0.5 Å for a stringent pass); above ~1.5–2.0 Å the catalytic geometry is effectively gone. Motif RMSD alone is necessary but not sufficient, so triage on a small set of complementary checks: the **catalytic-atom contacts** (re-measure the key distances and angles from the theozyme — base `O`···`H`, donor···phenolate, stacking distance — against their tolerances from §14.4); **pocket burial / solvent accessibility** of the catalytic groups (the motif should be in a cavity, not on the surface — this catches the exposed-motif failure); and **basic backbone sanity** (no chain breaks, reasonable radius of gyration, plausible secondary-structure content).

Rank the surviving backbones — by motif RMSD primarily, with the contact and burial checks as gates — and record the triage in a log: for each backbone, its parameters/seed, its motif RMSD, its contact-satisfaction flags, and its pass/fail decision. This log *is* the go/no-go gate for the scaffold stage (Method 13.1): if fewer than the pre-committed number of backbones (e.g., ≥ 200 below 1.0 Å motif RMSD) survive, the gate fails and you adjust generation rather than advancing a thin library to sequence design. Keeping the funnel honest at this gate is what prevents a campaign from spending its sequence-design and structure-prediction budget on backbones that never held the theozyme in the first place.

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 15 · Generate and triage a backbone library"}
Generate a 500–1,000-backbone library for your capstone theozyme and triage it by motif fidelity. Run on an HPC cluster or cloud GPU.

1. **Freeze inputs and pilot** (Method 15.1, steps 1–2): verify the exported theozyme round-trips, then generate 25–50 backbones and inspect for the four failure signatures (§15.2). Adjust one parameter at a time until the pilot is clean and the motif is buried.
2. **Plan for diversity** (§15.3): sweep 3–4 lengths and 2–3 noise scales across ≥ 2 generators (e.g., RFD2 and RFD3), independent seeds per output.
3. **Submit the batch** as a SLURM job array (or Snakemake/Nextflow DAG), capping concurrency; log seed, config hash, and version per backbone.
4. **Triage** (§15.5): for each backbone compute motif RMSD, re-measure the key catalytic distances/angles against tolerance, and check pocket burial and backbone sanity.
5. **Rank and gate**: rank by motif RMSD with contact/burial as gates; apply the scaffold-stage go/no-go criterion (e.g., ≥ 200 backbones below 1.0 Å motif RMSD). If the gate fails, adjust generation rather than advancing.

**Platform:** HPC / cloud (RFD2/RFD3/Riff-Diff + SLURM/Snakemake) · **Deliverable:** a ranked backbone library with a triage log (per backbone: parameters/seed, motif RMSD, contact flags, pass/fail).
:::

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 15.1 · Optimizing one design instead of sampling many"}
- Generating a handful of backbones, picking the "best-looking" one, and pouring all downstream effort into it — the campaign's statistics depend on library size, not on any single design.
- Reporting a library size without reporting topological diversity (1,000 backbones in three folds is three folds).
- Skipping motif-fidelity triage and advancing every backbone, so sequence design wastes its budget on scaffolds that lost the catalytic geometry.
- Tuning generation parameters to make one favorite backbone better, instead of improving the yield of the whole batch.
- Over-tight topology or noise settings that produce clean but near-identical backbones (mode collapse), mistaking regularity for quality.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **15.1** Modern generators (RFD2, RFD3, Riff-Diff) build backbones around an atomized catalytic motif by denoising from noise; campaigns run them as batch jobs producing hundreds to thousands of backbones, not single designs.
- **15.2** A few parameters dominate (steps, length, motif/contig spec, noise, guidance), each with a recognizable failure signature — exposed motif, broken backbone, mode collapse, unfoldable topology — that points back at the setting to change.
- **15.3** Diversity, geometric and topological, is the campaign's statistical engine; vary length, noise, seeds, and generators so the funnel survives attrition.
- **15.4** Condition topology toward catalysis-friendly folds (enclosable αβ/TIM-barrel-like pockets with converging secondary structure) by specification and by post-hoc selection — with a light hand, to avoid collapsing diversity.
- **15.5** Triage by motif fidelity (motif RMSD < 1.0 Å, contact-satisfaction, pocket burial); the triage log is the scaffold-stage go/no-go gate.
