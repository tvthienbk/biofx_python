## 7.1 Residue-Level versus Atom-Level Representations

Before a model can generate or score a protein, it must encode one as numbers. The choice of encoding is not a technical detail downstream of the science — it *is* the science, because it fixes the ceiling on what the model can ever say. A model that does not represent the atom you care about cannot reason about it, cannot place it, and cannot optimize against it. This is the whole chapter in one sentence.

The first widely successful representation in deep protein design was **residue-level**. Each amino acid is reduced to a small handful of backbone atoms — typically the N, Cα, and C — and the side chain is collapsed to, at most, a Cβ direction or a coarse identity label. The protein becomes a chain of *residue frames*: one local coordinate system per residue, carrying a position and an orientation. AlphaFold2's structure module, the original RFdiffusion, and ProteinMPNN all live in this world. Its advantage is efficiency and abstraction: backbone geometry is exactly what determines a fold, and a residue-frame model can generate and evaluate folds with remarkable fidelity while ignoring the combinatorial mess of side-chain atoms.

The price is precisely the thing an enzyme designer needs. Catalysis is an *atomic* phenomenon. A hydrogen bond is a relationship between a specific donor atom and a specific acceptor atom at a specific distance and angle. A metal coordination bond is a relationship between a metal ion and a particular side-chain nitrogen or oxygen. The orientation of a catalytic histidine's Nε2 versus Nδ1, the rotamer of a serine's Oγ, the distance from a substrate's carbonyl carbon to the attacking nucleophile — none of these exist in a model whose vocabulary is "residue frame plus identity label." A residue-level model can build you a beautiful pocket-shaped hole; it cannot natively guarantee that the catalytic oxygen points at the substrate.

An **all-atom** representation encodes every heavy atom — backbone *and* side chain, and critically the atoms of bound ligands, metals, and cofactors — as explicit, typed nodes in three-dimensional space. This is heavier and harder to learn, but it is the representation in which a catalytic geometry can be *stated and enforced*. The 2024–2025 generation of generative models (AlphaFold3, RFdiffusion3, the LigandMPNN line) moved decisively toward all-atom precisely so that the active site — the part of the protein that does the work — could finally be part of the model's vocabulary.

## 7.2 SE(3) Frames and Equivariance

A protein has no privileged orientation in space. Rotate the entire molecule, slide it three feet to the left, and it is the same protein with the same energy and the same function. Any sensible model must build this invariance in, because the alternative — learning separately that every rotated copy is "the same" — wastes capacity and generalizes badly. The mathematics of rigid motions in three dimensions (rotations plus translations) is the group **SE(3)**, and the design principle is **equivariance**: when the input is rotated and translated, the output transforms the same way, in lockstep.

Two words are worth keeping straight. A quantity is **invariant** if it does not change when you move the molecule — a bond *length* or a bond *angle* is invariant; rotate the protein and the distance between two atoms is unchanged. A quantity is **equivariant** if it moves *with* the molecule — atomic *coordinates* and force *vectors* are equivariant; rotate the protein 90° and every coordinate rotates 90° too. A generative model that outputs coordinates must be equivariant; a scoring model that outputs an energy should be invariant. Getting this wrong shows up as a model that gives different answers for the same molecule in a different pose.

For the designer, the payoff of equivariance is intensely practical and can be stated without a single tensor. *It means you can specify a catalytic geometry once, in whatever frame is convenient, and the model will honor it no matter how the rest of the protein is oriented.* When you tell an SE(3)-equivariant generator "place these three functional groups in this relative arrangement around the substrate," the relative arrangement is what is enforced; the absolute orientation in the global coordinate system is free, exactly as physics demands. Equivariance is the reason you constrain *relationships between atoms* — distances, angles, dihedrals — rather than absolute positions, and the reason those constraints transfer cleanly from the theozyme (Chapter 5) into the generator. The residue-frame representation of 7.1 is itself an SE(3) construction: each frame is a local rotation-plus-translation, and the network's job is to predict frames *relative to* one another.

## 7.3 Encoding Ligands, Metals, and Cofactors

A residue-level model has no slot for anything that is not an amino acid. That is the representational fact behind a blunt limitation: enzymes act on molecules, and a model that cannot see the molecule is designing a pocket blind. All-atom models add the missing vocabulary by **tokenizing** non-protein chemistry into the same coordinate stream as the protein.

A small-molecule **ligand** is supplied as a molecular graph — atoms, elements, bonds, formal charges — typically from a **SMILES** string or a Chemical Component Dictionary (**CCD**) code, then embedded as a set of typed atom nodes with three-dimensional positions the model can place or be conditioned on. A **metal ion** (Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe, Mn²⁺, Cu) is a single charged atom node, but the chemistry that matters is its *coordination*: which protein atoms lie within bonding distance, in what geometry (tetrahedral, octahedral), and with what coordination number. The representation must therefore support metal–protein edges, not just the lone ion. A **cofactor** — heme, PLP, NAD(P), FAD/FMN — is a large organic molecule (sometimes covalently linked to the protein, as PLP is via a Schiff base to a lysine), encoded as its own atom graph and, where relevant, with explicit covalent linkage to a specified residue.

The representational lesson is that each of these must be a *first-class citizen* of the model, not an afterthought bolted on after the protein is built. A model that designs the backbone first and only later tries to dock a ligand has already committed to a pocket that may not fit the chemistry. The point of all-atom conditioning is to generate the protein *in the presence of* the ligand, metal, and cofactor, so that the active site and the thing it acts on are co-designed.

::: {.bench data-title="From the Bench 7.1 · The atom that wasn't there"}
A cautionary pattern: a designed metalloenzyme co-folded confidently and the catalytic His residues sat at sensible distances from the modeled Zn²⁺ — but the model's representation had no explicit slot for the **catalytic water** that bridges the metal and the substrate in the real mechanism. The water is part of the chemistry and absent from the representation, so the design optimized a geometry that omitted the nucleophile that actually does the work. The bench lesson generalizes: before trusting a representation, list every atom in your mechanism — including ordered waters, protons whose position defines a pK_a, and the lone pairs that orient a hydrogen bond — and ask which ones the model can and cannot see. The atoms it cannot see, it cannot optimize.
:::

## 7.4 Encoding Catalytic Constraints

A theozyme (Chapter 5) is an idealized arrangement of functional groups around a transition state. To hand it to a generative model, you must translate that geometry into a **machine-readable constraint** — a statement of relationships between atoms that the model can enforce or score against. The form of the constraint is dictated by the representation: a residue-level model can only accept residue-level constraints; an atom-level model can accept atom-level ones.

The catalytic relationships worth encoding fall into a small set. A **distance** constraint fixes the separation of two atoms within a tolerance — a hydrogen bond's heavy-atom donor–acceptor distance of about 2.7–3.2 Å, or a metal–ligand bond near 2.0–2.3 Å. An **angle** constraint fixes the geometry of three atoms — the donor–hydrogen–acceptor angle of a good hydrogen bond is near linear (≳ 150°); the angle of nucleophilic attack on a carbonyl carbon approaches the Bürgi–Dunitz value of ~107°. A **dihedral** constraint fixes the four-atom torsion that orients, say, a planar guanidinium against an oxyanion. **Identity** constraints fix which residue type (and sometimes which rotamer) occupies a position. And **burial / solvent-accessibility** constraints state that a catalytic group must be desolvated — a buried general base behaves very differently from a surface-exposed one, because burial shifts its pK_a and removes competing water.

These are not abstractions; they are the literal contents of the constraint files that drive the tools of Part III. The crucial move for an enzyme designer is to express each constraint at the *finest level the model supports*. "Put a histidine near position X" is a residue-level constraint a coarse model can take; "place the Nε2 of a histidine 2.9 Å from the substrate carbonyl oxygen, with the N–H···O angle ≥ 150°, and bury the imidazole below 10% solvent accessibility" is the atom-level constraint that actually specifies catalysis — and it can only be honored by a model whose representation contains those atoms.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 7.1 · Writing an atom-level hydrogen-bond constraint"}
**Problem.** Your theozyme requires the backbone amide of a designed residue to donate a hydrogen bond to the substrate's carbonyl oxygen, stabilizing the developing oxyanion in the transition state. Express this as an atom-level constraint a generator can enforce.

**Solution.** Name the atoms explicitly and constrain the geometry of the donor–acceptor pair:

- **Donor atom:** backbone amide nitrogen N of the design residue (and its attached hydrogen H).
- **Acceptor atom:** carbonyl oxygen O of the substrate (a ligand atom — so this constraint *requires* an all-atom representation that contains the ligand).
- **Distance:** N···O between 2.7 and 3.2 Å (heavy-atom separation of a moderate-to-strong hydrogen bond).
- **Angle:** N–H···O angle ≥ 150° (near-linear, the hallmark of a productive H-bond rather than an incidental contact).
- **Optional dihedral / approach:** H···O=C angle near 120° so the donor approaches the oxygen's lone-pair direction.

Note what just happened. The acceptor is a *ligand* atom, so a residue-level model — which has no ligand and only a Cα/N/C backbone — literally cannot represent the acceptor or the bond. The same theozyme is *expressible* at the atom level and *inexpressible* at the residue level. The representation, not the geometry, is the limiting factor.
:::

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 7.1 · Constraint file formats"}
**Rosetta enzyme-design constraint (`.cst`)** — the classic enzyme-design format: `AtomPair`, `Angle`, and `Dihedral` blocks with target value, tolerance, and a penalty function; the `CST::BEGIN/END` "enzdes" block links a ligand atom to protein atoms.  
**RFdiffusion contig + potentials** — backbone-level conditioning: `contigmap.contigs` to fix/scaffold motifs and external `potentials` to bias inter-residue geometry.  
**RFdiffusion2 / RFdiffusion3 atom/functional-group conditioning** — specify the catalytic side-chain atoms (and, in RFD3, full all-atom context including ligand) the backbone must be built around — the representational advance of §7.5.  
**LigandMPNN inputs** — a parsed ligand (from PDB/SDF/SMILES) plus `fixed_residues` to pin catalytic positions during sequence design (Chapter 8).  
Always state, per constraint, the atom names, target value, tolerance, and the representation level it assumes.
:::

## 7.5 Why Representation Choice Drives Capability

Everything in this chapter converges on a single claim that explains the shape of Part III: *the major leaps in enzyme design capability have been leaps in representation, not merely in model size or data*. When the field's generators saw proteins only as residue frames, they were superb at making novel folds and stable binders — and structurally incapable of being told "build a backbone that holds these three side-chain atoms in this catalytic arrangement around this substrate." The limitation was not a lack of cleverness; it was a vocabulary that contained no side-chain atoms and no ligand.

This is exactly why **RFdiffusion2 (RFD2)** mattered for catalysis. Its central advance was **functional-group conditioning**: the designer specifies the catalytic side-chain atoms — the actual chemical groups of the active site — and the model generates a backbone *around* them, without having to pre-commit to which residue indices or rotamers will carry them. Suddenly a theozyme could be the input rather than something you hoped to graft in afterward. **RFdiffusion3 (RFD3)** pushed further to **all-atom conditioning**, bringing the full atomic context — side chains *and* the ligand, metal, and cofactor — into the generative process, so the protein is built in the presence of the very molecule it must act on. The reason these tools "unlock" enzyme design, in the language of this chapter, is that they expanded the representation to contain the atoms catalysis is made of. The geometry was always the same; the model finally had words for it.

The designer's takeaway is a habit of mind. Before adopting any tool, ask the representational question first: *what atoms and relationships can this model see?* If your mechanism depends on a metal coordination geometry and the tool represents only backbone frames, no amount of sampling will rescue you — you are asking for something the model cannot express. Match the representation to the chemistry before you match the model to the budget. That discipline is what carries you into Part III.

::: {.studio data-title="Studio Exercise 7 · One theozyme, two representations"}
Take a published or instructor-provided theozyme for a simple reaction (e.g., an ester hydrolysis with a Ser–His–Asp-like triad and an oxyanion hole, or a metal-assisted hydrolysis). Translate it into **two** constraint specifications:

1. A **residue-level** specification — fix residue *types* and approximate Cα–Cα relationships only. Note explicitly every catalytic relationship you are *forced to drop* because the representation has no atoms for it (side-chain orientation, donor–acceptor distance/angle, metal coordination, the substrate itself).
2. An **atom-level** specification — name the donor/acceptor and metal-coordinating atoms, give distances (H-bond 2.7–3.2 Å; metal–ligand ~2.0–2.3 Å), angles (H-bond ≥ 150°; Bürgi–Dunitz ~107°), and burial requirements, and include the substrate atoms as first-class nodes.

**Platform:** text editor + a constraint format from Tool Box 7.1 (Rosetta `.cst` recommended) · **Deliverable:** both constraint files, with a one-page commentary stating, line by line, what each representation *preserves* and what it *cannot express* — and a closing paragraph on which generator (residue-level vs. RFD2/RFD3 all-atom) each file is fit to drive.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **7.1** Residue-level representations encode the backbone as frames and ignore side-chain and ligand atoms; all-atom representations encode every heavy atom, and only the latter can express a catalytic geometry.
- **7.2** SE(3)-equivariance means a model's output transforms in lockstep with rotations and translations of the input; for the designer it means catalytic geometry is specified as *relationships between atoms* and honored regardless of global orientation.
- **7.3** Ligands (SMILES/CCD graphs), metals (charged nodes with coordination edges), and cofactors (atom graphs, sometimes covalently linked) must be first-class citizens so the protein is co-designed with the molecule it acts on.
- **7.4** A theozyme becomes machine-readable as distance, angle, dihedral, identity, and burial constraints; express each at the finest level the representation supports.
- **7.5** The capability leaps of enzyme design were representational: RFD2's functional-group conditioning and RFD3's all-atom conditioning expanded the model's vocabulary to contain the atoms catalysis is made of.
