## 23.1 X-ray and Cryo-EM for Top Hits

Solution biophysics (Chapter 21) establishes that a design is folded and of the right size and shape; it cannot resolve where individual atoms sit. For a validated catalytic hit, **atomic-resolution structure determination** is the decisive test of whether the active site is arranged as designed. Two techniques dominate.

**X-ray crystallography** remains the workhorse for the small (10–40 kDa), well-folded, monodisperse proteins typical of de novo designs. It requires growing a **crystal** — usually by vapor diffusion (hanging or sitting drop) against a sparse-matrix screen of precipitants, salts, and pH, optimizing the conditions that yield diffracting crystals. A diffraction dataset collected at a synchrotron (or a good home source) is phased — by molecular replacement using the design model itself as the search template (a major advantage: the model is usually close enough to phase the data), or experimentally (SeMet/SAD) when MR fails — then the atomic model is built and refined against the data. Resolution of ~1.5–2.5 Å is typical and ample to place catalytic side chains and bound ligands. The bottleneck is crystallization: many well-behaved designs simply do not crystallize, and de novo proteins with idealized, low-complexity surfaces can be either unusually easy or stubbornly hard to crystallize.

**Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM)** is the alternative when crystals do not form, and is increasingly capable at small sizes. Historically limited to large complexes, single-particle cryo-EM now reaches near-atomic resolution for assemblies and, with modern detectors, for proteins below ~50 kDa — though the smallest monomeric de novo enzymes (often <40 kDa) remain challenging without a rigid fiducial or oligomerization. Cryo-EM needs no crystal, works on near-native solution samples vitrified on a grid, and is the method of choice for designed assemblies, membrane-associated designs, and any hit that resists crystallization. For most single-domain de novo enzymes, attempt crystallography first; escalate to cryo-EM (or engineer an assembly/fiducial) when crystals fail.

## 23.2 Comparing Experimental Structure to Design Model and Theozyme

Once you have an experimental structure, validation is a *quantitative* comparison against two references: the **design model** (the full predicted backbone and sequence) and the **theozyme** (the idealized catalytic-residue geometry the design was built to reproduce — Part I). The central methodological point is that *overall fit and active-site fit are different measurements, and the active site is the one that matters for catalysis.*

The standard global metric is the **backbone RMSD** (root-mean-square deviation) of Cα (or all backbone) atoms after optimal superposition of the experimental structure onto the design model. A low backbone RMSD — say **< 1.0–1.5 Å over the ordered core** — means the design folded into the intended topology. This is the headline number most design papers report, and a sub-angstrom-to-low-angstrom backbone RMSD is a genuine achievement. But it is not sufficient: catalysis lives in the relative geometry of a handful of side chains, and a near-perfect backbone can host a catalytic constellation that is rotated, shifted, or differently rotamer-packed enough to abolish the chemistry.

The decisive metric is therefore the **catalytic side-chain RMSD** (or, more precisely, the deviation of the *functional-atom geometry* — the nucleophile Oγ, the general-base Nε, the oxyanion-hole donors, a metal site — from the theozyme). You superpose on the backbone, then measure how far the catalytic functional atoms have moved relative to the designed/theozyme arrangement. Sub-angstrom catalytic-atom agreement means the active site was built as intended; a backbone RMSD of 0.8 Å paired with a catalytic-atom RMSD of 3 Å tells you the *fold* succeeded but the *active site* did not — and explains a weak or absent activity that the backbone number alone would have left mysterious. Always report both, and report the active-site number as the one that bears on the mechanism. Where a substrate analog or transition-state mimic can be soaked or co-crystallized, a liganded structure showing the substrate poised against the catalytic atoms in the designed orientation is the strongest possible structural validation.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 23.1 · Backbone vs. catalytic side-chain RMSD"}
**Problem.** A designed retro-aldolase crystallizes at 1.9 Å. Superposing the experimental structure on the design model over all ordered Cα atoms gives **backbone RMSD = 0.7 Å**. The theozyme placed a catalytic lysine (the nucleophile that forms the Schiff base) and a water-positioning network. Measuring the catalytic Lys Nζ and the two supporting residues' functional atoms against the theozyme, after backbone superposition, gives a **catalytic-atom RMSD = 2.9 Å**, with the Lys side chain pointing away from the modeled pocket. The measured kcat/KM is barely above background. Reconcile the numbers.

**Solution.** The two RMSDs tell a coherent story. **Backbone RMSD 0.7 Å** means the design folded essentially perfectly into the intended scaffold — the *as-designed structure* claim (in the sense of fold/topology) is fully supported. But **catalytic-atom RMSD 2.9 Å** means the catalytic constellation is *not* arranged as the theozyme prescribed: the nucleophilic lysine adopts a different rotamer and points away from where a substrate would sit, so the Schiff-base chemistry the design depends on cannot proceed efficiently. The barely-above-background activity is exactly what this geometry predicts. **Diagnosis:** correct fold, broken active site — most likely a rotamer/packing problem around the catalytic Lys, not a topology failure. **The headline 0.7 Å backbone RMSD, reported alone, would have been actively misleading.** This is precisely why both numbers, with the active-site number foregrounded, must appear in any validation. The redesign action (23.5): re-pack the residues surrounding the catalytic Lys to fix its rotamer, or reposition the theozyme constraint, then re-test.
:::

## 23.3 Why the Field Now Demands Structures and Dead-Mutant Controls

De novo enzyme design has matured into a field with explicit *evidentiary standards*, and they exist because the failure modes are real and have repeatedly produced published claims that did not hold up. Two requirements now define a credible report.

First, a **dead-mutant control** (Chapter 22): the claim that catalysis comes from the designed active site is only credible if knocking out the proposed catalytic residue(s) abolishes the activity. Without it, a measured rate could be — and historically often was — a co-purifying host enzyme, an adsorption artifact, or a stoichiometric reaction. Reviewers and the community now treat the absence of a dead-mutant control as disqualifying for a *de novo catalysis* claim, not optional.

Second, an **experimental structure** of at least the top hit(s), with the active site compared quantitatively to the design and theozyme (23.2). A predicted model — even a confident AlphaFold or design-pipeline model — is a hypothesis about atomic arrangement, not evidence of it; the active-site geometry that catalysis depends on is exactly the feature predictions are least reliable about (side-chain rotamers and sub-angstrom placements), and exactly the feature a structure resolves.

This is not abstract. Several high-profile de novo design papers in the recent literature were publicly criticized — in correspondence, in review, and on community forums — for reporting designed catalysts *without* a dead-mutant control, without an experimental structure, or without distinguishing the design's activity from contaminating background. The reproducibility concerns that followed reshaped expectations: the strongest 2025 work (the de novo serine hydrolases of Lauko et al. and de novo metallohydrolases) now ships with crystal structures showing the catalytic machinery in place, dead-mutant controls knocking activity to background, and substrate/intermediate evidence tying the rate to the designed mechanism. The way to avoid the criticized pattern is to *build these requirements into the campaign from the start* — design the dead mutant alongside the active design, plan crystallization for any confirmed hit, and never let a kinetic number stand alone.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 23.1 · Refinement and overlay tools"}
**Phenix** (v1.21) — crystallographic and cryo-EM model building, refinement (`phenix.refine`, real-space refinement), and validation (MolProbity built in); also molecular replacement (`Phaser`).
**Coot** (v0.9/1.x) — interactive model building and rebuilding into electron/Coulomb density; rotamer and Ramachandran tools for fixing the very side chains that matter for catalysis.
**CCP4 suite** — `Aimless`/`Pointless` (data processing), `Phaser` (MR), `REFMAC5` (refinement), `Superpose`/`gesamt` (structural alignment).
**PyMOL** (v3.x) — `align` and `super` for backbone superposition and RMSD; `pair_fit` for measuring catalytic-atom deviations against a theozyme; publication overlays.
**ChimeraX** (v1.8) — `matchmaker` superposition, per-residue RMSD, and excellent active-site visualization.
**MolProbity** — geometry/clash validation; report it for any deposited structure.
:::

## 23.4 Diagnosing Failure from Structure

A structure is most valuable when a design *fails* or underperforms, because it converts a mysterious negative result into a specific, actionable diagnosis. The recurring failure modes each leave a structural signature.

**Correct fold, displaced active site.** The backbone matches (low RMSD) but the catalytic atoms deviate from the theozyme (Worked Example 23.1). Cause: rotamer or local-packing errors around the catalytic residues; the most common and most fixable failure. Action: re-pack the active-site neighborhood, re-impose the theozyme constraint, possibly add a residue to lock the catalytic rotamer.

**Wrong fold or partial fold.** High backbone RMSD, register shift, a strand that became a loop, a helix that frayed. The protein folded into something other than the design. Action: revisit backbone designability and sequence design; this is a generation/sequence problem, not an active-site tweak.

**Occluded or collapsed pocket.** The fold and even the catalytic residues are roughly right, but a side chain or a flexible loop collapses into the pocket, or the cavity the design assumed is filled by ordered water or a crystallization additive. The transition state cannot bind. Action: redesign the pocket lining, add steric definition, or reconsider the cavity solvation the model ignored.

**Misincorporated metal or cofactor / unexpected ligand.** A metallohydrolase that bound the wrong metal, mis-coordinated geometry, or a bound buffer component occupying the active site. Action: revisit metal-site design and assay conditions.

**Crystallographic caveats.** Disordered (unmodeled) active-site loops, crystal-packing contacts distorting the site, or alternate conformations — these can mimic a failure or, conversely, hide a real one. Always check the **electron density** for the catalytic residues directly (is the side chain actually resolved, or modeled into weak density?), the B-factors, and whether a packing contact touches the active site. A diagnosis is only as good as the local map quality supporting it.

## 23.5 Feeding the Redesign Loop

Structure determination is where the Design–Build–Test–Learn cycle (Chapter 1) closes and the **Learn** stage produces its most precise output. Each structural diagnosis maps to a concrete next design move, and the value of a failed design is exactly this transferable knowledge.

A displaced catalytic rotamer says *constrain that rotamer harder* — tighten the theozyme geometric constraint, re-pack neighbors, or add an anchoring contact — and re-generate sequences for the same backbone. A collapsed pocket says *the scoring undervalued cavity definition* — a lesson that should propagate to the filtering criteria (Chapter 17) for the whole campaign, not just one design. A wrong fold says the problem is upstream in backbone generation or sequence design, redirecting effort there. Systematically logging *predicted vs. observed* active-site geometries across many designs is how a group builds the empirical correction that no single design provides: it reveals which theozyme geometries are reliably realized and which the current tools systematically distort, sharpening every future campaign. The redesign loop also naturally hands off to directed evolution — a structurally validated but weak hit, with its mechanism confirmed and its deficient geometry identified, is an excellent starting point for the local optimization that finishes most designed enzymes (Part VI). The honest, structure-grounded negative result is not the end of a design; it is the input to the next one.

::: {.method data-title="Method 23.1 · A design-validation checklist"}
1. **Confirm catalysis with the full control suite first** (Chapter 22). Do not pursue a structure for an artifact. Require: rate > background, dead mutant and empty-vector mock at background, turnover > 1.
2. **Determine the structure.** Crystallize (vapor diffusion, sparse-matrix screen → optimize); collect and process diffraction; phase by molecular replacement using the design model; build and refine in Coot/Phenix. Escalate to cryo-EM if crystals fail.
3. **Validate the structure's geometry.** Run MolProbity; check Ramachandran, clashscore, rotamer outliers, and the *local electron density of the catalytic residues* specifically.
4. **Superpose and measure backbone RMSD.** Align experimental onto design model (PyMOL `super`/ChimeraX `matchmaker`); report Cα/backbone RMSD over the ordered core.
5. **Measure catalytic-atom RMSD against the theozyme.** After backbone superposition, measure deviations of the functional atoms (nucleophile, general base, oxyanion donors, metal site) from the designed/theozyme geometry. Report this as the primary validation number.
6. **Inspect ligand/intermediate (if available).** For a soaked/co-crystallized substrate analog or covalent intermediate, confirm it sits against the catalytic atoms in the designed orientation.
7. **Render a diagnosis.** Classify as as-designed, correct-fold/displaced-site, wrong-fold, occluded-pocket, or cofactor problem (23.4), supported by the map.
8. **Feed the redesign loop.** Translate the diagnosis into a specific design action and update campaign-wide filtering criteria.
9. **Deposit and report.** Deposit coordinates and data (PDB/EMDB); report both RMSDs, the dead-mutant control, and the structural diagnosis.
:::

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 23.1 · Why predicted ≠ deposited"}
It is tempting to treat a high-confidence predicted model as a stand-in for a structure. The track record says otherwise for the feature that matters. Backbone topology is now predicted very well, and backbone RMSDs between prediction and crystal structure are often impressively low. But the *catalytic side-chain geometry* — rotamers, sub-angstrom functional-atom placement, the exact pocket shape — is precisely where predictions and design models diverge from experiment, and it is precisely what determines catalysis. A model can be "right" at the fold level and wrong by several angstroms at the catalytic atoms (Worked Example 23.1), which is the difference between an enzyme and an inert protein. This is not a knock on the predictors; it is a statement about which scale of error catalysis is sensitive to. The deposited structure exists to resolve exactly the scale the prediction cannot guarantee.
:::

::: {.ethics data-title="Ethics & Responsibility 23.1 · Publishing controls and structures — the community standard"}
De novo enzyme design carries an outsized responsibility to its evidentiary standards because a designed catalyst is an extraordinary claim, easily mimicked by mundane artifacts (host enzymes, adsorption, stoichiometric reactions). The community standard that has emerged — and that you should hold yourself to regardless of whether a given venue enforces it — has three pillars. **(1) Report the dead-mutant control.** A catalysis claim without it is not publishable on its merits; the inactive mutant is the experiment that distinguishes your design from a contaminant. **(2) Provide an experimental structure for top hits**, with the active site quantitatively compared to the design and theozyme, and deposit the coordinates and primary data so others can check the map for themselves. **(3) Report honestly and completely**: state how many designs were tested, not just the successes; report kcat/KM with controls and the background it is measured against; do not present a model as if it were a structure; and disclose the contaminant controls you ran. The recent, well-publicized criticism of designed-enzyme papers for omitting dead-mutant controls or experimental structures is a cautionary example, not of bad faith, but of how easy it is to over-claim in a field where the artifacts are convincing. The corrective is cultural and individual: build the controls and the structure into the campaign plan, and let the negative results — the inactive mutants, the failed designs, the diagnosed-from-structure failures — be part of the published record. A field that publishes its controls and its structures is a field whose claims can be trusted and built upon; that trust is the shared resource this standard protects.
:::

You are given (as coordinate files) the **experimental structure** of a validated de novo enzyme hit, its **design model**, and the **theozyme** (idealized catalytic-residue geometry). Using PyMOL or ChimeraX, produce a validation report that:

- Superposes the experimental structure onto the design model over the ordered core and reports the **backbone (Cα) RMSD**.
- After backbone superposition, measures and reports the **catalytic functional-atom RMSD** of each catalytic residue against the theozyme (use `pair_fit`/per-atom distances); identify the worst-deviating catalytic atom.
- Inspects the local geometry (rotamers, any clash) of the catalytic residues and, if a ligand is present, whether it sits as designed.
- Renders an explicit **diagnosis** (as-designed / correct-fold-displaced-site / wrong-fold / occluded pocket / cofactor problem) consistent with both RMSDs.
- Translates the diagnosis into a **specific redesign action** and one campaign-wide change to the filtering criteria.

**Platform:** PyMOL / ChimeraX (structural superposition and analysis) · **Deliverable:** a validation report with both RMSDs, an annotated active-site overlay figure, a diagnosis, and a redesign recommendation.

## Chapter Summary

- **23.1** For validated hits, X-ray crystallography (the workhorse for small de novo proteins, often phased by MR with the design model) or cryo-EM (when crystals fail or for assemblies) provides the atomic-resolution test of the active site.
- **23.2** Validation is quantitative: report **backbone RMSD** (fold success) *and*, decisively, the **catalytic side-chain / functional-atom RMSD** against the theozyme (active-site success) — the latter is what bears on catalysis.
- **23.3** The field now demands a dead-mutant control and an experimental structure for credible de novo catalysis claims, in direct response to high-profile reports criticized for omitting them; build both into the campaign from the start.
- **23.4** Structure converts failure into diagnosis: correct-fold/displaced-site, wrong fold, occluded pocket, or cofactor problem — each with a distinct signature and a distinct fix, read against the local electron density.
- **23.5** The structural diagnosis is the Learn stage's output: it maps to a specific redesign move, updates campaign-wide filters, and provides an ideal starting point for directed-evolution optimization.

## Problem Set

**Review.**

1. When would you choose cryo-EM over X-ray crystallography for a de novo enzyme hit, and what is the trade-off?
2. Why can the design model itself usually be used to phase a crystallographic dataset by molecular replacement?
3. Distinguish backbone RMSD from catalytic side-chain RMSD, and explain why the latter is the decisive validation number.
4. State the two evidentiary requirements the field now demands for a de novo catalysis claim, and what each establishes.
5. List three structural failure modes and the redesign action each implies.
6. Why must you inspect the *local electron density* of catalytic residues before trusting a structural diagnosis?

**Quantitative & Computational.**

1. A design gives backbone Cα RMSD = 0.9 Å but catalytic functional-atom RMSD = 2.6 Å to the theozyme. Interpret the pair and state the most likely failure mode.
2. Two structures of 180 residues each are superposed; the per-residue Cα deviations are mostly ~0.5 Å except a 12-residue loop deviating ~4 Å. Estimate the effect on the global RMSD of including vs. excluding the loop (treat the rest as uniform 0.5 Å) and explain why core-only RMSD is often reported.
3. A catalytic histidine's Nε is 3.1 Å from its theozyme position. If the substrate's reactive carbon must sit within ~3.5 Å of Nε for proton abstraction, argue quantitatively whether this deviation is likely catalytically tolerable.
4. You collect data to 2.1 Å with an Rfree of 0.24 and Rwork of 0.20. Comment on whether these statistics support trusting the catalytic-residue placement, and what else you would check.
5. Across 20 designs, predicted-vs-crystal backbone RMSD averages 0.8 Å (SD 0.3) but catalytic-atom RMSD averages 1.9 Å (SD 1.1). What does the disparity in means and spreads tell you about where the design pipeline is reliable and where it is not?

**Challenge.**

1. Design a complete validation and reporting plan for a de novo metallohydrolase you intend to publish, such that no reviewer can fault it for missing controls or structural evidence. Specify every control, every structural measurement, what you deposit, and how you report failures.
2. Given a structure showing the correct fold (backbone RMSD 0.6 Å) but a catalytic dyad in which one residue is in the designed rotamer and the other is flipped ~150°, and a measured activity ~30-fold over background, construct a diagnosis and a two-round redesign-and-test plan, stating the falsifiable prediction your redesign makes.
