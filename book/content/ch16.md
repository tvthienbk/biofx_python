## 16.1 LigandMPNN Around Substrate, Metal, and Cofactor

A generated backbone (Chapter 15) is a string of α-carbons and a fold; it has no amino-acid identities. **Inverse folding** is the task of assigning a sequence that will fold to that backbone, and the field's workhorse is **ProteinMPNN** (Dauparas et al., 2022) — a message-passing graph neural network that reads the backbone geometry and predicts, residue by residue, the amino acids most likely to encode it. ProteinMPNN raised the experimental success of sequence design dramatically over physics-based packing (Rosetta), and it is fast: a few hundred residues in well under a second on a GPU.

For enzyme design, ProteinMPNN has one disabling blind spot: it sees only the protein backbone, not the **ligand**. An active site is defined by the substrate, the transition state, and any metal or cofactor sitting in it, and a model blind to those will happily assign a residue that clashes with the ligand or that ignores a coordinating metal. **LigandMPNN** (Dauparas et al., 2023) closes the gap: it augments the ProteinMPNN architecture with explicit awareness of non-protein atoms — small-molecule ligands, metals, nucleic acids, cofactors — so that the sequence it designs is conditioned on the chemistry in the pocket. When it places a residue lining the active site, it "knows" the substrate (or transition-state model) is there, and it places residues whose side chains are compatible with, and complementary to, that ligand. For metalloenzymes it conditions on the metal so that the coordinating residues (His/Asp/Glu/Cys, the geometry of Chapter 12) are chosen to satisfy the coordination sphere. This ligand-awareness is precisely what makes LigandMPNN, rather than vanilla ProteinMPNN, the correct tool for the design campaign.

In practice you run LigandMPNN on each surviving backbone from §15.5 together with the ligand/TS model placed in the theozyme geometry. The model emits sequences (with per-residue confidences and a sequence-recovery/score readout) that you then validate by structure prediction. The key conceptual point: LigandMPNN designs the *rest* of the protein to be compatible with an active site you have already specified — it is the carpentry that frames the blueprint, not the architect of the catalysis.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 16.1 · LigandMPNN (version-stamped)"}
**LigandMPNN** (Dauparas et al., 2023; actively maintained 2024–2025) — ligand-, metal-, nucleic-acid-aware inverse folding; PyTorch. Key arguments: `--pdb_path` (backbone + ligand), `--fixed_residues` (catalytic/second-shell positions to hold), `--out_folder`, `--number_of_batches` / `--batch_size` (sequences per backbone), `--temperature` (sampling diversity, typically 0.1–0.3), and ligand/membrane/soluble model checkpoints.

**Soluble-model checkpoints** — LigandMPNN ships variants biased away from surface hydrophobics for soluble (non-membrane) targets; use the soluble checkpoint for cytoplasmic enzymes.
Report the model, checkpoint, temperature, fixed-residue set, and sequences-per-backbone.
:::

## 16.2 Fixing Catalytic and Second-Shell Residues

The chapter's epigraph states the central constraint: make the protein foldable, soluble, and expressible *without moving a single catalytic atom*. LigandMPNN, left to itself, optimizes for foldability and ligand compatibility — it does not know which residues you intend to *be* the catalysis. If it is free to redesign every position, it may swap your general-base glutamate for an aspartate that scores better for folding, or replace the π-stacking tryptophan with a phenylalanine, subtly changing the geometry that the entire theozyme was built to enforce. The fix is to **fix** those positions: declare the catalytic residues as immutable, so the model designs around them.

The **catalytic residues** are non-negotiable: the general base, the H-bond donor, the metal ligands, the π-stacking aromatic — every functional group that appeared in the theozyme (Chapter 14). You pass these as a fixed-residue list (LigandMPNN's `--fixed_residues`), and the model holds both their identity and (since they come from the backbone-plus-rotamer input) their geometry while designing the remaining positions.

But fixing the first shell alone is often insufficient, because the catalytic residues' *positions and pK_a values* depend on their neighbors. The **second shell** — the residues that contact, orient, and electrostatically tune the catalytic side chains — does much of the quiet work of catalysis: a hydrogen bond that holds the base in its productive rotamer, a buried environment that raises the base's pK_a, a residue that completes the oxyanion-stabilizing network. If LigandMPNN is free to redesign the second shell arbitrarily, it can disturb the first shell indirectly even though the catalytic residues themselves are fixed. The defensible practice is to fix the first shell rigidly and to *constrain* the second shell — either by fixing the most geometrically critical second-shell positions too, or by designing them but then checking explicitly that the first-shell geometry survived (§16.5). How much of the second shell to fix is a judgment call: fix too little and the catalytic geometry drifts; fix too much and you starve the model of the freedom it needs to make a foldable, soluble protein. Start by fixing the first shell plus the handful of second-shell residues that directly hydrogen-bond or pack against it, and let design optimize the rest.

::: {.method data-title="Method 16.1 · LigandMPNN with fixed residues"}
1. **Assemble the input PDB**: the triaged backbone (§15.5) with the ligand/TS model placed in theozyme geometry, and the catalytic side chains present as fixed rotamers. Verify atom names and the ligand parameterization.
2. **Define the fixed-residue set**: list the first-shell catalytic residues (base, donor, metal ligands, π-stacking aromatic). Add the geometrically critical second-shell residues that pack against or hydrogen-bond the first shell.
3. **Choose the checkpoint**: the soluble model for a cytoplasmic enzyme; the ligand model for the active site.
4. **Set sampling**: temperature 0.1–0.3 (lower = more conservative, higher = more diverse), and the sequences-per-backbone count (§16.3) via batches × batch size.
5. **Run** LigandMPNN with `--fixed_residues` and the ligand context; collect sequences with per-residue confidences and the overall score.
6. **Predict structure** of each sequence (AlphaFold2/3, ESMFold, or Boltz) in single-sequence mode; compute backbone RMSD to the target and, critically, the **catalytic-residue RMSD** (§16.5).
7. **Filter and flag**: discard sequences whose predicted fold disagrees with the backbone or whose catalytic residues drifted beyond tolerance; record solubility/expression heuristics (§16.4) per surviving sequence.
:::

## 16.3 Sequences-per-Backbone Strategy

Inverse folding is stochastic: each call to LigandMPNN samples a *distribution* over sequences for the same backbone, and they differ. A single sequence per backbone wastes the diversity the model offers; thousands per backbone wastes compute and downstream validation. The **sequences-per-backbone budget** is the parameter that balances these, and it should be set deliberately rather than by default.

The reasoning runs through the funnel. Each backbone is one geometric chance at the active site; each sequence is one chance that *that geometry can be encoded by a foldable, soluble protein*. Because most sequences will fail the structure-prediction agreement check or a solubility heuristic, you design several per backbone so that at least one good sequence survives per promising backbone. A common, defensible range is **8 to 48 sequences per backbone**: 8 for a large backbone library where breadth across backbones matters more than depth per backbone, 48 (or more) when the backbone library is small and you want to exhaust each backbone's sequence space. The temperature interacts with this: at low temperature (0.1) the samples are similar and you need fewer; at higher temperature (0.2–0.3) they diversify and more samples are worthwhile.

The strategic question is how to *spend* a fixed total sequence-design budget — say 10,000 sequences. Breadth (more backbones, fewer sequences each) hedges against backbone-level failure: if a backbone is fundamentally unfoldable, no number of sequences rescues it. Depth (fewer backbones, more sequences each) hedges against sequence-level failure on backbones you already trust. The principled allocation tracks the funnel: spend breadth early (a triaged library of hundreds of backbones at ~8 sequences each), then, after structure-prediction triage identifies the most foldable backbones, spend depth on those survivors (re-design at 48+ sequences each). This two-pass strategy concentrates compute where it converts to candidates, and it keeps the campaign's "generate widely, judge ruthlessly" logic intact at the sequence level.

::: {.bench data-title="From the Bench 16.1 · Sequence features that predict soluble expression"}
A computationally perfect design that does not express is a non-result, and the wet lab pays for it. A handful of sequence features predict soluble expression well enough to use as filters *before* ordering genes:

- **Net charge.** Strongly net-neutral proteins near their pI aggregate; a modest net charge (often slightly negative for *E. coli* cytoplasm) improves solubility. Compute the theoretical pI and net charge at the expression pH.
- **GRAVY (grand average of hydropathy).** The mean Kyte–Doolittle hydrophobicity; high (positive) GRAVY flags an over-hydrophobic surface prone to aggregation. Soluble cytoplasmic enzymes typically sit negative.
- **Hydrophobic-patch / surface exposure.** Large contiguous exposed hydrophobic patches drive aggregation even at acceptable GRAVY; the LigandMPNN soluble checkpoint helps avoid them.
- **Low-complexity / disorder regions.** Long low-complexity stretches and predicted disorder correlate with poor expression and degradation.
- **CamSol (Sormanni et al., 2015).** A sequence-based intrinsic-solubility score (per-residue and whole-protein) that is fast and predictive; rank designs by CamSol and deprioritize the low scorers.

Use these as a *triage*, not a verdict — they are heuristics, and a borderline score is a reason to include a control, not to discard a promising catalytic design outright.
:::

## 16.4 Solubility and Expression Heuristics

Sections 16.1–16.3 produce sequences that should fold to the target backbone; this section asks whether they can be *made*. The gap between a confident structure prediction and a band on a gel is where many campaigns quietly lose half their candidates, so solubility and expression heuristics belong in the design loop, not as an afterthought when the protein refuses to express.

Two levers act before any sequence leaves the computer. First, **the design model itself** can be biased toward solubility: LigandMPNN's soluble checkpoint avoids placing hydrophobic residues on the surface, which is the single most common cause of designed-protein aggregation. Using it for a cytoplasmic enzyme is nearly free insurance. Second, **post-hoc sequence scoring** with the features of From the Bench 16.1 — net charge and pI, GRAVY, hydrophobic-patch exposure, low-complexity content, and a CamSol intrinsic-solubility score — ranks the surviving sequences so that, among several that fold equally well, you advance the ones most likely to express solubly.

Expression adds organism-specific considerations that are cheap to handle. **Codon optimization** for the expression host (*E. coli* most often) avoids rare-codon stalls; this is a back-translation step applied to the chosen protein sequences before gene synthesis and does not change the protein. **Affinity tags and cleavage sites** (a His₆ tag, often with a TEV or SUMO fusion) are appended for purification; a solubility-enhancing fusion (SUMO, MBP, Thioredoxin) can rescue an otherwise insoluble design, at the cost of an extra cleavage step. These are Build-stage decisions (Part V), but they should be anticipated here so that the sequence you score is close to the sequence you will actually express, and so that a borderline-solubility design is paired with a solubility fusion rather than discarded.

The honest framing matches the rest of the book: these heuristics improve the odds, they do not guarantee expression. A design that passes every solubility filter can still be insoluble, and a design that fails one can still express beautifully. Their value is statistical — applied across a library, they shift the expressed fraction upward, which is exactly the leverage the campaign needs.

## 16.5 Guarding Against Catalytic Drift

The most dangerous failure in sequence design is the quiet one. A design can fold confidently, score well for solubility, and express perfectly — and still be inactive because a catalytic atom moved. **Catalytic drift** is the displacement of a catalytic side chain (or the ligand/metal) from its theozyme geometry, and because it can happen even when the catalytic residue's *identity* was fixed, it slips past every check that does not specifically look for it.

Drift arises by several routes. The obvious one is **identity drift** — the catalytic residue was *not* fixed and the model substituted a different amino acid; this is caught simply by comparing the designed sequence to the intended catalytic identities at the fixed positions. The subtler and more common one is **geometric drift**: the catalytic residue's identity is correct, but in the *predicted structure of the designed sequence*, its side chain adopts a different rotamer or sits in a slightly shifted backbone, so the functional atom is no longer at the theozyme distance and angle. This happens when the surrounding (second-shell) sequence, redesigned freely, no longer holds the catalytic residue in its productive conformation — exactly the indirect disturbance §16.2 warned about.

The guard is to measure drift explicitly, on the *predicted holo structure* of each designed sequence (not on the backbone you handed to LigandMPNN). For each design: predict the structure (ideally a ligand-aware predictor — AlphaFold3 or Boltz — so the holo geometry is modeled), superpose on the theozyme motif, and compute the **catalytic-residue RMSD** of the functional atoms (base oxygen, donor heteroatom, metal ligands, ring centroid) against the theozyme reference. Then re-measure the key catalytic distances and angles against their tolerances from §14.4. A design passes only if every functional atom is within tolerance (a useful default: catalytic-atom RMSD < ~1.0 Å and all theozyme distances within their stated ranges). Designs that drift are flagged and either discarded or sent back for re-design with more second-shell residues fixed. This drift check is the sequence-stage go/no-go gate (Method 13.1): it is the last purely computational defense of the catalytic geometry before structure prediction and the full filter stack, and it is the cheapest place to catch a protein that would fold but never turn over.

::: {.conceptcheck data-title="Concept Check 16.1"}
A designed sequence folds to the target backbone with high pLDDT and passes every solubility heuristic, yet you suspect it will be inactive. What single measurement, on what structure, would most directly confirm or rule out catalytic drift — and why is the input backbone the wrong structure to measure it on?
:::

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 16.1 · Detecting a drifted catalytic residue"}
**Problem.** You design 24 sequences for one Kemp-eliminase backbone. The general base was fixed as Glu at position 102. For sequence #14, AlphaFold3 (with the substrate) predicts the fold at high confidence, and the protein scores well for solubility. Determine whether the catalysis is preserved.

**Solution.**
1. **Check identity.** Position 102 is Glu in sequence #14 — identity drift ruled out (as expected, since it was fixed).
2. **Predict the holo structure** of sequence #14 *with the ligand* (AlphaFold3/Boltz), not the input backbone.
3. **Superpose** the predicted structure on the theozyme motif (align on the invariant backbone/motif atoms).
4. **Measure the catalytic geometry.** In the prediction, Glu102 `OE2` is 3.9 Å from the substrate C3 proton position and the `OE2···H–C3` angle is 138°. The theozyme requires 2.7 ± 0.3 Å and 165 ± 15°.
5. **Verdict: drifted.** Both the distance (3.9 Å vs ≤ 3.0 Å) and the angle (138° vs ≥ 150°) are out of tolerance. Although the identity is correct, the side chain adopts an unproductive rotamer — geometric drift, almost certainly because a second-shell residue that should orient Glu102 was redesigned away.
6. **Action.** Flag #14 as drifted; re-design this backbone with the key second-shell orienting residue(s) added to `--fixed_residues`, and re-screen. Had we trusted the high pLDDT and skipped the drift check, this inactive design would have gone to gene synthesis.
:::

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 16 · Design sequences and flag catalytic drift"}
Design sequences for your triaged backbone library and flag any whose catalytic residues move. Run in Colab or locally with a GPU.

1. **Prepare inputs** (Method 16.1, step 1): each triaged backbone with the ligand/TS model in theozyme geometry and the catalytic side chains present as fixed rotamers.
2. **Set fixed residues** (§16.2): first-shell catalytic residues plus the geometrically critical second-shell residues.
3. **Run LigandMPNN** with the soluble checkpoint, temperature 0.1–0.3, designing **8–48 sequences per backbone** per your funnel strategy (§16.3).
4. **Score for expression** (§16.4, From the Bench 16.1): net charge/pI, GRAVY, hydrophobic patches, low-complexity content, CamSol.
5. **Predict holo structures** of each design (AlphaFold3/Boltz) and measure **catalytic-residue RMSD** and the theozyme distances/angles (§16.5).
6. **Build the table**: one row per sequence with its expression scores, structure-agreement metric, catalytic-residue RMSD, and a **drift flag** (pass/fail against tolerance). Designs that pass the drift gate advance; drifted designs are flagged for re-design.

**Platform:** Colab / local (LigandMPNN + a ligand-aware structure predictor) · **Deliverable:** a sequence table with per-design expression scores and catalytic-drift flags.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **16.1** LigandMPNN is the ligand-, metal-, and cofactor-aware inverse-folding model that designs sequences compatible with a specified active site; ProteinMPNN's ligand blindness makes it the wrong tool for enzymes.
- **16.2** Fix the first-shell catalytic residues (identity and geometry) and the geometrically critical second-shell residues; fix too little and geometry drifts, too much and the protein cannot be made foldable and soluble.
- **16.3** Set the sequences-per-backbone budget (≈ 8–48) by the funnel: breadth early across many backbones, depth later on the most foldable survivors.
- **16.4** Bias toward solubility with the LigandMPNN soluble checkpoint and rank designs by net charge/pI, GRAVY, hydrophobic patches, low-complexity content, and CamSol; anticipate codon optimization and solubility fusions.
- **16.5** Guard against catalytic drift by measuring catalytic-residue RMSD and theozyme distances/angles on the *predicted holo structure* of each design; this drift check is the sequence-stage go/no-go gate.
