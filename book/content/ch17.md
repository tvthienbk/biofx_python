## 17.1 Self-Consistency as the Primary Filter

A generative pipeline will hand you more sequences than you can ever test. A single backbone passed through a sequence-design model such as ProteinMPNN typically yields 8–48 candidate sequences, and a campaign may carry hundreds or thousands of backbones. The arithmetic is unforgiving: tens of thousands of designs, an experimental budget of perhaps one or two 96-well plates, and a raw success rate that — without filtering — sits well below one percent. Filtering is not a tidying-up step at the end of the pipeline; it is the lever that converts a hopeless numbers game into a tractable one.

The most important filter is **self-consistency**, and it is worth stating its logic precisely because it is easy to misapply. When you designed a sequence, you used a model (ProteinMPNN, LigandMPNN, or the inverse-folding head of a generative tool) that was *told* the backbone and asked which amino acids would stabilize it. That model has every incentive to find a sequence that rationalizes the coordinates you gave it. To test whether the sequence actually *encodes* that structure, you must ask an **independent** model — one that was not shown the target backbone — to predict the structure from sequence alone, and then check whether the prediction returns to the intended backbone. Concretely: design a sequence for a backbone, predict its structure with a folding model such as AlphaFold3, Boltz-2, or ESMFold, and measure how far the prediction has moved.

This forward-and-back loop is the **self-consistency cycle**, and it is the closest thing the field has to a free in-silico assay. Its power comes from the independence of the two models. ProteinMPNN reasons from structure to sequence; the structure predictor reasons from sequence to structure. When their answers agree — when the predicted structure lands on the designed backbone — the sequence has cleared a genuine consistency check rather than a circular one. When they disagree, the sequence does not robustly specify the fold you wanted, and no amount of downstream analysis will rescue it. The doctrine in the epigraph is literal: refold what you designed, and if it does not come back, discard it.

It is essential to understand what self-consistency does and does not certify. A high-confidence refold says the sequence is *foldable* and that it folds to *the intended geometry*. It says nothing, by itself, about catalysis — a perfectly self-consistent backbone can have a dead active site. That is why self-consistency is the *first* filter, not the only one, and why Sections 17.4 and 17.5 layer additional, orthogonal checks on top. But it is first for a reason: a design that fails self-consistency has no path to activity, so spending docking or molecular-dynamics compute on it is wasted. Filter cheap and decisive before filtering expensive and subtle.

## 17.2 scRMSD, pLDDT, and Catalytic-Geometry RMSD

Three metrics carry most of the weight in self-consistency filtering, and a graduate practitioner should be able to compute, interpret, and defend each.

**Self-consistency RMSD (scRMSD)** is the root-mean-square deviation between the designed backbone and the structure predicted from the designed sequence, after optimal superposition. It is computed over backbone atoms — most commonly Cα only, sometimes the full N, Cα, C, O backbone — and reported in ångströms. The standard pass threshold for a well-behaved monomeric domain is **scRMSD < 2 Å**; the best designs come back at well under 1 Å. A value above ~2 Å means the predictor placed the chain somewhere other than where you designed it, and the sequence does not reliably encode your fold. Because ProteinMPNN is stochastic, the usual practice is to design several sequences per backbone and keep the *minimum* scRMSD over that set — the backbone "passes" if at least one of its sequences refolds cleanly.

**pLDDT** (predicted Local Distance Difference Test) is the per-residue confidence score emitted by AlphaFold-family and ESMFold predictors, on a 0–100 scale. It is a self-assessment: how sure the model is about each residue's local environment. Globally, pLDDT > 90 indicates a confidently predicted region; below ~70 the model is essentially guessing. For enzyme design the global average is less informative than the **local confidence at the catalytic residues**. A design can have an excellent average pLDDT while the very residues that must hold the transition state are floppy and uncertain. The operational threshold this book uses is **catalytic-residue pLDDT > 90** — every residue in the catalytic set must be confidently placed, because a residue the model cannot localize is a residue you cannot trust to do chemistry.

**Catalytic-geometry RMSD** is the most function-specific of the three and the one most often omitted by beginners. It measures whether the *relative arrangement* of the catalytic residues in the refolded structure matches the geometry you designed — the theozyme. You compute it by superposing only the catalytic-set atoms (for example, the imidazole nitrogens of a His, the carboxylate oxygens of an Asp/Glu, and any metal-coordinating atoms) between the design and the refold, and taking the RMSD over those atoms. The threshold is tight: **geometry RMSD < 0.5 Å**. The logic is biophysical. Catalysis depends on sub-ångström positioning of functional groups relative to the transition state; a geometry that drifts by even half an ångström can abolish a hydrogen bond or misalign a nucleophile's lone pair. A design can pass scRMSD and pLDDT yet fail geometry RMSD because the overall fold is fine while the active site has relaxed — and the active site is the entire point.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 17.1 · Computing scRMSD and catalytic-geometry RMSD"}
**Problem.** You designed a backbone with an Asp–His–Ser catalytic triad and a sequence that ProteinMPNN proposed. AlphaFold3 returned a model. Compute (a) the Cα scRMSD over the full chain and (b) the catalytic-geometry RMSD over the six functional atoms (Ser Oγ; His Nδ1, Nε2; Asp Oδ1, Oδ2; plus the substrate carbonyl carbon as a pseudo-atom). The catalytic atoms, after superposing the *full backbone*, deviate by the following per-atom distances (Å): Ser Oγ 0.31, His Nδ1 0.44, His Nε2 0.52, Asp Oδ1 0.28, Asp Oδ2 0.39, carbonyl C 0.61. The full-chain Cα superposition gave a backbone RMSD of 1.34 Å.

**Solution.** The Cα scRMSD is reported directly from the global superposition: **1.34 Å**, which passes the < 2 Å threshold. For the catalytic-geometry RMSD, take the root mean square of the six per-atom deviations:

<p class="eqn">RMSD = √[(0.31² + 0.44² + 0.52² + 0.28² + 0.39² + 0.61²) / 6]</p>
<p class="eqn">= √[(0.096 + 0.194 + 0.270 + 0.078 + 0.152 + 0.372) / 6] = √(1.162 / 6) = √0.194 = 0.44 Å</p>

The geometry RMSD is **0.44 Å**, which passes the < 0.5 Å threshold — but only just, and the substrate-carbonyl pseudo-atom (0.61 Å) is the worst offender. The correct report is both numbers *and* the per-atom breakdown, flagging that the substrate-proximal atom drifted most. A subtle but critical point: the geometry RMSD here was computed *after a full-backbone superposition*, which mixes global fold error into the active-site number. For a pure active-site comparison, superpose on the catalytic atoms themselves; the difference between the two conventions is exactly the question of whether the site is misplaced *within* a correct fold or the fold itself is off. Always state which superposition you used.
:::

## 17.3 Holo Refolding with Ligand and Metal

Apo refolding — predicting the structure of the protein alone — is necessary but not sufficient for an enzyme. An enzyme is defined by its relationship to a ligand, and many active sites are organized only in the presence of the substrate, a metal, or a cofactor. A backbone that refolds beautifully in the apo state can collapse its pocket, swing a catalytic loop, or fail to coordinate a metal once you account for the partner that is supposed to be there. **Holo refolding** asks the harder, more relevant question: when the predictor is given the sequence *and* the ligand or metal, does it keep them correctly placed in a competent active site?

The tooling for this matured rapidly in 2024–2025. **AlphaFold3** and **Boltz-2** predict protein–ligand and protein–metal complexes directly from sequence plus a ligand specification (a SMILES string or CCD code) and ion identity. This lets you run the self-consistency cycle in the holo state: design with a ligand-aware model such as **LigandMPNN** (which conditions the sequence on the ligand and any metal), then refold the complex and ask whether the predicted ligand pose still sits in the designed pocket with the catalytic residues arranged around it.

Holo refolding adds two metrics to the three from Section 17.2. **Ligand-pocket RMSD** measures how far the predicted ligand has moved from its designed pose after superposing on the protein; a competent design keeps the heavy-atom ligand RMSD below ~2 Å, and the catalytically relevant moiety (the scissile bond, the reacting carbon) much tighter. **Metal-coordination geometry** checks that the predicted metal retains its intended coordination number and donor set — for example, that a designed Zn²⁺ site keeps its three His / one water tetrahedral arrangement with metal–ligand distances near 2.0–2.2 Å rather than expelling the ion or recruiting the wrong residues. A design that places the right residues in the apo state but cannot hold the metal in the holo state is not an enzyme; it is a sequence that happens to fold.

The practical lesson is that the more of the *real* catalytic context you put into the refold, the more predictive the filter becomes — and the more compute it costs. Holo prediction with AlphaFold3 or Boltz-2 is markedly more expensive than ESMFold apo prediction, which is why the four-layer funnel of Section 17.4 runs cheap apo self-consistency first and reserves holo refolding for the survivors.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 17.1 · Refolding and analysis (version-stamped)"}
**AlphaFold3** (2024; server + open weights for non-commercial use, 2024) — protein–ligand–ion complex prediction; emits pLDDT, PAE, and ligand poses.  
**Boltz-2** (2025) — open-source diffusion predictor of biomolecular complexes with an affinity head; a practical, license-friendly AF3 alternative for holo refolding.  
**ESMFold** (v1, 2022) — single-sequence apo predictor; fast enough for first-pass scRMSD on thousands of designs.  
**ProteinMPNN** (2022) / **LigandMPNN** (2023) — inverse-folding sequence design; LigandMPNN conditions on ligand and metal context.  
**PyMOL** (open-source 2.5+) — `align`/`super` for superposition; `rms_cur` for RMSD over selected atoms.  
**Biopython** (v1.83+) — `Bio.PDB.Superimposer` for scripted Cα and catalytic-atom RMSD in a pipeline.  
**US-align / TM-align** — robust superposition when sequence registration is ambiguous.
:::

## 17.4 The Four-Layer Filter

No single metric is sufficient, and stacking *correlated* metrics buys little. The art is to layer filters that fail for *different reasons*, ordered cheapest-and-most-decisive first. The funnel this book recommends has four layers.

![**Figure 17.1** The four-layer filter: each layer removes designs for an orthogonal reason — foldability, active-site geometry, physical plausibility, and diversity — and the surviving set is far smaller and far richer in real candidates than the input.](assets/figures/four_layer_filter.png)

**Layer 1 — Self-consistency (apo).** The cheap, decisive gate. Refold every sequence with a fast predictor (ESMFold) and keep those with scRMSD < 2 Å and catalytic-residue pLDDT > 90. This typically removes the majority of designs and costs the least per design.

**Layer 2 — Catalytic geometry (holo).** For the survivors, run holo refolding (AlphaFold3 / Boltz-2) and apply catalytic-geometry RMSD < 0.5 Å plus ligand-pocket and metal-coordination checks. This layer is orthogonal to Layer 1: a design can fold correctly yet position its catalytic set wrongly, and only this layer catches it.

**Layer 3 — Physics-based and biophysical sanity.** Score the survivors with checks that the deep-learning metrics do not capture: a Rosetta or molecular-mechanics relaxation energy (no severe clashes or strain), buried-surface and packing quality, the absence of buried unsatisfied polar groups, predicted solubility/aggregation propensity, and pocket openness (is the substrate channel actually accessible?). These are orthogonal because a self-consistent, well-positioned design can still be physically pathological.

**Layer 4 — Diversity and selection.** Among survivors, cluster by sequence and backbone similarity and select a *diverse* set rather than 96 near-identical variants of one lucky backbone. Diversity hedges against systematic model error and is the bridge to Chapter 19's selection logic.

Each layer should be both orthogonal (failing for an independent reason) and ordered (cheap before expensive). Running holo refolding on designs that already failed apo self-consistency, or running MD on designs that failed geometry, simply burns compute on doomed candidates.

::: {.method data-title="Method 17.1 · The four-layer in-silico filter"}
1. **Assemble inputs.** Collect all designed sequences with their parent backbone IDs and the catalytic-set residue numbers. Record the ligand SMILES/CCD and any metal.
2. **Layer 1 — apo self-consistency.** For each sequence, predict the apo structure (ESMFold). Superpose on the design backbone; compute Cα scRMSD and per-residue pLDDT. **Keep** scRMSD < 2 Å AND min catalytic-residue pLDDT > 90.
3. **Layer 2 — holo geometry.** For survivors, predict the holo complex (AlphaFold3 or Boltz-2) with ligand and metal. Superpose on the catalytic atoms; compute catalytic-geometry RMSD, ligand-pocket RMSD, and metal-coordination distances. **Keep** geometry RMSD < 0.5 Å AND ligand heavy-atom RMSD < 2 Å AND intact metal coordination.
4. **Layer 3 — physics.** Relax survivors (Rosetta FastRelax or an MM minimization); compute interface/total energy, packing, buried-unsatisfied-polar count, and pocket accessibility. **Keep** designs with no severe clashes, acceptable energy z-score, and an open substrate channel.
5. **Layer 4 — diversity.** Cluster survivors by sequence identity (e.g., MMseqs2 at 70%) and backbone TM-score; select representatives spanning clusters to fill the experimental budget.
6. **Record provenance.** Log every threshold, tool version, and random seed for each surviving design (Chapter 19).
:::

```python
# Four-layer filter pipeline (pseudocode)
shortlist = []
for seq in designs:
    refold_apo = esmfold(seq.sequence)                      # Layer 1
    sc_rmsd   = ca_rmsd(seq.backbone, refold_apo)
    cat_plddt = min(refold_apo.plddt[r] for r in seq.catalytic_residues)
    if sc_rmsd >= 2.0 or cat_plddt <= 90:
        continue

    holo = af3_or_boltz(seq.sequence, seq.ligand, seq.metal) # Layer 2
    geom = catalytic_rmsd(seq.theozyme, holo, atoms=seq.catalytic_atoms)
    lig  = ligand_rmsd(seq.ligand_pose, holo)
    if geom >= 0.5 or lig >= 2.0 or not metal_intact(holo, seq.metal):
        continue

    relaxed = rosetta_relax(holo)                           # Layer 3
    if relaxed.clashes or relaxed.energy_z > 1.0 or not pocket_open(relaxed):
        continue

    seq.scores = dict(sc_rmsd=sc_rmsd, cat_plddt=cat_plddt,
                      geom=geom, lig=lig, energy=relaxed.energy)
    shortlist.append(seq)

selection = diversity_select(shortlist, n=96)               # Layer 4
```

## 17.5 Which Metrics Predict Experimental Success

Not every number a tool prints is informative, and a recurring failure mode is to rank designs by a metric that correlates with nothing measurable. The graduate skill here is to distinguish **predictive** metrics from **decorative** ones.

The metrics with the strongest retrospective association with experimental success are, unsurprisingly, the ones tied to the active site: **catalytic-geometry RMSD** in the holo state and **local pLDDT at the catalytic residues**. These directly probe whether the chemistry-bearing residues are confidently and correctly placed. Apo **scRMSD** is a strong *negative* predictor — a high scRMSD reliably indicates failure — but a low scRMSD is only weakly *positive*, because plenty of well-folded backbones have dead sites. This asymmetry matters: scRMSD is an excellent gate (it removes the hopeless) but a poor ranker (it does not order the survivors well).

Several widely reported numbers are largely **decorative** for ranking purposes. Global average pLDDT saturates near 95 for almost every design that passes Layer 1, so it has little discriminating power among survivors. PAE (predicted aligned error) is informative for multi-domain or complex geometry but adds little for a single compact domain. And any metric computed only in the *apo* state tells you nothing about ligand engagement. The deepest trap is the **circular metric**: scoring a design with the same model family that generated it, or evaluating the refold using the backbone as a template. These produce reassuring numbers that predict nothing because the test was not independent.

The honest position, reinforced by every campaign in the literature, is that in-silico filtering enriches but does not guarantee. The metrics shift the odds — a stack of orthogonal, predictive filters can turn a sub-percent raw hit rate into a few-percent tested hit rate — but the only metric that settles the question of catalysis is a kinetics assay. Filter to improve your odds, and stay humble about what a green checkmark means.

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 17.1 · Low scRMSD ≠ activity"}
A design with scRMSD 0.7 Å, average pLDDT 96, and a textbook refold image is *foldable*, not *active*. In real campaigns, the large majority of designs that clear self-consistency are still catalytically inactive — the site may be subtly mispositioned, the substrate may not bind, the transition state may not be stabilized, or the protein may not express. Self-consistency removes the hopeless; it does not promote the survivors to enzymes. Treat a clean refold as a license to spend more compute (holo, geometry, MD), not as a prediction of turnover.
:::

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 17.1"}
- **Single-layer filtering** — ranking by scRMSD alone and ignoring catalytic geometry; this passes well-folded dead sites.
- **Circular self-consistency** — refolding with the design backbone supplied as a template, so the test is not independent.
- **Apo-only validation** — never checking whether the ligand or metal stays placed in the holo state.
- **Global pLDDT as a ranker** — it saturates and does not discriminate among survivors; use *local* catalytic pLDDT.
- **No diversity step** — shipping 96 near-clones of one backbone, so a single model error sinks the whole plate.
- **Unstated superposition convention** for geometry RMSD, making the number uninterpretable.
:::

## 17.6 Building the Pipeline

A filtering pipeline is software, and it should be written like software: modular, logged, and reproducible. The architecture that scales is a sequence of independent stages, each reading a table of surviving designs and writing a smaller, annotated table. Stage boundaries are where you checkpoint, so a crashed holo-prediction job does not force you to recompute Layer 1.

Three engineering principles keep a pipeline honest. First, **never throw away a rejected design silently** — record *why* it failed (which threshold, what value) so that a too-aggressive threshold becomes visible when survivors are scarce. Second, **make every threshold a named parameter in a config file**, not a magic number buried in code; you will tune them, and a reviewer will ask what they were. Third, **carry provenance with the data**: each surviving row should accumulate its scores, the tool versions that produced them, and the seeds used, so that the final shortlist is fully traceable (the subject of Chapter 19). A pipeline that produces a ranked list but cannot explain how is not defensible.

The output of this chapter is a single, ranked table: every surviving design with its scRMSD, catalytic pLDDT, geometry RMSD, ligand RMSD, and physics scores, ordered by a provisional composite (we formalize composite scoring in Chapter 19). That table feeds the rest of Part IV — the survivors go to molecular dynamics and docking (Chapter 18), and the final ranking and plate selection (Chapter 19). The lab below builds exactly this pipeline.

::: {.lab data-title="Hands-On Lab 17 · From 500 designs to a ranked top 96"}
You are given a set of **500 designs** (sequences with parent backbones, catalytic-set annotations, and a ligand SMILES) for a hydrolase active site.

**Tasks.**
1. In a Jupyter notebook, load the 500 designs into a table with columns for sequence, backbone path, and catalytic residues.
2. **Layer 1:** run ESMFold on each sequence (batch on GPU), superpose with `Bio.PDB.Superimposer`, and compute Cα scRMSD and minimum catalytic-residue pLDDT. Filter at scRMSD < 2 Å and catalytic pLDDT > 90. Log every rejection with its failing value.
3. **Layer 2:** run holo refolding (Boltz-2 or AlphaFold3) on Layer-1 survivors; compute catalytic-geometry RMSD (catalytic-atom superposition) and ligand-pocket RMSD. Filter at < 0.5 Å and < 2 Å.
4. **Layer 3:** run a quick MM minimization; flag clashes and compute a packing/energy score.
5. **Layer 4:** cluster survivors (MMseqs2 at 70% identity) and select a diverse **top 96**.
6. Plot the funnel (counts surviving each layer) and a scatter of geometry RMSD vs. catalytic pLDDT colored by survival.

Make every threshold a config parameter; record tool versions and seeds in the notebook header.

**Platform:** Jupyter (GPU for ESMFold/Boltz-2) · **Deliverable:** a filtering-pipeline script plus a ranked shortlist of 96 designs with all scores and a funnel plot.

## Chapter Summary

- **17.1** Self-consistency — designing a sequence, refolding it with an *independent* predictor, and checking it returns to the intended backbone — is the primary in-silico filter; its power comes from model independence.
- **17.2** scRMSD (< 2 Å), catalytic-residue pLDDT (> 90), and catalytic-geometry RMSD (< 0.5 Å) are the three core self-consistency metrics; geometry RMSD is the most function-specific.
- **17.3** Holo refolding with ligand and metal tests whether the active site stays organized in its real catalytic context, adding ligand-pocket RMSD and metal-coordination checks.

- **17.5** Active-site metrics (geometry RMSD, local catalytic pLDDT) are most predictive; scRMSD is a strong gate but weak ranker; global pLDDT and circular metrics are decorative.
- **17.6** Build the pipeline as modular, logged, config-driven software that carries provenance and outputs one ranked, traceable table.
