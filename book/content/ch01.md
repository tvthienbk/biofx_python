## 1.1 The Spectrum: Evolution, Engineering, De Novo

Three and a half billion years of evolution have produced an extraordinary catalogue of catalysts, yet that catalogue is a record of accidents that happened to be useful — not a systematic survey of what protein chemistry *can* do. Natural enzymes are local optima reached by a search that could only ever move one viable mutation at a time. The premise of this book is that we are no longer restricted to that search. We can specify the chemistry we want and ask a computer to build a protein around it.

It helps to place the available approaches on a single axis defined by **how much natural sequence information the method reuses**. At one end sits **directed evolution**: begin with a natural enzyme that already does something close to the target reaction, introduce random mutations, and screen or select for improvement. Directed evolution is powerful and reliably productive — it earned Frances Arnold a share of the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry — but it is fundamentally a *local* optimizer. It cannot conjure an activity that has no evolutionary foothold in the starting scaffold.

Moving along the axis, **rational engineering** introduces targeted mutations guided by structural and mechanistic insight: widen a pocket, install a catalytic histidine, stabilize an oxyanion. This requires a structure and a hypothesis, and it too begins from a natural protein. Further still, **de novo design from a template** grafts a catalytic motif onto an existing fold. Only at the far end do we reach **de novo design from scratch**, in which the backbone itself is generated — a protein with no natural homolog, built to host a chemistry of our choosing.

![**Figure 1.1** The protein-engineering spectrum, ordered by how much natural sequence information each method reuses. De novo design from scratch is the only approach that is not anchored to a pre-existing natural protein.](assets/figures/engineering_spectrum.png)

These categories are not rivals so much as a toolkit. Many of the field's landmark "designed" enzymes were *finished* by directed evolution, and the most pragmatic modern campaigns deliberately combine generation with optimization. The distinction that matters for this book is the starting point: a de novo design begins from a specification of catalytic chemistry rather than from an existing protein.

::: {.conceptcheck data-title="Concept Check 1.1"}
A lab takes a natural esterase and, over six rounds of mutagenesis and screening, improves its activity on a non-natural substrate by 400-fold. Where on the spectrum does this work sit, and why is it *not* de novo design?
:::

## 1.2 What "De Novo" Means Operationally

"De novo" is easy to say and easy to overclaim, so the field has converged on **operational** definitions that a reviewer can check. The most common is structural novelty measured by **TM-score**, a length-normalized measure of fold similarity that runs from 0 (unrelated) to 1 (identical). A widely used threshold is that a backbone is *novel* if its TM-score to every entry in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) is **below 0.5** — the value below which two structures are generally agreed not to share a fold. Tools such as **Foldseek** make this search fast enough to run on thousands of candidate backbones.

Sequence novelty is the companion criterion: a designed sequence should not simply recapitulate a natural protein. A design that scores a confident structure but turns out to be 70% identical to a known enzyme has not demonstrated de novo capability; it has rediscovered evolution's answer.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 1.1 · Computing a TM-score novelty threshold"}
**Problem.** You have generated 1,000 backbones and run each through Foldseek against the PDB. For a candidate, the single best structural hit has a TM-score of 0.42 (normalized by the *shorter* chain) and 0.39 (normalized by the *query*). Is this backbone "novel" by the standard threshold? What subtlety should you report?

**Solution.** TM-score is asymmetric: it depends on which chain length is used for normalization, so a single number is ambiguous. Report both. Here both values (0.42 and 0.39) fall below the 0.5 fold-similarity threshold, so by the standard operational criterion the backbone is structurally novel. The subtlety: always state the normalization convention, and be wary of partial matches where a sub-domain is highly similar even though the global TM-score is low. A reviewer will ask for the per-residue alignment, not just the scalar.
:::

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 1.1 · Novelty checks (version-stamped)"}
**Foldseek** (v9, 2024) — ultrafast structure search against the PDB/AFDB; use `easy-search` with TM-score output.  
**TM-align** (2005, still standard) — pairwise structural alignment and TM-score.  
**US-align** (2022) — generalizes TM-align to complexes and nucleic acids.  
Report TM-score with its normalization, and sequence identity from a BLAST/MMseqs2 search in parallel.
:::

## 1.3 Why Design Enzymes

There are three durable motivations, and it is worth being explicit about which one drives a given project, because they imply different success criteria.

**New chemistry.** Some reactions have no good natural catalyst — non-natural bond formations, abiological substrates, reactions that evolution never had a reason to optimize. De novo design is the only route that does not depend on borrowing an evolutionary precedent. This is the most scientifically ambitious motivation and the hardest.

**Designer biocatalysts.** Industry wants enzymes that work under *its* conditions: a particular solvent, temperature, pH, or substrate that matters commercially. Here the goal is often a robust, manufacturable catalyst rather than a record-breaking one, and a modest-but-real de novo activity can be a superb starting point for optimization.

**Understanding catalysis.** A design is a falsifiable hypothesis about how catalysis works. If a theozyme that places three functional groups in a particular geometry produces measurable rate enhancement, that is positive evidence that the geometry matters. Designs that fail are equally informative — which is why this book treats *failure as curriculum*.

::: {.reality data-title="Reality Check 1.1 · The hit-rate landscape"}
A de novo enzyme campaign typically tests on the order of 10–100 designs experimentally and finds that **1–5%** show the intended activity above background. Early Rosetta-era campaigns sometimes screened thousands of designs for a handful of weak hits; the best 2025–2026 atom-level pipelines have reached active catalysts within fewer than ~96 tested sequences for favorable chemistries. Either way, expect most designs to be inactive or unexpressed. The transformative fact is not the hit rate — it is that the hits exist at all, for chemistries with no natural template.
:::

The honest framing is that de novo enzyme design is a numbers game played against steep odds, and the entire computational pipeline exists to improve those odds before a single gene is ordered. A campaign that generates widely and filters ruthlessly converts a 0.1% raw success rate into a few-percent tested success rate. That leverage is the subject of Parts II–IV.

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 1.1"}
- Claiming "de novo" while starting from a close natural homolog (check TM-score *and* sequence identity).
- Reporting a hit rate without stating how many designs were tested.
- Presenting one active design out of thousands as if the success rate were 100%.
- Calling a tightly binding protein an "enzyme" without turnover data (see Chapter 22).
:::

## 1.4 The Design–Build–Test–Learn Cycle

Every campaign in this book is an instance of one loop, borrowed from synthetic biology and engineering: **Design → Build → Test → Learn → (redesign)**. We adopt it as the organizing diagram for the whole text.

![**Figure 1.2** The Design–Build–Test–Learn (DBTL) cycle. Each quadrant maps to a Part of this book: Design to Parts II–IV, Build and Test to Part V, and Learn to Part VI's optimization chapter.](assets/figures/dbtl_cycle.png)

- **Design** (Parts II–IV): define the reaction and transition state, build a theozyme, generate backbones, design sequences, and filter *in silico* to a shortlist.
- **Build** (Part V): synthesize genes, express in a host, and purify protein.
- **Test** (Part V): confirm folding and stability, measure catalysis with the full control suite, and — for the best hits — determine a structure.
- **Learn** (Part VI): diagnose why designs failed and feed that knowledge into a redesign or a directed-evolution loop.

The cycle is also a discipline. It insists that success be *defined before* it is pursued (Chapter 13), that controls be designed in from the start (Chapter 19), and that every result be recorded so the loop can actually close (the design notebook). A design is a hypothesis until the assay says otherwise.

::: {.studio data-title="Studio Exercise 1 · Classifying real designs"}
Select two *designed* and two *natural* enzymes from the recent literature. For each, locate the reported structure, run a quick Foldseek search if a model is available, and classify the work on the engineering spectrum (directed evolution / rational / template-based / de novo-from-scratch). Note what evidence the authors give for novelty.

**Platform:** literature + Foldseek web server · **Deliverable:** a one-page classification memo with a short justification per entry.
:::

## 1.5 How This Book Is Organized

The book follows the DBTL loop. **Part I** builds the chemistry and biophysics you must own before generating anything. **Part II** covers how models *see* proteins, ligands, and active sites, and how prediction becomes a filter. **Part III** teaches the generative models, culminating in the atom-level tools built for catalysis. **Part IV** is the hands-on core: one complete design campaign, end to end. **Part V** crosses from silicon to cell — expression, characterization, kinetics, structure — the half of the field a purely computational text ignores. **Part VI** covers frontiers, optimization, and responsible practice.

Closing Part I, we will commit to the **four questions** that structure every campaign and that recur throughout Part IV: *What reaction? What transition state? What catalytic set? What pocket?* Everything downstream is, in a sense, carpentry built on the answers.
