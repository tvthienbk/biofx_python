## 11.1 Sequence–Structure Co-Design

The pipelines of Chapters 9 and 10 are **sequential**: a generator produces a backbone, then an inverse-folding network (ProteinMPNN/LigandMPNN) produces a sequence for it. This separation is clean and has worked extraordinarily well, but it has a conceptual seam. The backbone is committed before the sequence is known, so the generator must produce a backbone that *some* sequence can encode — and the sequence stage cannot revisit a backbone choice that turns out to be hard to realize. **Sequence–structure co-design** removes the seam by generating sequence and structure *jointly*, so that the two are mutually consistent by construction rather than reconciled in two passes.

For enzymes the appeal is specific. Catalysis couples sequence and structure tightly: the identity of a catalytic residue, its rotameric preference, and the backbone geometry that supports it are not independent choices. A co-design model can, in principle, reason about all three at once — choosing a residue *because* the local backbone favors the rotamer that reaches the substrate, rather than placing a backbone and hoping a sequence cooperates. **GENzyme** and **EnzyGen2** are representative enzyme-oriented co-design efforts: they aim to generate the catalytic pocket, the surrounding scaffold, and a compatible sequence together, often conditioned on a reaction or a substrate specification.

The trade-offs are real and worth stating plainly. Joint generation is a harder learning problem than either piece alone, and the training data for *functional* enzymes is far scarcer than for static structures, so co-design models can be less mature and less thoroughly validated than the well-benchmarked RFD family. They can also be harder to control: a sequential pipeline lets you intervene between stages (filter backbones, re-run MPNN at a different temperature), whereas a monolithic co-design step offers fewer hand-holds. The honest current status is that co-design is conceptually attractive and improving quickly, but for most enzyme campaigns in 2025–2026 the validated workhorse remains a generator-then-inverse-folding pipeline, with co-design models worth trialing in parallel rather than relied upon as the sole route.

## 11.2 Other Backbone Generators

RFdiffusion is not the only backbone generator, and several alternatives carry distinct strengths a designer should recognize when reading a methods section.

**Chroma** (Ingraham et al., *Nature* 2023) is a programmable generative model for proteins built around a diffusion process with a correlated-noise prior that scales efficiently to large complexes. Its distinctive feature is **conditioning by arbitrary "programs"** — user-supplied constraints on symmetry, shape, substructure, semantics, and even natural-language-derived properties — composed at sampling time. Chroma is strong for large or shape-constrained assemblies and for exploratory generation where you want to steer global properties.

**FrameFlow** applies **flow matching** rather than denoising diffusion to the SE(3) frames that represent residues. Flow matching learns a continuous transport from noise to data and can generate competitive backbones in *far fewer* integration steps than diffusion, making it attractive when sampling throughput matters. It exemplifies a broader shift in the field from diffusion toward flow-matching formulations.

**Genie2** is a diffusion model notable for strong **unconditional designability** and for supporting **multi-motif scaffolding** — placing several functional motifs in one scaffold — which is relevant when an active site is specified as more than one separated motif.

**Protpardelle** is an **all-atom** generative model (predating the RFD3 line) that co-generates backbone and side-chain atoms, an early demonstration that all-atom generation is feasible and useful for sites where side-chain placement matters.

The point of cataloguing these is not to crown a winner but to build the reflex of asking, for any new model, *what representation does it use* (frames? all-atom?), *what generative process* (diffusion? flow matching?), *what can it condition on*, and *what has it actually been validated to do*. Those four questions place almost any backbone generator on the map relative to the RFD family.

::: {.worked data-title="Worked Example 11.1 · Benchmarking a co-design model's in-silico claims"}
**Problem.** A new co-design preprint reports that 78% of its generated enzymes are "designable" and claims this beats RFdiffusion. You must decide whether the comparison is meaningful before recommending the tool. What do you check?

**Solution.** Interrogate the *definition* and the *protocol*, not the headline. (1) **What is "designable" here?** Self-consistency by refolding the co-designed sequence and measuring Cα-RMSD and pLDDT — with what thresholds? If they used a looser RMSD cut (e.g., < 3 Å) than the 2 Å used in the RFD comparison, the numbers are not comparable. (2) **Which predictor refolded the sequences?** A model that co-designs sequence and structure may be implicitly optimized against the same predictor used to score it, inflating self-consistency through circularity. (3) **Same test set and lengths?** Designability varies by length and fold (Chapter 9); a barrel-heavy benchmark is harder than short helical bundles. (4) **Is the claim about *enzymes* backed by *catalytic-geometry* metrics**, or only global designability? Global designability says nothing about active-site preservation. (5) **Any wet-lab data?** Absent expression or activity, "78%" is an in-silico statement only. The defensible conclusion: re-run both models on a *common* benchmark with *identical* thresholds and an *independent* predictor before believing any ranking. A headline percentage with mismatched protocols is not evidence of superiority.
:::

## 11.3 Protein Language Models in the Loop

A parallel lineage learns from sequence rather than coordinates. **Protein language models (PLMs)**, trained on hundreds of millions of natural sequences, learn statistical regularities that encode structure, stability, and function implicitly. The ESM series is the landmark; **ESM3** (Hayes et al., 2024/2025) is a *multimodal generative* model that reasons jointly over sequence, structure, and function tracks, and can be prompted to generate or complete a protein given partial information in any of those modalities — for example, "generate a sequence consistent with this structure and this functional annotation."

In a design loop, PLMs play several roles. They serve as **priors and filters**: scoring designed sequences for naturalness/plausibility, flagging sequences a generator produced that no natural protein resembles in a way that predicts poor expression. They serve as **generators or in-painters** of sequence, sometimes replacing or complementing inverse folding. And they encode **functional** signal that pure structure models lack — useful when the specification includes a functional annotation rather than only a geometry. The caution mirrors co-design: PLMs are trained on natural proteins, so they can be biased *against* genuinely novel de novo sequences, scoring a perfectly good designed enzyme as "unnatural." Use a PLM as one signal among several, not as a veto on novelty — the whole point of de novo design is to leave the natural distribution.

:::

## 11.4 Closed vs. Open Tools

The model zoo divides along a practical axis that shapes what you can actually do: **open** versus **closed**. RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN/LigandMPNN, Chroma (weights released), ESM2 and much of the ESM line, Genie2, FrameFlow, and Protpardelle are **open** — code and/or weights available, runnable on your own hardware, inspectable, modifiable, and citable with a pinned version. Open tools are the backbone of reproducible academic work: you control the seed, the version, and the provenance, and a reviewer can rerun you.

Other capabilities are **closed or partially gated** — available only through a web interface or API, with weights withheld, terms of use restricting commercial application, or rate limits. Closed tools can be excellent and sometimes lead on a specific capability, but they impose costs a campaign must weigh: you cannot pin a version that the provider may silently change, you cannot inspect failure modes, your data may transit a third party, and reproducibility depends on a service that may not persist. For an enzyme design textbook the guidance is to *prefer open, version-pinned tools for the load-bearing steps* and to treat closed tools as conveniences or specialized accelerators — never as the irreproducible heart of a published result. The licensing terms matter too: some open weights carry non-commercial or attribution clauses that a startup must read before building on them.

::: {.toolbox data-title="Tool Box 11.1 · Emerging models (version-stamped)"}
**EnzyGen2** (2026) — enzyme-oriented sequence–structure co-design conditioned on reaction/substrate; evaluate against an RFD pipeline on a common benchmark before relying on it.  
**Chroma** (Ingraham et al., *Nature* 2023; weights released) — programmable diffusion with composable constraints (symmetry, shape, substructure, semantics); strong for large/shape-constrained complexes.  
**FrameFlow** (2023–2024) — SE(3) flow-matching backbone generator; fewer sampling steps than diffusion.  
**Genie2** — diffusion backbone generator with strong unconditional designability and multi-motif scaffolding.  
**Protpardelle** — early all-atom co-generation of backbone and side chains.  
**ESM3** (Hayes et al., 2024/2025) — multimodal generative PLM over sequence/structure/function; open small variants, larger access via API/terms.  
Record version, weights hash or commit, and license for every model used in a campaign.
:::

## 11.5 Reading the Frontier Critically

Because the map is redrawn every year, the durable skill is not memorizing today's leaderboard but reading a new method honestly. A few habits separate signal from hype.

**Demand a common benchmark.** A claim of superiority is meaningful only against a shared test set with identical metrics, thresholds, and an *independent* predictor. Self-reported percentages on a custom benchmark are descriptive, not comparative.

**Watch for circular validation.** If a model is scored by the same predictor it was trained or tuned against, high self-consistency can be an artifact of the loop closing on itself rather than evidence of designability. Insist on an orthogonal evaluator.

**Separate designability from function.** Global Cα-RMSD and pLDDT measure whether a fold is encodable; they say nothing about catalytic-geometry preservation, expression, solubility, or turnover. For enzymes, only active-site metrics and, ultimately, wet-lab kinetics speak to function.

**Count the wet-lab evidence.** How many designs were tested, by what assay, with what controls (Chapters 19, 22)? One active design out of an unstated number is not a hit rate. A preprint with no experimental data is a hypothesis, however elegant.

**Mind the data.** Functional enzyme training data is scarce and biased toward natural proteins; ask what a model was trained on and whether that biases it against the novelty you actually want.

These are the same disciplines Chapter 1 set out, applied to a moving target. They let you adopt a genuinely better tool quickly and ignore a louder one safely — which is exactly what a field that reinvents itself annually demands.

::: {.redflags data-title="Red Flags 11.1 · Benchmark-only validation"}
- A superiority claim with **no shared benchmark** — different test set, lengths, or thresholds than the method it "beats."
- **Circular validation:** scored by the predictor it was optimized against, with no independent evaluator.
- Reporting **global designability for an enzyme** while omitting any catalytic-geometry or active-site metric.
- **No wet-lab data**, or one active design with the denominator unstated.
- A **closed tool** presented as the reproducible core of a result, with no version, weights, or rerun path.
- A **language-model "naturalness" score used as a veto** on de novo designs — penalizing exactly the novelty the project exists to produce.
:::

## Chapter Summary

- **11.1** Sequence–structure co-design (GENzyme, EnzyGen2) generates sequence and structure jointly, removing the sequential seam but on scarcer functional data and with fewer control hand-holds; the validated workhorse for now remains generator-then-inverse-folding.
- **11.2** Chroma (programmable constraints), FrameFlow (flow matching, fewer steps), Genie2 (multi-motif scaffolding), and Protpardelle (all-atom) each occupy a distinct point in representation × process × conditioning × validation.
- **11.3** Protein language models, ESM3 chief among them, contribute sequence and *function* priors and act as filters/generators — but are biased toward natural proteins and must not veto novelty.
- **11.4** Prefer open, version-pinned tools for load-bearing steps; treat closed tools as accelerators, mindful of reproducibility, data transit, and licensing.
- **11.5** Read the frontier critically: demand common benchmarks, watch for circular validation, separate designability from function, count the wet-lab evidence, and mind the training data.
