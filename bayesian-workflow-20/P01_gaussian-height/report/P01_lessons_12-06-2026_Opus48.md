# Lessons Report — Project 01: Mean Height (Gaussian)

**Author:** Truong Van Thien, PhD · **Date:** 12-06-2026 · **Model tag:** Opus48
*(Student-facing.)*

## 1. The concept taught
This project runs the **entire Bayesian workflow loop once** on the simplest non-trivial model:
adult heights as a single Gaussian, `height ~ Normal(μ, σ)`. Nothing about the science is hard —
the point is to internalise the *rhythm* (Stage 0 → Stage 12) and the discipline you will reuse
in all 19 later projects: recover known parameters first, check priors on the **outcome** scale,
read the four core diagnostics, and let a posterior predictive check tell you what to build next.

## 2. Workflow lessons from this run (with the actual numbers)
1. **Recover before you trust.** Fitting 200 simulated heights with known μ = 178, σ = 8 returned
   μ̂ = 177.4 (90% HDI 176.6–178.2) and σ̂ = 7.46 (6.84–8.08) — both truths inside the interval.
   Only *then* did we touch real data. A model that fails this step is broken regardless of what
   it does on real data.
2. **Priors live on the outcome scale.** The prior predictive put **0.0%** of mass on negative
   heights and centred on human values, even though `μ ~ Normal(178, 20)` looks abstract. Checking
   the *parameters* alone would not have told us the implied *heights* are sane.
3. **The diagnostics all agreed the sampler was healthy:** R̂ = 1.00, ESS-bulk ≈ 3200, ESS-tail
   ≈ 2300, **0 divergences**, BFMI ≈ 1.0. This is what "nothing to see here" looks like — learn it
   so the pathologies in P11 stand out.
4. **The posterior is sharp and honest:** μ = **154.6 cm** (94% HDI 153.8–155.4), σ = 7.8 cm.
   With n = 352 the mean is pinned to ±0.8 cm, but individual heights still vary by ~±16 cm.
5. **The PPC found the one real flaw.** Mean and sd matched (p ≈ 0.50) but the minimum had
   Bayesian p = 0.04 — real adults have a slightly heavier *short* tail than a symmetric Gaussian.
   That single number is the honest motivation for the next model (P03, height ~ weight), and
   power-scaling confirmed the headline μ is robust (moves < 0.01 cm as the prior is scaled 0.8–1.25×).

## 3. Common mistakes
- **Forgetting `sep=";"`.** The rethinking CSVs are semicolon-delimited; a default `read_csv`
  silently loads one mangled column.
- **Including children.** Without `age >= 18` the heights are bimodal and a single Gaussian is
  simply the wrong model — the mean lands between two clusters.
- **Judging priors on parameters, not outcomes.** A "reasonable-looking" `σ` prior can still imply
  absurd data once pushed through the likelihood. Always plot the prior predictive outcome.
- **Reading `elpd_diff` without its SE.** Normal beat Student-t by ≈1.5 elpd with SE ≈0.5 — small;
  never quote a difference without comparing it to `dse`.

## 4. Try it yourself
1. Re-fit with tighter priors (`μ ~ Normal(178, 10)`, `σ ~ HalfNormal(10)`); compare the prior
   predictive and confirm via power-scaling that the posterior barely moves.
2. Drop the `age >= 18` filter and use the PPC to *demonstrate* (not just assert) why one Gaussian
   fails on the full sample.
3. Build the 90% **posterior predictive** interval for one new adult and explain why it is far
   wider than the 90% interval for μ.

## 5. Self-check
1. Why do we simulate and recover known parameters (Stage 0) *before* loading real data?
2. What does "prior predictive on the outcome scale" mean, and why is it stronger than inspecting
   the priors on μ and σ directly?
3. The minimum-height Bayesian p-value was 0.04. What does that tell you, and what does it *not*
   tell you?
4. R̂ = 1.00 and ESS-tail ≈ 2300 — state in one sentence what each of these guarantees (and what
   neither guarantees).
5. Power-scaling moved E[μ] by < 0.01 cm. If instead it had moved by 3 cm, what would you conclude
   and what would you do next?
